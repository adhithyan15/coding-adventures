Lesson 1 of 6.
开花 (kāihuā) — to bloom; to flower.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Mandarin for a monkey, then the Mandarin for a rabbit.

You'll want to know: 开花.
开花 — kāihuā — "to bloom; to flower".
这些花三月开花 zhèxiē huā sānyuè kāihuā — These flowers come out in March.
The new character, in pieces: 花 (huā) — seven strokes: the grass piece of 菜 on top, then the person piece, then two strokes like the right half of 比. The next lesson puts it in your hand.

The word, taken apart.
开花 (kāihuā) is built from 开 'to open' and 花 (huā) 'flower', both inherited from Middle Chinese, khoj and xwae (Guangyun, in Baxter's transcription), which notes the second as the everyday form of the word for blossom.

Script — the grass piece over the person piece and two more strokes.
花 (huā).
Three pieces: one on top, two side by side under it. Count the pieces, not the strokes.
The grass piece, three strokes, as in 菜 and 茶: a bar across, then two short verticals through it.
The person piece, 亻, two strokes, as in 你: a stroke falling to the left, then a vertical.
The right piece, two strokes, like the right half of 比: a short stroke falling to the left, then a vertical that bends right along the foot and flicks up.
3 + 2 + 2 = seven.

[once you have stopped driving — this part needs your eyes or your hands; if you are driving, skip ahead to the next part]

[once you have stopped driving — cover: write it again from memory, and count the pieces, not the strokes]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
How many strokes in 花 (huā)? (Seven.) What is on top? (The grass piece of 菜.) Which piece comes next? (亻, the person piece.)

Its characters come later; for now it is a word for the ear.

The word, taken apart.
húdié is built from two characters, and the dictionary glosses the second, dep there (Guangyun, in Baxter's transcription), as a butterfly. No dictionary we use records an older form of the first, so its history is not settled.

Lesson 6 of 6.
mìfēng — bee; honeybee.

[pause 2 seconds]

You'll want to know: mìfēng.
mìfēng — "bee; honeybee".
huāyuán lǐ de huā kāi le mìfēng yě lái le — The flowers in the garden are out, and the bees have come too.
Its characters come later; for now it is a word for the ear.

The word, taken apart.
mìfēng is built from mì 'honey' and fēng 'bee', both inherited from Middle Chinese, mjit and phjowng (Guangyun, in Baxter's transcription), which glosses the first as the food bees make and explains the second, after an older book, as a flying insect that stings.
