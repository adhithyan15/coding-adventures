Hindi, chapter 94: The Pronouns, And The Article Hindi Does Not Have.
7 lessons. You can do the first 2 of them in the car; after that you will want to have stopped.


Lesson 1 of 7.
हम — the word that was already inside hamārā.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say hamārā. You learned it as "our" and were told what it was built on. Say that word on its own — you have been carrying it for a chapter without being given it.

You'll want to know: हम.
हम (ham) — "we."
The possessive chapter said hamārā was built on हम, and there the matter rested. Here is the word itself.

Grammar Lens: where Hindi says "we" and English says "I".
हम (ham) does a second job that has no English equivalent. In much ordinary speech it stands in for मैं, "I," when the speaker is being modest or speaking as part of a household:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
said: ham jāte haiṅ. usually means: we go — or, often, I go.
said: hamārā ghar. usually means: our house — or my house.
This is the same instinct that made hamārā parivār the natural way to say "my family." A Hindi speaker places themselves inside a group by default where an English speaker steps out of it.
A listening paper will play you ham where you expect maiṅ, and nothing marks the difference but context.

Lesson 2 of 7.
वे (ve) — they, and the respect that looks like a plural.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say वह and हम (ham). You can point at one person and you can include yourself in a group. Say "they," and notice the gap.

You'll want to know: वे.
वे (ve) — "they."
It is the plural of वह, and the relationship is visible: the same word with a different vowel.

Grammar Lens: the plural that is also a courtesy.
The same habit you met with आप appears here. Hindi shows respect by using a plural form for one person:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
form: वह. about: one person, familiar or neutral.
form: वे. about: several people — or one person spoken of with respect.
So ve ā rahe haiṅ can be "they are coming" or "he is coming," said of someone senior. This is the third time the pattern has appeared: āp for a respected listener, the -iye command for a respected listener, and now ve for a respected third person.
In everyday speech many speakers say वो for both vah and ve. Recognise it; write the forms above.

Lesson 3 of 7.
हमें / तुम्हें (hamẽ / tumhẽ) — what mujhe was all along.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, because the lesson points at something written down. You can listen to all of it now and come back to the parts that need looking at once you have stopped.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say mujhe … pasand hai. You learned it as a frame for saying what you like. Say the first word on its own and ask what it actually is.

You'll want to know: हमें and तुम्हें.
हमें (hamẽ) — "to us." तुम्हें (tumhẽ) — "to you," at the tum level.
Mujhe is the same kind of word: मैं in its oblique form, meaning to me. The liking frame was never a lump — it was a pronoun in a case, and now the rest of the set arrives with it.

Grammar Lens: the same bend, a third time.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
subject: मैं. oblique: mujhe. meaning: to me.
subject: हम (ham). oblique: हमें. meaning: to us.
subject: तुम. oblique: तुम्हें. meaning: to you.
The possessive chapter bent -आ to -ए in front of a postposition. The imperative chapter bent the verb to match the listener. Here the pronoun itself bends, and the ending it takes is the nasal -ें.
That is three separate systems doing the same thing, which is the reason Hindi rewards a learner who looks for patterns rather than lists: the language keeps asking the same question — what stands after this word? — and answering it the same way.

Lesson 4 of 7.
āpko / use — the paradigm closes.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say hamẽ and tumhẽ. Two corners of the set are filled. Say the respectful one and the third-person one, and notice you cannot yet.

You'll want to know: āpko and use.
आपको (āpko) — "to you," respectfully. use — "to him, to her."
These two are built differently from the last pair. Instead of the nasal ending they take को (ko), the postposition meaning to:
आप + को becomes आपको.
Use is the worn-down version of the same thing: us + ko, squeezed together by centuries of use, exactly as maiṅ + ko became mujhe.

Grammar Lens: the whole set, at last.
[a table of 5 rows, read aloud]
subject: मैं. oblique: mujhe.
subject: हम (ham). oblique: हमें.
subject: तुम. oblique: तुम्हें.
subject: आप. oblique: आपको.
subject: वह. oblique: use.
Five forms, and only the first and the last look irregular — because they are the two that wore down. The middle three still show their parts.
वे (ve) has one too — unhẽ — built the same way, which the review lesson adds.
Note that the level you learned to choose in the possessive and imperative chapters is here too: tumhẽ to a friend, āpko to a stranger. The same decision, for the fourth time.

Lesson 5 of 7.
binā ārṭikal — the word Hindi does not have.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say वह, "that." Now try to say "the book" in Hindi, and read on when you find you have no word for the.

You'll want to know: there is no article.
Hindi has no article. No word for the, and no word for a.
This is the largest single structural difference between Hindi and English, and it is worth being told once rather than discovered slowly:
किताब — kitāb — can mean a book or the book.
The bare noun covers both. Nothing is missing from the sentence; the slot the English article fills does not exist.

Grammar Lens: what does the work instead.
Hindi has three ways to be more specific when it needs to be, and you have all three already:
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
want to say: a book, one book. Hindi reaches for: एक. example: ek kitāb.
want to say: that book, the one there. Hindi reaches for: वह. example: vah kitāb.
want to say: plain "book". Hindi reaches for: nothing at all. example: kitāb.
एक is the numeral one, pressed into service when indefiniteness matters — which is why ek chāy dījiye is "a tea, please" rather than a fixed café formula. It was a number the whole time.
वह points, and pointing is how Hindi says the when the really means that particular one.
Most of the time neither is needed, and this is the habit to build: do not reach for a word to translate the. There isn't one, and putting vah in every place English puts the sounds as odd as saying that book in English every time you mean the book.

Lesson 6 of 7.
sarvanām kī tālikā — subject and oblique, side by side.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, because the lesson points at something written down. You can listen to all of it now and come back to the parts that need looking at once you have stopped.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Name the five subject pronouns and as many obliques as you can, before reading the table.

Grammar Lens: the table, complete.
[a table of 6 rows, read aloud]
subject: मैं. oblique: mujhe.
subject: हम (ham). oblique: हमें.
subject: तुम. oblique: तुम्हें.
subject: आप. oblique: आपको.
subject: वह. oblique: use.
subject: वे (ve). oblique: unhẽ.
Two of the obliques are visibly को attached — āpko plainly, use after wear. Three take the nasal ending. The set is small and none of it is arbitrary once you know that a postposition is hiding in each one.

Grammar Lens: the fourth appearance of one decision.
[a table of 4 rows, read aloud]
system: possessive. at the tum level: तुम्हारा. at the āp level: आपका.
system: copula. at the tum level: हो. at the āp level: हैं.
system: command. at the tum level: बोलो. at the āp level: बोलिए.
system: oblique pronoun. at the tum level: तुम्हें. at the āp level: आपको.
Four systems, one decision. Choosing how to address someone settles all four at once, which is why the grid has been worth building across three chapters rather than learning four unrelated lists.

Lesson 7 of 7.
हम स्टेशन पर मिलेंगे (ham sṭeśan par milẽge) — the sentence the paper actually prints.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say हम (ham). Until this chapter the track could not say it, and a reading item that opens with it was unreadable from the first word.

Reading.
Read aloud. Every word has been taught:
हम स्टेशन पर मिलेंगे (ham sṭeśan par milẽge)। वे (ve) भी आ रहे हैं।.
Two sentences. Note what is not in the first one: no word for the in front of sṭeśan, because there is none to put there. An English speaker reads "we will meet at the station" and supplies the article themselves.

Grammar Lens: choosing between the obliques in a real question.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
asking: a friend. form: तुम्हें क्या पसंद है?
asking: a stranger. form: आपको क्या पसंद है?
The frame is the one the track has had since its liking chapter. What is new is that the pronoun in it can now change — it was never a fixed phrase, and the first word was always a pronoun in a case.
