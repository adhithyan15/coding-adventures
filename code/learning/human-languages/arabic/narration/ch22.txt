Arabic, chapter 22: Green and Yellow.
2 lessons. All 2 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 2.
أخضر, أصفر — "greens" you'd recognize, and a hidden zero.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Arabic's green word hides an English-style pun. Its yellow word hides something even better — the exact root behind the word "zero" itself.

Why it's said this way — أخضر — the same root as "vegetables," and a Quranic name.
أخضر (akhḍar, "green") comes from the root خ-ض-ر (kh-ḍ-r, "to be green"). That same root gives خضار (khuḍār, "vegetables") — echoing the exact same move English makes when it calls vegetables "greens." The same root also names the mysterious Quranic figure الخِضْر (al-Khiḍr), "the Green One" — traditionally said to be called this because the ground turned green wherever he sat or prayed.

Why it's said this way — أصفر — the same root as "zero" itself.
Here's the real surprise: أصفر (aṣfar, "yellow") shares its root, ص-ف-ر (ṣ-f-r), with صفر (ṣifr, "zero, empty, void") — the exact Arabic word that gave English both zero (via Italian zefiro/zero) and cipher (via Medieval Latin cifra). So the same three-letter Arabic root sits behind "yellow" and behind the number zero itself. The sources don't fully explain why "emptiness" and "yellow" share a root — but the shared root itself, connecting Arabic's yellow-word straight back to the origin of "zero," is genuine and well documented.

Lesson 2 of 2.
The head and the hand — five words the book met once — two body parts, two pairs of relatives and an age — which turn out to share one property: every root is older than Arabic itself.

Warm-up.
[pause 4 seconds]
Touch your head, then your hand, and give the Arabic for each. Both arrived a dozen lessons ago in single words and have not been asked for since.

Across the family: five words older than Arabic.
[a table of 5 rows, read aloud]
Arabic: رأس raʾs. meaning: head. Hebrew cousin: ראש rosh.
Arabic: يد yad. meaning: hand. Hebrew cousin: יד yad.
Arabic: أب ab. meaning: father. Hebrew cousin: אב av.
Arabic: أم umm. meaning: mother. Hebrew cousin: אם em.
Arabic: أخ akh. meaning: brother. Hebrew cousin: אח ach.
These are not borrowings in either direction. Both languages inherited them, and يد is the most striking of the set: spelled and said almost identically on both sides, worn down hardly at all in several thousand years.
رأس is the word inside Rosh Hashanah, the head of the year.

Across the family: what the roots still build.
None of these roots is a museum piece.
رئيس raʾīs — president, chief — is built straight on head, the same way English capital and captain grow out of Latin caput.
أم and أبو still form the kunya, a living honorific: mother of and father of, plus a child's name.
أخت ukht is أخ with a single letter added — the simplest gendering in the language, against the reshaped templates the colour words needed.

Grammar Lens: an age is a thing you own.
عمر ʿumr is age, lifetime, life span, from the root ع-م-ر, tied to living and flourishing.
كم عمرك؟ kam ʿumruka? — literally how much is your lifetime?
The Romance languages have their years; the Germanic ones are their years. Arabic does neither. It hangs the same your suffix on a noun and drops the verb altogether, exactly as the name and wellbeing questions do.
