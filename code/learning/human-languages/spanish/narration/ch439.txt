Spanish, chapter 439: Andenes Y Cierres.
7 lessons. All 7 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 7.
el andén — where you wait for the train.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say el tren, la estación and la parada. You are inside the station and the train has not come. Name the strip of concrete you are standing on.

You'll want to know: el andén.
el andén — the platform.
El tren sale del andén 4 — the train leaves from platform 4. Announcements put the number after the word, never before, and they say it fast: andén cuatro.
The written accent tells you where to put the weight: an-DÉN, on the last syllable. Without it the word would be read AN-den, and the stress is the part a listener uses to recognise it.

The word, taken apart.
It does not come apart, and this time that is not a trick. Nobody knows where andén comes from.
The tempting guess is andar, to walk, because a platform is a place you walk along. Dictionaries do not accept it: the endings do not line up, and the word turns up first in senses that have nothing to do with walking. Other proposals have been made and none has carried the field.
So the honest entry is origin uncertain, and that is worth meeting on purpose. Any book that produces an etymology for every word is inventing some of them. Spanish has a few hundred words like this, and the right response is to learn the word and let the history stay open.

Lesson 2 of 7.
trasladar — carried across.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say la oficina, el piso and el andén. All three are places a thing or a person can be taken from one of and to another.

You'll want to know: trasladar.
trasladar — to move something or someone from one place to another. trasladarse — to move yourself, to relocate.
Han trasladado la oficina al centro — they have moved the office to the centre. Me traslado a Sevilla en enero — I am relocating to Seville in January.
This is the formal word, the one on a letter from a bank or a clinic. For moving house among friends you would hear mudarse, but the notice on the door will say trasladado.

The word, taken apart.
tras- from Latin trans, across, plus -ladar from lātus, carried.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
tras- means across.
-lad- means carried.
Carried across. English has the same two pieces bolted together in translate, which is a text carried across into another language, and in relate, collate and dilate.

Why it's said this way.
Lātus is the odd piece, and it is worth a minute.
Latin's verb for carrying was ferre. Its past participle was not ferred or anything built from fer-; it was lātus, from a completely different root that had been absorbed into the same verb long before anyone wrote it down. A verb whose forms come from two unrelated stems is called suppletive, and English has the famous one: go and went, where went was borrowed whole from wend.
So trasladar carries a fossil. The -lad- in it is the past participle of a Latin verb whose present tense left no trace in the Spanish word at all.

Lesson 3 of 7.
cerrado — the word on the glass.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say cerrar and la puerta. The office has been trasladada and you are standing in front of the old door. Name what the card hanging on it says.

You'll want to know: cerrado.
cerrado — closed.
CERRADO POR VACACIONES — closed for holidays. La piscina está cerrada los lunes — the pool is closed on Mondays. It agrees like any adjective: cerrado, cerrada, cerrados, cerradas, matching the thing that is shut.
It also stretches past doors. Una curva cerrada is a tight bend; un acento cerrado is a thick accent you struggle to follow. The common thread is not letting anything through.

The word, taken apart.
cerrar plus -ado, the ending that turns an -ar verb into its past participle. Nothing exotic: hablar gives hablado, cerrar gives cerrado.
Underneath sits Latin claudere, to shut, which you met under cerrar. It is still visible in la clausura, excluir and English closure, clause and claustrophobia — a fear named for a shut room.

Grammar Lens: two participles, two shapes.
abrir gave you abierto, which does not follow the pattern. cerrar gives cerrado, which does. The pair sits on opposite sides of the same shop door, and their shapes do not match.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
verb: cerrar. participle: cerrado — regular.
verb: abrir. participle: abierto — irregular.
The regular ones you build; the irregular ones you learn one at a time, and there are only about a dozen that matter. Spanish put the irregularity on the oldest and most-used verbs, which is the usual bargain a language makes: the words you say constantly are the ones that resist tidying.

Lesson 4 of 7.
cortar — and what a cut road means.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say corto and el cuchillo. One is a shape, one is a tool, and the same idea is inside both. Name the verb.

You'll want to know: cortar.
cortar — to cut.
Corta el pan — cut the bread. Me he cortado — I have cut myself. Se ha cortado la llamada — the call has dropped, which Spanish sees as a line cut rather than a call lost.

The word, taken apart.
corto and cortar are the same Latin word, one taken as a description and one as an action. Curtus meant shortened, cut off.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
word: corto. the same idea as: the result of the cut.
word: cortar. the same idea as: the act of cutting.
English keeps curtus in curt, a reply cut short, and in curtail. The scissors in a barber's window and the adjective for a short skirt are relatives.

Why it's said this way.
Now the meaning that catches travellers out. On a road or a rail line, cortado does not mean damaged. It means closed.
VÍA CORTADA POR OBRAS.
A vía cortada is a line out of service. A calle cortada is a street shut to traffic, often for a market or a race, with nothing wrong with it at all.
Set it beside cerrado, which you met a moment ago, because the two are not interchangeable. A shop is cerrado: someone shut it and will open it again. A road is cortada: the through-route has been severed, and you must go round. Spanish picks the verb by how the way is blocked, and a sign that says cortada is telling you there is no waiting for it to reopen today.

Lesson 5 of 7.
tirar — the door that says pull.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say la caja and la puerta. The street was cortada, you carried an empty box round the long way, and now you want rid of it. Name what you do with the box.

You'll want to know: tirar.
tirar — to throw, and above all to throw away.
Tira la caja — throw the box out. No tires eso — do not throw that away. Tirar la basura is the everyday phrase for taking the rubbish out, and the bin itself is where things se tiran.

Why it's said this way.
Now the sign, and it surprises every English speaker exactly once.
TIRAR on a door means PULL.
The opposite side says EMPUJAR, push. A reader who knows tirar as throw reads the door as an instruction to hurl something, and gets it backwards.
The two senses are one idea seen from each end. Tirar is about applying force along a line away from where the thing sits: a box goes out of your hand, and a door comes out of its frame toward you. Spanish did not split the word; English happens to use two.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
the sign: TIRAR. what to do: pull.
the sign: EMPUJAR. what to do: push.
Read the door before you lean on it. The mistake is harmless and universal, and knowing it in advance saves you making it in a doorway with people behind you.

The word, taken apart.
It does not come apart, and like el andén at the start of this chapter its history is unsettled. A Germanic verb meaning to tear, reaching Spanish through the Goths, is the usual suggestion; an origin in the sound of a snapping cord has also been argued. Neither is agreed.
Two words in one chapter with no recoverable history is not unusual. The very common, very short, very old verbs are the ones whose trail goes cold soonest, because they were worn down long before anyone was writing Spanish at all.

Lesson 6 of 7.
(andén, trasladar, cerrado, cortar, tirar) — from cold.

Warm-up.
[pause 3 seconds]
Close the lessons before this one. Say all five: the platform, to move something across, closed, to cut, to throw away.

Grammar Lens: three signs that stop you, and they are not the same.
English puts closed on all three of these. Spanish does not, and the word it picks tells you what to do next.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
the sign: CERRADO. what it tells you: shut for now; come back later.
the sign: CORTADA. what it tells you: the route is severed; go round.
the sign: TRASLADADO. what it tells you: it is open, somewhere else.
A card reading TRASLADADO is the one worth recognising fastest, because it is the only one of the three with an address underneath it.

What you've built.
You can now read the signs that decide where you are allowed to walk. A station, a shut shop, a severed street and an office that has gone somewhere else are four different pieces of bad news, and you can tell them apart at a glance instead of guessing from context.
You also met the limit of etymology. el andén and tirar have no recoverable history, and the honest entry for both is origin uncertain. Every other word in this chapter and the last came apart; these two did not, and saying otherwise would mean making something up.

Why it's said this way.
Sign 2 repays a second look. Cortada al tráfico names what the cut applies to, and then the sign tells pedestrians where to go. A street can be cut to cars and open to feet, and Spanish says so in one phrase rather than two sentences.
Sign 4 is the one that saves your afternoon: trasladada, agreeing with oficina, followed by an address. Compare it with a card that said only CERRADO — that one would leave you waiting on a pavement for a door that is never opening again.
And when you finally reach number 40, the glass door will say TIRAR. Pull it.
