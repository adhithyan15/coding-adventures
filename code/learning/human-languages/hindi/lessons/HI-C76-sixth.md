## You'll want to know: छठा
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[HI-LEX-C76-ORDINAL-01]; assesses=[] -->

**छठा** (*chaṭhā*) — "**sixth**".

**छठा दिन** (*chaṭhā din*), **छठी किताब** (*chaṭhī kitāb*).

The last lesson gave you a rule one lesson ago and this is where it fails. **छह**
is six, and the sixth is **not** *chhahvā̃*. It is **छठा** (*chaṭhā*), with a retroflex **ठ**
that comes from nowhere in the cardinal.

## The word, taken apart: why this one resisted
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[] -->

Sanskrit had **षष्ठ** (*ṣaṣṭha*) for sixth, and Hindi inherited it directly,
worn down to *chaṭhā*. It never went through the *-वाँ* machinery because it was
already there when the machinery was built.

Which is the ordinary shape of this kind of hole: a rule spreads through a
language and the words that are said most often resist it longest, because they
are already known and nobody has to work them out. English does the same thing —
*went* is not *goed* for exactly this reason.

So the ordinals are five inherited words and one ending: *pahlā, dūsrā, tīsrā,
cauthā, chaṭhā*, and **-वाँ** for everything else. That is the whole system, and
the rest of this chapter is the ending doing its work.
