# midi — noon

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Two hours in the day do not get a number. They get a name, because
they are the pivots the rest of the day turns on. Here is the first.

## Sounds you'll need
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[FR-LEX-MIDI-05]; assesses=[] -->

- **midi** = *mee-DEE*, two clean syllables with the weight at the end.
- Both vowels are the tight French **i** of *machine*, never the *i* of *sit*.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[FR-ETYMON-MIDI-MEDIUS-06]; assesses=[] -->

**midi** is Latin **medius diēs**, "the middle day," and both halves are still
visible:

> **mi-** ("middle," from *medius*) + **-di** (*diēs*, "day") = **the day's
> middle**

You already own the second half. That **-di** is the same *diēs* that ends
*lundi* and *mardi* — here it has come round to the front of a different word.
And *medius* is the English **medium**, **median**, **mid** and **middle**, all
one family.

## Grammar Lens: a name instead of a number
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[] -->

Because **midi** *is* the hour, it does not take **heures**. Drop the number and
drop the noun:

> **Il est midi.** — "It is noon."

Not *il est douze heures*. The frame stays; what goes into it is a name.

## Why it's said this way: English noon slid
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[] -->

English **noon** is Latin **nōna hōra**, "the **ninth** hour" — which in Roman
reckoning was the middle of the afternoon. Over centuries the meal it named
crept earlier, and the word came with it until it landed on midday and stopped.
So English says *noon* and means "the ninth hour"; French says *midi* and means
what it says.
