# दोपहर का खाना / रात का खाना — the two meals you already know the words for

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[HI-CONCEPT-C37-KHAANA-01, HI-CONCEPT-C17-DOPAHAR-AADHI-RAAT-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] You have **खाना** (*khānā*), "food". You have **दोपहर** (*dopahar*)
and **रात** (*rāt*). Before reading on, guess what **दोपहर का खाना** is.

## You'll want to know: दोपहर का खाना and रात का खाना
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[HI-LEX-C79-MEALS-01, HI-LEX-C79-MEALS-02]; assesses=[] -->

**दोपहर का खाना** (*dopahar kā khānā*) — "lunch."
**रात का खाना** (*rāt kā khānā*) — "dinner."

You guessed them, and that is the point of putting them here rather than in a
word list. Neither is a new word. Each is **a time you already have, plus का,
plus the food-word you already have** — "the afternoon's food", "the night's
food."

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[HI-CONCEPT-C17-DOPAHAR-AADHI-RAAT-01] -->

The transparency runs deeper than the compound.

**दोपहर** is itself two pieces you can see: **दो** ("two") + **पहर**, a
traditional watch of roughly three hours. Two watches into the day is noon —
the word is a clock reading that turned into a time of day.

**रात** comes from Sanskrit **रात्रि** (*rātri*), the formal word that shows up
in **शुभ रात्रि** ("good night"). The everyday **रात** is the same word worn
down by use, which is why the greeting sounds more ceremonious than the noun.

So a three-word phrase for "dinner" contains a number, an obsolete time unit,
and a Sanskrit noun — and a learner who has met all three can read it cold.

## Grammar Lens: the pattern, not the pair
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[HI-LEX-C73-EATING-02] -->

Set the day's three meals beside each other:

| meal | Hindi | what it is built from |
|---|---|---|
| breakfast | नाश्ता | its own word, borrowed whole |
| lunch | दोपहर का खाना | a time + का + खाना |
| dinner | रात का खाना | a time + का + खाना |

**नाश्ता** is the odd one out: a single borrowed noun with no parts to see. The
other two are phrases Hindi builds on demand. That asymmetry is worth noticing
because it keeps recurring — Hindi borrows a whole word where it has one, and
builds a transparent phrase where it does not.
