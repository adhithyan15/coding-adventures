# ਦਿਲ — the one body word that is not Sanskrit

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[PA-LEX-AKKH, PA-LEX-NAKK, PA-LEX-DOST] -->

[PAUSE 2s] **ਅੱਖ**, **ਕੰਨ**, **ਮੂੰਹ**, **ਨੱਕ** — four body words, all inherited
from Sanskrit. This one breaks the pattern the same way **ਦੋਸਤ** once did.

## You'll want to know first — one word
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[PA-LEX-DIL]; assesses=[] -->

> **ਦਿਲ** — *dil* — **heart**

## The letters in this word
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[] -->

**ਦ** *d*, **ਿ** short *i*, **ਲ** *l*. Every letter already yours, from
**ਦੁੱਧ** and **ਲੈਣਾ**.

## The word, taken apart — borrowed, but a genuine cousin all the same
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[PA-ETYMON-DIL-HEART]; assesses=[PA-LEX-NAKK, PA-LEX-DOST, PA-ETYMON-DOST-CHOOSE] -->

**ਦਿਲ** is **Persian**, borrowed the way **ਦੋਸਤ** was — Punjabi's everyday
word for "heart" is not the word Sanskrit handed down. Sanskrit's own word,
**hṛdaya**, exists in Punjabi too, but only as a formal, literary borrowing;
ordinary speech reaches for *dil*.

And yet *dil* is not a stranger to Sanskrit's word. Both descend from the same
Proto-Indo-European root, \**kerd-*, "heart" — Persian's branch carried it to
*dil*, Sanskrit's branch carried it to *hṛdaya*, and a third branch carried it,
unbroken, to English **heart** itself. Three languages, three shapes, one
ancestor. What makes *dil* worth flagging is not the deep root — that root is
shared honestly — but the fact that Punjabi took its *everyday* word for heart
by borrowing sideways from Persian, rather than by inheriting straight down
from Sanskrit the way *akkh* and *nakk* did.
