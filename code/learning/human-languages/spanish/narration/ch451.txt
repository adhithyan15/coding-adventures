Spanish, chapter 451: La cola y el carné.
8 lessons. You can do the first 2 of them in the car; after that you will want to have stopped.


Lesson 1 of 8.
la cola — the queue.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say esperar and la entrada. Name what forms between the two.

You'll want to know: la cola.
la cola — the queue, the line of people.
Hay mucha cola — there's a long queue. Hacer cola — to queue: llevo media hora haciendo cola. Ponte a la cola — get in line. El último de la cola — the last person in the queue.
Spanish says hacer cola, never estar en cola. You make a queue by standing in it, which puts the work where it belongs.

Grammar Lens: two colas, and one of them is glue.
There are three separate words spelled cola in Spanish, and none of them is related to the others:
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
the word: la cola — tail, queue. where it comes from: Latin cauda.
the word: la cola — glue. where it comes from: Greek kólla.
the word: la cola — the drink. where it comes from: a West African word for the nut.
La cola del perro is the dog's tail. Un bote de cola is a pot of glue. Una cola at a bar is the drink. Nothing but context separates them, and context always does: nobody queues in a tube and nobody glues at the entrance.
Words that collide like this are called homonyms, and Spanish has fewer of them than English because its spelling is so regular that unrelated words rarely arrive at the same shape. When they do, it is worth knowing, because a dictionary will list them as two entries and you might otherwise think you had missed a connection.

The word, taken apart.
Latin cauda, tail.
The jump from tail to queue is one picture: a line of people trailing behind something, the way a tail trails behind an animal. French made the same jump with queue, which English then borrowed — and queue is also from cauda. So the English and Spanish words for a line of people are the same Latin word, arrived at independently.
The au closing to o is regular: causa gave cosa, paucum gave poco, taurum gave toro. The l is not. Spanish inherited the word through a spoken form coda, and the step from coda to cola is one nobody has a tidy rule for — which is worth saying, because most of the sound changes in this material do have one.

Lesson 2 of 8.
apuntar — put it down.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say el punto and la cola. The first is inside the verb you are about to meet.

You'll want to know: apuntar.
apuntar — to note down, to jot.
Apunta el número — write the number down. Voy a apuntarlo — I'll make a note of it.
And with -se, the meaning most useful at a counter: apuntarse, to put your name down, to sign up.
Me apunto a la excursión — I'm putting my name down for the trip. ¿Te apuntas? — are you in? That last one is an invitation to anything at all, and it is the phrase you will hear when a group is deciding something.

Grammar Lens: apuntar is not escribir.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
what you are doing: producing text. the verb: escribir.
what you are doing: capturing something before it escapes. the verb: apuntar.
You escribes a letter, an essay, a message. You apuntas a phone number, an address, a time somebody has said aloud. The difference is not length; it is purpose. Apuntar is always about not losing the thing.
That is why apuntarse means signing up: you are getting your name captured onto a list before the places run out.

The word, taken apart.
a-punt-ar, built straight on el punto, and behind that Latin punctum, a prick — from pungere, to pierce.
So apuntar is to put a point on something. All three of its senses are that one action: to note a thing down is to mark a point on paper, to apuntar a weapon is to aim its point, and to apuntarse is to put a point beside your own name.
The family is wide. A puncture is a piercing, a punctual person hits the point in time, and puntiagudo in Spanish is sharp-pointed. Every one of them has a small sharp thing in it.

Lesson 3 of 8.
el carné — the card with your name on it.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, because the lesson points at something written down. You can listen to all of it now and come back to the parts that need looking at once you have stopped.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say el documento and apuntarse. You sign up, and then they hand you something.

You'll want to know: el carné.
el carné — the card: an official one, with your name on it.
El carné de identidad — the ID card. El carné de conducir — the driving licence. El carné de la biblioteca — the library card. El carné joven — the youth discount card.
Plural los carnés. You will also see the French spelling carnet, which is common on forms and older signs.

Grammar Lens: carné is not tarjeta.
Two Spanish words land on English "card", and they divide the work cleanly:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
the card: proves who you are or what you may do. the word: el carné.
the card: pays, greets, or carries an address. the word: la tarjeta.
La tarjeta de crédito, la tarjeta de cumpleaños, la tarjeta de visita — none of those could be a carné. And a driving licence could never be a tarjeta, however much plastic it is made of.
The test is whether somebody official issued it about you. If yes, it is a carné.

The word, taken apart.
French carnet, a small notebook — from Old French quernet, and behind that Latin quaternum, a set of four.
Four what? Four sheets of paper folded together, which is how a small notebook was made. The same quaternum gave Spanish el cuaderno, and the two words are the same object seen at different sizes: a cuaderno stayed a notebook, while the French carnet shrank to the single card you carry.
Latin quattuor, four, is behind both, and behind cuatro, cuarto and cuarenta. A driving licence has four sheets of paper folded inside its history.

Lesson 4 of 8.
mostrar — show it.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say ver and el carné. Somebody at a desk wants to do the first to the second.

You'll want to know: mostrar.
mostrar — to show.
Es obligatorio mostrar el carné a la entrada.
Muéstrame la lista — show me the list. El gráfico muestra tres años — the chart shows three years.
In speech Spaniards more often say enseñar for this — enséñame el carné. Mostrar is what the sign says and what the form says, so it is a word you read more than you speak. Knowing which register a word belongs to is part of knowing the word.

Grammar Lens: the o that breaks again.
You have watched a stressed o turn into ue in la rueda. Here it is inside a verb, doing exactly the same thing:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
stress on the stem: muestro, muestras. stress on the ending: mostramos.
stress on the stem: muestra, muestran. stress on the ending: mostráis.
The infinitive mostrar has the plain o because the stress is on the -ar. Push the stress back onto the stem and the vowel breaks.
So this verb is not irregular in any interesting sense. It is one vowel obeying one condition, and the same condition you used to explain a noun now explains a conjugation.

The word, taken apart.
Latin monstrare, to point out — from monstrum, and that is where it gets interesting.
A monstrum was an omen: something strange that the gods had shown you as a warning. The horror came later, from the strangeness of the sign. So mostrar and monstruo are the same root, one keeping the showing and the other keeping the thing shown.
English has both halves as well, in demonstrate and monster, and the pair is a clean example of a root splitting into a neutral word and a frightening one while the idea inside stays the same: something put where you cannot miss it.

Lesson 5 of 8.
el ticket — the receipt.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say el billete and la entrada. Neither of them is what the till prints.

You'll want to know: el ticket.
el ticket — the receipt, the slip from the till.
¿Quiere el ticket? — would you like the receipt? Guarde el ticket para cambios — keep the receipt for exchanges. El ticket de la compra — the shopping receipt.
Said TI-ket. Spanish also writes it tique, and the shorter el recibo covers the same ground in more formal use — on an invoice, or where money has changed hands between businesses.

Grammar Lens: three slips of paper, three words.
English calls all of these a ticket. Spanish will not:
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
what it does: carries you somewhere. the word: el billete.
what it does: gets you in. the word: la entrada.
what it does: proves you paid. the word: el ticket.
A train journey is un billete. A concert is una entrada. What the supermarket hands you is un ticket, and asking for un billete at a till would suggest you wanted a banknote, since billete covers that too.
Sort them by the job rather than by the paper, and all three stay apart.

The word, taken apart.
This word has been around the houses.
Old French estiquier meant to stick — a Frankish word, Germanic in origin, the same family as English stick. From it came estiquet, a little notice stuck onto something: a label.
English borrowed estiquet and wore it down to ticket, meanwhile narrowing it from any label to a paid-for slip. Spanish then borrowed the English word back, keeping the English spelling.
Spanish also kept the original French form separately, as la etiqueta — a label, and by extension the rules of polite behaviour, which were once literally the notices posted at court telling visitors how to behave. One Frankish word for sticking something on a surface, and it has ended up as a supermarket receipt and as the word etiquette.

Lesson 6 of 8.
revisar — look at it again.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, because the lesson points at something written down. You can listen to all of it now and come back to the parts that need looking at once you have stopped.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say ver and el aviso. Both have the same Latin verb inside them, and so does the word coming next.

You'll want to know: revisar.
revisar — to check, to go over, to inspect.
Voy a revisar el ticket — I'll check the receipt. Han revisado el coche — they've serviced the car. Revisa las respuestas antes de entregar — check your answers before handing it in.
Two nouns come with it: la revisión, the check-up or the service, and el revisor, the inspector who comes down the train.

Grammar Lens: revisar is not mirar.
Three verbs of looking, now sorted:
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
what happens: it arrives at your eyes. the verb: ver.
what happens: you aim your eyes at it. the verb: mirar.
what happens: you go over it for faults. the verb: revisar.
Veo el ticket — the receipt is in my field of vision. Miro el ticket — I direct my attention at it. Reviso el ticket — I am checking whether they charged me twice.
Only the third carries the idea of a verdict at the end. That is why a mechanic revisa a car and a passenger merely mira the timetable.

The word, taken apart.
re-vis-ar: the prefix re-, again, on the stem vis- from Latin videre, to see, through its participle visus.
So revisar is to see again — the second look, which is the one where you find what the first look missed.
You have met this root twice already without the seeing being visible. Ver is videre worn down to two letters. El aviso is a-vis-o, a bringing to someone's sight. Add revisar and you have three words from one Latin verb, each hiding it to a different degree. The English cousins run alongside: vision, advise, revise.

Grammar Lens: which slip of paper.
Four words now cover what English calls a card or a ticket. The sort is by job, not by material:
[a table of 4 rows, read aloud]
what it does: says who you are. the word: el carné.
what it does: carries you somewhere. the word: el billete.
what it does: gets you in. the word: la entrada.

Read the left-hand column aloud and the right-hand one answers itself. Try to remember it as four pieces of plastic and paper and it will not, because three of them look identical.

Grammar Lens: two verbs you already half-owned.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
new verb: apuntar. what it was built on: el punto, and behind it pungere, to pierce.
new verb: revisar. what it was built on: ver and el aviso, and behind them videre.
new verb: mostrar. what it was built on: monstrum, an omen — something shown.
Revisar is the clearest case in the chapter. You have been saying ver since the beginning and el aviso for many chapters, without either one looking like Latin videre. The third member makes the family visible, and now all three are cheaper to hold than any one of them was alone.

Why it's said this way.
The notice asks for two pieces of plastic and the words are not interchangeable. The carné de socio says who you are; the ticket says you paid. Lose the first and they cannot identify you; lose the second and they cannot see what you bought. So step three deals only with the missing ticket, and identity is still required.
Then look at the verbs. Muestre, apúntese, haga are all usted commands — the register of any counter in Spain. Apúntese carries its pronoun on the end and takes a written accent to hold the stress where it was in apunte; without the mark you would read apuntese with the stress in the wrong place.
Revisamos breaks the pattern deliberately. The first three lines tell you what to do; the fourth says what we do. Moving to nosotros at the end is how a notice stops giving orders and starts making a promise.

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Now your turn, out loud and then in writing. Four numbered lines for a counter notice: tell people to queue, to show a card, to keep the receipt, and say that you check each case within two days.
Then check yourself: did you use the usted command for the instructions and switch to nosotros for what the office does?
