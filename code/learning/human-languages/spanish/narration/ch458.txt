Spanish, chapter 458: El plazo.
5 lessons. The first lesson already needs your eyes or your hands, so save this one for when you have stopped.


Lesson 1 of 5.
el plazo — by when.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, because the lesson points at something written down. You can listen to all of it now and come back to the parts that need looking at once you have stopped.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say el placer and la fecha. One of those two is an odd relative of the word coming next.

You'll want to know: el plazo.
el plazo — the period allowed, and so the deadline.
Plazo de presentación: hasta el 30 de abril.
Fuera de plazo — past the deadline. En el plazo de diez días — within ten days. A corto plazo — in the short term.
A second sense on any purchase: pagar a plazos — to pay in instalments, each one a period agreed in advance.

Grammar Lens: plazo is not fecha.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
what it names: a day on the calendar. the word: la fecha.
what it names: the stretch of time you are allowed. the word: el plazo.
La fecha límite is the last day; el plazo is the whole window that ends there. So el plazo termina el viernes and la fecha límite es el viernes say the same thing from opposite ends — one names the run, the other the wall at the end of it.
Most official Spanish prefers plazo, because a form cares how long you have, not which square on the calendar.

The word, taken apart.
Latin placitum — "a thing agreed", the past participle of placēre, to please. What pleased both parties was what they had settled on.
That makes el plazo a relative of el placer, which you already have. Pleasure and a deadline sit oddly together until you see the middle step: placitum meant an agreement, then the term of an agreement, then the time you have got.
French took the same placitum and made plaid, a court hearing, and English has plea and plead from it — all of them agreements argued over. Spanish kept the calmest branch: a plazo is what was agreed, and the paperwork holds you to it.

Lesson 2 of 5.
entregar — hand it over.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say el documento and el almacén. Things leave both of them the same way.

You'll want to know: entregar.
entregar — to hand in, hand over, deliver.
Entregar los papeles — to hand the paperwork in. Hay que entregarlo antes del viernes — it has to be in before Friday. El paquete se entrega mañana — the parcel is delivered tomorrow.
La entrega is the noun: la entrega a domicilio is home delivery, and el plazo de entrega is the delivery time — both of this chapter's first two words in one phrase.

Grammar Lens: entregar is not dar.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
what is happening: passing something to somebody. the verb: dar.
what is happening: handing over what was owed or due. the verb: entregar.
Dame el boli — pass me the pen. Entrega el boli would sound as though the pen were evidence.
Entregar carries an obligation with it: homework, a form, a parcel, a set of keys. Something was due, and now it has gone across. That is why a courier entrega and a friend da.

The word, taken apart.
Latin integrare, to make whole — from integer, untouched, complete, which is in- plus the root of tangere, to touch. An integer is a thing nobody has broken into.
So entregar is to hand a thing over entire — the sense of a delivery that arrives with nothing missing.
The same integer gave Spanish entero, whole, and íntegro, which is the bookish twin and means upright about a person. English took the number sense as integer and the character sense as integrity — and integrity is exactly what entregar asks for: the thing handed over undiminished.

Lesson 3 of 5.
admitir — let it through.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say meter and aceptar. The word coming next is a cousin of the first and a rival of the second.

You'll want to know: admitir.
admitir — to allow in, to accept as valid, and to own up to.
No se admiten solicitudes fuera de plazo.
No se admiten perros — no dogs. Admitir un error — to admit a mistake. ¿Admiten tarjeta? — do you take cards, on a sign or a form.
La admisión is the noun, and admitido is the word beside your name on a list when you are in.

Grammar Lens: admitir, aceptar, entregar.
Three verbs about a thing crossing a line, sorted by who is doing what:
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
the move: you agree to take what is offered. the verb: aceptar.
the move: an institution lets it through. the verb: admitir.
the move: you hand over what was due. the verb: entregar.
You entregas the form; the office admite it or not; a shop acepta your card. Notice who is behind admitir: nearly every time, an institution. That is why it turns up in the se form on notices — no se admiten — with nobody named.
The other sense follows from the same picture: to admit a mistake is to let it in rather than keep it outside.

The word, taken apart.
Latin admittere: ad-, towards, plus mittere, to send.
You have met mittere three times already, and this makes four:
[a table of 4 rows, read aloud]
word: meter. what the prefix does: the bare verb, worn down: put in.
word: el permiso. what the prefix does: per-, sent through.
word: permitir. what the prefix does: the verb of that noun, let through.
word: admitir. what the prefix does: ad-, sent towards — let in.
So the family is a set of sendings, and the prefix does all the steering. English holds the same shelf: admit, permit, submit, transmit, commit, mission — every one a sending in some direction.
Meter is the one that came down through speech, which is why it looks least like the others and is the only one you would use about a box.

Lesson 4 of 5.
(el plazo, entregar, admitir) — from cold.

Warm-up.
[pause 3 seconds]
Close the lessons before this one. Say all three: the deadline, to hand in, to let in.

Grammar Lens: the three of them are one sentence.
Put them in the order they happen and you have the whole of official life:
Entregue la solicitud dentro del plazo. Fuera de plazo no se admiten.
You hand it in, there is a window, and the office decides. Three words, and every form you meet in Spanish will use at least two of them.
Note dentro del plazo against fuera de plazo: the first takes an article, the second does not, because fuera de + bare noun is the fixed frame you met in fuera de servicio.

Grammar Lens: mittere, for the third time.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
word: meter. the prefix, and what it does: none — put in.
word: el permiso. the prefix, and what it does: per-, sent through.
word: admitir. the prefix, and what it does: ad-, sent towards, let in.
One Latin verb, three Spanish words, and the prefix is the whole difference. Meter came down through speech and looks least like the others; permiso and admitir were lifted off the page and kept their shape — the same split you saw in rueda against rotativo.
And entregar hides a different picture: integrare, to make whole, so what you hand over should arrive entire.

Why it's said this way.
Aunque queden plazas is the hard line, and it is where the grammar earns its keep: aunque with the subjunctive — queden, not quedan — says the office is not conceding that there are places. Had it written aunque quedan plazas it would have been admitting there are some and refusing anyway, which reads as spite rather than procedure.
So the subjunctive is doing something worth having: it lets a notice state a rule without commenting on the facts.
Then no se admiten again, with nobody named. Compare the three verbs in their natural order: usted entrega, la oficina admite, and you would aceptar only what is offered to you. The notice never says who decides, because in official Spanish the decision is a thing that happens rather than a person who acts.

Then check yourself: did you use entregar for the handing in and admitir for the office, and did fuera de plazo go without an article?
