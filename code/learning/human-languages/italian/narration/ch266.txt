Italian, chapter 266: Dovrei, Spiegare --- Advice, and the Shape of an Explanation.
3 lessons. All 3 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 3.
dovrei — what you ought to do about it.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Ask for a coffee politely. (Vorrei un caffè.) Then run a reason forward: "I'm tired, so I'm staying at home." (Sono stanco, quindi resto a casa.)
[pause 1 second]
An explanation usually ends on a third move: what somebody ought to do. That move has its own form.

You'll want to know: dovrei.
You have dovere, "to have to, must". Soften it and it becomes advice:
Dovrei studiare. — I should study. Dovresti dormire. — You should sleep. (to a friend) Dovrebbe riposare. — He or she should rest; and, to someone you call Lei, "you should rest".
A bare infinitive follows, with no di and no che, exactly as after vorrei. And listen to the ending: dovrei, vorrei. It is the same -ei. Both are the condizionale, the "would" form of Italian, which you have been using since chapter eighty-six without its name. Chapter two hundred and seventy-two builds it properly. For now, two forms of it are yours.
Devo studiare is "I must study": no way out. Dovrei studiare is "I should": I know I ought to, and I have not promised. That softness is the register an explanation usually wants.

The word, taken apart.
dovere is inherited from Latin dēbēre, "to owe" (EtymDB 2.1), and dēbēre is a compound: dē, "from", plus habēre, "to have" (Lewis & Short). To owe is to have something from someone: you are holding what is theirs. You met the noun in chapter two hundred and fifty-three: il debito, "a debt". English took the same Latin word as debt, due and duty.

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
What is the difference between devo and dovrei? (Devo is "I must"; dovrei is "I should" — softer.) Which form you already knew has the same ending? (Vorrei.) What are the two Latin pieces of dēbēre? (Dē + habēre — to have from someone.)


Lesson 2 of 3.
spiegare — to unfold.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Three pieces are in your hands: secondo me for an opinion, perché and quindi for the two arrows, dovrei for advice. This lesson puts them in a row, under the verb that names the act.

Grammar Lens: the shape of an explanation.
You met spiegare, "to explain", in chapter one hundred and forty-one. It is a regular -are verb with one spelling catch: spiego, spieghi, spiega. The h in spieghi keeps the g hard before the i, the same trick the h plays in che.
Here is a whole explanation, every word of it taught:
— Perché resti a casa? — Secondo me piove. E ho il raffreddore, perché ieri faceva freddo. Quindi resto a casa. Dovrei dormire. — Why are you staying at home? — If you ask me, it's going to rain. And I've got a cold, because it was cold yesterday. So I'm staying at home. I ought to sleep.
Four moves, and a word for each: an opinion (secondo me), a cause pointing back (perché), a consequence pointing forward (quindi), and advice (dovrei). With both arrows, you can build it cause first or claim first.

The word, taken apart: folded, and unfolded.
piegare, "to fold", which you met in chapter one hundred and eighty, is inherited from Latin plicāre, "to fold" (EtymDB 2.1). spiegare is the same verb with Latin ex-, "out", worn down to an s-: Latin explicāre, "to unfold, to spread out" (Lewis & Short, under explico). EtymDB 2.1 has no row for spiegare, so that pairing is the usual reading rather than a cited one.
To explain is to unfold: something was folded shut, and you open it out. English kept the Latin in explicit, and complicated is the opposite, folded together. explain is another verb, Latin explānāre, "to make plain" (EtymDB 2.1).

[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Name the four moves of an explanation and a word for each. (Opinion secondo me, cause perché, consequence quindi, advice dovrei.) Why is there an h in spieghi? (To keep the g hard.) What is spiegare, taken apart? (Piegare with ex-: to unfold.)


Lesson 3 of 3.
Review — explain yourself.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say "you should sleep" to a friend. (Dovresti dormire.) Which English word for owing shares its Latin? (Debt, or due, or duty.)

What you've built.
Two chapters, four moves. You can say what you think, why, what follows from it, and what ought to be done. And because chapter two hundred and sixty-three gave you the scene, your reasons can reach into the past:
— Perché non sei andato al mercato? — Faceva freddo ed ero stanco, quindi sono restato a casa. Secondo me dovrei uscire di più.
The reason is scene, faceva and ero; the consequence is an event, sono restato; the advice is about now. Three times in one answer.

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
In faceva freddo, quindi sono restato a casa, which half is the scene and which the event? (Faceva freddo is the scene; sono restato is the event.) What does spiegare do, literally? (It unfolds.) And what is softer than devo? (Dovrei.)
