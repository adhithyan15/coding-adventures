Italian, chapter 269: How It Felt --- Feelings and Hopes.
3 lessons. All 3 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 3.
ero contento — how it felt.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say "I was tired, but I was happy". (Ero stanco, ma ero contento, or ero stanca, ma ero contenta.)
[pause 1 second]
You said that sentence in chapter two hundred and sixty-three. This lesson is about why it worked.

Grammar Lens: a feeling is scene.
You already own the feelings: contento, felice, triste, stanco, nervoso. Describing an experience is putting them in the past, and the past they want is the imperfetto:
Prima dell'esame ero nervoso. Poi ero felice. — Before the exam I was nervous. Then I was happy.
A feeling has no edge. It was going on, like the weather, so in a story it is usually scene: ero. Sono stato felice exists too, for a feeling summed up as one finished stretch: sono stato felice a Roma, the whole stay.
And the adjective agrees with whoever felt it. Contento is a regular four-ending adjective: contento, contenta, contenti, contente. Felice and triste end in -e and have only two: felice for one, felici for more, man or woman.
Eravamo contenti. — We were happy. Mia madre era felice. — My mother was happy.

Grammar Lens: contento di.
To say what you were happy about, add di:
Ero contento del viaggio. — I was pleased with the journey. Sono felice di essere qui. — I'm happy to be here.
di + il is del, the same welding as a + il in al. Before an infinitive, di stays as it is.

The word, taken apart.
contento comes from Latin contentus, "satisfied", the participle of continēre, "to hold together, to contain", itself con- + tenēre, "to hold". Lewis & Short, under contineo, tie contentus to the verb's sense "to hold back, to keep in check": to be content is to hold yourself in, wanting nothing further. English kept both senses apart: the content of a box, and a content person. EtymDB 2.1 links the Italian word to contentus, though it also mixes in a different Latin word, contemptus, "scorn", which is not its source.

Lesson 2 of 3.
spero di — saying what you are reaching for.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say "I'm happy to be here". (Sono felice di essere qui.) Then "I would like to travel". (Vorrei viaggiare.)
[pause 1 second]
An experience has a past and a future. Last lesson was how it felt; this one is what you want next.

You'll want to know: spero di.
spero di + infinitive — "I hope to".
Spero di viaggiare. — I hope to travel. Spero di imparare bene l'italiano. — I hope to learn Italian well.
You have sperare, "to hope"; spero is its "I" form. The di is the same small word as in contento di and credo di sì: it hooks the infinitive on, and Italian will not leave it out. English says I hope to; Italian says spero di.
The di works when the hoper and the doer are the same person. Hoping something for someone else takes spero che and the congiuntivo, which is a later chapter's work.

Grammar Lens: il mio sogno è.
An ambition is bigger than a hope, and it has its own frame:
Il mio sogno è vivere al mare. — My dream is to live by the sea. Il mio sogno è studiare all'università. — My dream is to study at university.
il sogno, "the dream", you have; the article stays in front of mio, as it has throughout this book. After è the infinitive follows bare, with no di: the dream is the doing.
Three strengths, then, for the future: vorrei viaggiare, I'd like to; spero di viaggiare, I hope to; il mio sogno è viaggiare, it is my dream.

The phrase, taken apart.
sperare is inherited from Latin spērāre, "to hope, to expect" (EtymDB 2.1; Lewis & Short, under spero). English desperate is the same verb with dē- in front, "hope taken away", and la speranza, "hope", which you met in chapter one hundred and eighty-six, is its noun.

Lesson 3 of 3.
Review — how it felt, and what comes next.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say "I hope to travel". (Spero di viaggiare.)

What you've built.
An experience told well has two ends. The feeling looks back, in the imperfetto; the hope looks forward, with spero di or il mio sogno è.
Il viaggio era lungo. Ho perso la coincidenza, quindi ero arrabbiato. Ma alla fine ero contento del viaggio. Spero di tornare.
[pause 2 seconds]
Follow the feelings. Ero arrabbiato came from an event, ho perso, and quindi joined them; ero contento closed the story; spero di tornare opened the next one.
