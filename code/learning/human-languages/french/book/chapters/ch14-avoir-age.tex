\chapter{To Have, and How Old You Are}
\label{ch:avoir-age}

The verb French leans on hardest, and the first thing it lets you say that
English cannot say the same way: your age. French does not \emph{be} twenty ---
it \emph{has} twenty years. \emph{Practice lessons:
\texttt{lessons/FR-C14-*.md}.}

\section{\emph{avoir} --- ``to have''}
\label{lesson:avoir}

The verb French leans on hardest. It's irregular, it's everywhere, and --- as
you'll see --- you have already met its root without knowing it.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}
\toprule
Singular & Plural \\
\midrule
\emph{j'\textbf{ai}} (I have) & \emph{nous \textbf{avons}} (we have) \\
\emph{tu \textbf{as}} (you have) & \emph{vous \textbf{avez}} (you have) \\
\emph{il/elle \textbf{a}} (he/she has) & \emph{ils/elles \textbf{ont}} (they have) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{sounds}
Say the singular column: \emph{ai, as, a} --- three forms, and they all sound
the \emph{same}. The written endings differ; your ear can't tell them apart.
Only the pronoun tells you who.\\[3pt]
Note \emph{j'ai}, not \emph{je ai}: before a vowel, \emph{je} loses its
\emph{e}. That is the elision French does everywhere --- the same move that
turns \emph{le eau} into \emph{l'eau}.
\end{sounds}

\begin{cousinweb}
\textbf{avoir} (``to have'') $\leftarrow$ Latin \emph{hab\=ere}. And the verb
\emph{habiter} (``to live somewhere'') $\leftarrow$ \emph{habit\=are} --- which
is just \emph{hab\=ere} run through a ``keep doing it'' pattern: \emph{to keep
having a place}. Same root, both verbs.
\end{cousinweb}

The family in English is large, because English borrowed it whole:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}
\toprule
English word & Inside it \\
\midrule
\textbf{habit} & what you \emph{have} regularly \\
\textbf{inhabit} & to \emph{have} a place --- $=$ \emph{habiter} \\
\textbf{exhibit} & to \emph{hold} out \\
\textbf{prohibit} & to \emph{hold} back \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{grammarlens}
\textbf{How far it wore down.} Latin \emph{habe\=o} (``I have'') $\to$ French
\emph{j'ai}. Four syllables of Latin reduced to a \emph{single vowel sound}.
French does this more than any of its sisters --- compare Italian \emph{ho},
Spanish \emph{he}, and the Latin \emph{aqua} that French ground down to
\emph{eau}.
\end{grammarlens}

\begin{sounds}
\textbf{Practice.} Say the six: \emph{j'ai, tu as, il a, nous avons, vous avez,
ils ont}. Then say the three that sound alike --- \emph{ai} $\cdot$ \emph{as}
$\cdot$ \emph{a}. Then a whole sentence: \emph{J'ai un fr\`{e}re} (``I have a
brother''). Then the root pair: \emph{avoir} $\cdot$ \emph{habiter}.
\end{sounds}

\section{\emph{j'ai vingt ans} --- having your years}
\label{lesson:age}

Ask a French speaker their age and the answer uses \emph{avoir}. Not ``I am
twenty'' --- ``\textbf{I have twenty years}.'' English can't do this; Spanish
and Italian do exactly the same thing.

\begin{center}\itshape
--- Quel \^{a}ge as-tu ? --- ``What age do you \emph{have}?''\\
--- J'ai vingt ans. --- ``I \emph{have} twenty years.''
\end{center}

\begin{grammarlens}
Two things to notice. First, the verb is \textbf{avoir}, never \emph{\^{e}tre}
--- \emph{je suis vingt ans} is simply wrong. Second, \emph{ans} is compulsory:
English drops it (``I'm twenty''); French cannot.
\end{grammarlens}

\begin{cousinweb}
\textbf{an} (``year'') $\leftarrow$ Latin \emph{annus}, which gives English
\textbf{annual}, \textbf{anniversary}, \textbf{annals}, and \emph{per annum}.
You have already met its descendants: the month names come from the same
calendar.
\end{cousinweb}

\begin{sounds}
Careful with the sound: \emph{an} is \textbf{nasal}, and in \emph{vingt ans} the
silent \emph{t} of \emph{vingt} \emph{wakes up} and links ---
\emph{vin-\textbf{t}-an}. That is \textbf{liaison}, French's habit of reviving a
dead consonant when a vowel follows.
\end{sounds}

Who else does it this way:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
Language & ``I am 20'' & Verb \\
\midrule
French & \emph{j'\textbf{ai} vingt \textbf{ans}} & have \\
Spanish & \emph{t\textbf{engo} veinte a\~{n}os} & have \\
Italian & \emph{h\textbf{o} venti anni} & have \\
Portuguese & \emph{t\textbf{enho} vinte anos} & have \\
German & \emph{ich \textbf{bin} zwanzig Jahre alt} & be \\
English & \emph{I \textbf{am} twenty} & be \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

A clean split: \textbf{Romance has its years; Germanic is its years.} Age as
something you \emph{carry} versus age as something you \emph{are}.

\begin{culture}
\textbf{Practice.} Say \emph{Quel \^{a}ge as-tu ?} Then \emph{J'ai vingt ans},
with the liaison --- \emph{vin-t-an}. Then your own age, using the numbers you
have. Then the split itself: French \emph{a} its years; English \emph{is} its
years. Next chapter: what French builds \emph{out of} \emph{avoir} --- a whole
past tense.
\end{culture}
