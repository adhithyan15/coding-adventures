# બે and ત્રણ — two surprises

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GU-LEX-NUMBERS-ONE-TO-FIVE] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Count to five once more: *ek, be, traṇ, chār, pā̃ch*. Three of the
five will look familiar from other Indian languages. Which two won't?

## What you've built: two surprises
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[GU-FORM-BE-INITIAL-B, GU-FORM-TRAN-R]; assesses=[] -->

Most neighbouring languages have a *d* in “two,” but Gujarati says **બે** *be*.
Hindi says *tīn* for “three,” but Gujarati says **ત્રણ** *traṇ*, with an *r*.

Do not carry both histories while the count is still new. Make the two forms
automatic first; the next lesson explains why *be* and *traṇ* took those
shapes.

- [YOU SAY: "two" — Gujarati **be**, with *b*, not *d*]
- [YOU SAY: "three" — Gujarati **traṇ**, with an *r*]

## Wrap-up Recall
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GU-FORM-BE-INITIAL-B, GU-FORM-TRAN-R] -->

[PAUSE 3s] Why does **બે** look unlike its neighbours? (It begins with *b*, not
*d*.) What does **ત્રણ** contain that Hindi *tīn* does not? (*r*.) Next: trace
the two surprising number histories.
