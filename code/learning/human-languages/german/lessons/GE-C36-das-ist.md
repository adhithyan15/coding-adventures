# das ist — introducing somebody who is not you

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GE-LEX-FREUT-MICH-02, GE-LEX-ICH-HEISSE-01, GE-LEX-MEIN-NAME-IST-02, GE-GRAMMAR-SEIN-ER-IST-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] You can give your own name three ways and shake hands afterwards.
You cannot yet introduce anybody else, and the phrase that does it is two words
you already have.

## You'll want to know: das ist
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[GE-LEX-DAS-IST-01]; assesses=[GE-LEX-MEIN-01, GE-LEX-BRUDER-01, GE-LEX-SCHWESTER-01, GE-GRAMMAR-SEIN-ER-IST-01, GE-LEX-FREUT-MICH-02] -->

> **das ist** — "this is", "that is".

> **Das ist mein Bruder.** — "This is my brother."

> **Das ist meine Schwester.** — "This is my sister."

And the reply is one you have owned since your first introduction: **Freut mich.**

## Grammar Lens: the das that ignores gender
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GE-LEX-DAS-IST-01, GE-LEX-DER-DIE-DAS-02, GE-ETYMON-ARTIKEL-03, GE-LEX-MEIN-01] -->

Look at the second sentence again. **Schwester** is a *die*-word, and the phrase
still opens with **das**. That is not a mistake, and it is not the article.

The **das** at the front of the sentence is the older, pointing word the article
descends from — the demonstrative behind English **that**. It points at a person
without committing to a gender, so it stays **das** in front of everybody:

> **Das ist mein Vater.** · **Das ist meine Mutter.** · **Das ist mein Hund.**

English does the same thing and hides it as well: *that is my sister* uses a
word that started life as the neuter *þæt* and long ago stopped caring.

## Wrap-up Recall
