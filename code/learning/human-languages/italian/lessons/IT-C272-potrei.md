# potrei, vorrei, dovrei — the would of Italian

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[IT-LEX-C86-VOLERE86-02, IT-LEX-DOVREI-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say "I would like a coffee". (*Vorrei un caffè.*) Say "I should
study". (*Dovrei studiare.*)

[PAUSE 1s] Two forms of one tense, and you have known one of them since
chapter eighty-six. This lesson gives the tense its name and its recipe.

## Grammar Lens: the -ei family
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[IT-GRAMMAR-CONDIZIONALE-01]; assesses=[IT-LEX-C85-POTERE85-01, IT-GRAMMAR-PARLARE-04, IT-LEX-CAFFE-02] -->

This is the **condizionale**, Italian's "would". Build it on the whole
infinitive, minus its final *-e*, and add **-ei** for "I":

> parlare, **parlerei** — I would speak *(the -ar- turns to -er-)*
> credere, **crederei** — I would believe
> dormire, **dormirei** — I would sleep

The other persons change only the ending: **parlerei**, **parleresti**,
**parlerebbe**, **parleremmo**, **parlereste**, **parlerebbero**.

A few common verbs squeeze their infinitive first, and these are the three
you meet most:

> potere, **potrei** — I could · volere, **vorrei** — I'd like · dovere,
> **dovrei** — I should

Three jobs, all of them about softening. A **polite request**: *Potrei avere
un caffè?* — Could I have a coffee? A **wish**: *Vorrei viaggiare.* And
**advice**: *Al tuo posto, studierei.* — In your place, I'd study.

## The word, taken apart: "I had to speak"
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[IT-ETYMON-CONDIZIONALE-02]; assesses=[IT-LEX-AVERE-02] -->

Latin had no would-tense. Spoken Latin made one by gluing a past of *habēre*,
"to have", onto the infinitive: roughly "I had to speak", which slid into "I
would speak". Italian chose the perfect, *habuī*, "I had", whose Italian
descendant is *ebbi*; worn down on the end of a verb it became **-ei**, and
*ebbe*, "he had", stayed nearly whole in **parlerebbe**. French and Spanish
glued on a different past of the same verb, so their endings do not match.
Neither EtymDB nor Lewis & Short covers a change this late; this is the usual
explanation in histories of Romance, given as that. The *habēre* is the same one
that became **avere**.
