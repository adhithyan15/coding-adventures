# conmigo · contigo — the word that says "with" twice

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-GRAMMAR-TONIC-PRONOUN-AFTER-PREPOSITION, ES-ORTHOGRAPHY-MI-ACCENT] -->

[PAUSE 2s] *Para mí*, *para ti*. The pattern is steady: preposition, then the
stressed pronoun.

[PAUSE 1s] One preposition refuses to follow it.

## The word, taken apart: conmigo
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-CONMIGO, ES-LEX-CON, ES-ETYMON-CONMIGO-MECUM]; assesses=[] -->

***Con*** means *with*. But *with me* is not *con mí*:

> *¿Vienes* ***conmigo****?* — Are you coming with me?

[PAUSE 2s] Here is where it came from, and it is one of the best stories in the
language. Latin did not put *cum* in front of a pronoun; it stuck it on the
**end**: ***mecum***, literally *me-with*.

[PAUSE 2s] Spanish inherited that as *-migo* — and by then nobody could hear a
preposition in it any more. It had become a meaningless tail. So Spanish put
*con* back on the **front**, where prepositions belong.

[PAUSE 2s] Which means ***conmigo*** contains *with* **twice**: once at each
end, in two different languages, fifteen centuries apart. And *contigo* does
exactly the same.

## Why it's said this way
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-CONMIGO, ES-GRAMMAR-APOCOPE-PATTERN] -->

[PAUSE 2s] This is the same instinct you saw turn *mucho* into *muy* and *a el*
into *al*, running the other way. Spanish reshapes small words to keep them
audible — usually by wearing them down, and here, once, by **doubling** one
that had worn away to nothing.

[PAUSE 2s] There are only two of these. From *él* onward the ordinary pattern
returns: *con él*, *con ella*.

[PAUSE 2s] Why does *conmigo* begin with *con* when it already ends in one?

[PAUSE 2s] How many pronouns do this? (**Two** — *conmigo*, *contigo*.)
