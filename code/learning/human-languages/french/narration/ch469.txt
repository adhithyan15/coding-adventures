French, chapter 469: De quelle humeur ?.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
l'humeur — mood.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the French for cunning, then the French for to lie.

You'll want to know: l'humeur.
l'humeur — "mood".
Ce matin, je suis de bonne humeur. This morning I am in a good mood.
De bonne humeur — in a good mood; de mauvaise humeur — in a bad mood. It is feminine: une humeur.

The word, taken apart.
l'humeur is inherited from Latin ūmor "moisture, fluid" (EtymDB; Lewis & Short): old medicine linked a person's mood to the fluids of the body.

Lesson 2 of 5.
serein — calm, serene.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the French for to lie, then the French for mood.

You'll want to know: serein.
serein — "calm, serene".
Après les vacances, je me sens plus serein. After the holidays I feel calmer.
Feminine sereine. It describes a calm mind, not loud or worried.

The word, taken apart.
serein is inherited from Latin serēnus "clear, bright, calm" (Lewis & Short), said first of the sky. Old French also had a noun serein "evening", from Latin sērum (EtymDB).

Lesson 3 of 5.
lunatique — moody.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the French for mood, then the French for calm.

You'll want to know: lunatique.
lunatique — "moody".
Mon frère est lunatique: un jour il rit, un jour il boude. My brother is moody: one day he laughs, the next he sulks.
Not like English lunatic: it means moody, full of ups and downs.

The word, taken apart.
lunatique is a learned borrowing from Late Latin lūnāticus "moonstruck" (Lewis & Short), built on lūna "the moon": moods were thought to follow its phases.

Lesson 4 of 5.
énerver — to annoy, to get on someone's nerves; (s'énerver) to get worked up.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the French for calm, then the French for moody.

You'll want to know: énerver.
énerver — "to annoy, to get on someone's nerves; (s'énerver) to get worked up".
Je m'énerve quand le train est en retard. I get worked up when the train is late.
Often reflexive: s'énerver, past je me suis énervé. Ça m'énerve — that gets on my nerves.

The word, taken apart.
énerver is a learned borrowing from Latin ēnervāre "to cut the sinews, to weaken" (EtymDB), built on nervus "a sinew" (Lewis & Short). French later gave it the sense "to set the nerves on edge".

Lesson 5 of 5.
agacer — to irritate, to get on someone's nerves.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the French for moody, then the French for to annoy.

You'll want to know: agacer.
agacer — "to irritate, to get on someone's nerves".
Le bruit de la rue agace mon voisin. The noise from the street irritates my neighbour.
Spelling: nous agaçons, with ç before o.

The word, taken apart.
agacer goes back to Old French agacier. The origin is not settled: a link with agace, an old word for the magpie, is often suggested.
