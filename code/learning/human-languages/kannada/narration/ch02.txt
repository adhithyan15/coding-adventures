Lesson 1 of 8.
ಹೆಸರು (hesaru) — "name," and the p becomes h shift.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, for letter shapes on the page. You can listen to everything else now — leave the section called The letters in this word until you have stopped, and I will say so again when we reach it.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
"Name" — and a word that shows off a famous Kannada sound-law.

The letters in this word.
(Skim if you read Kannada.) ಹ (ha) + ೆ (the e-sign) becomes ಹೆ (he); ಸ (sa); ರು (ru). Read ಹೆ, ಸ, ರು becomes hesaru.

The word, taken apart.
ಹೆಸರು (hesaru, "name") is native Dravidian, from Proto-Dravidian pesar — and it hides a famous Kannada sound-law: an old initial p became h. So pesar becomes hesar becomes hesaru. That makes it a cousin of Tamil peyar (same root; Tamil kept the p, Kannada softened it to h) — and, like peyar, not related to Sanskrit nāma (Hindi nām, English name).
Across the family: Kannada hesaru, Tamil peyar, Telugu pēru, Malayalam pēr — one Dravidian word; only Hindi nām / English name are Indo-European.

Sounds you'll need.
ಹ (ha) + ೆ (the e-sign) becomes ಹೆ (he); ಸ (sa); ರು (ru). Read ಹೆ, ಸ, ರು becomes hesaru.

The letters in this word.

Lesson 3 of 8.
ನನ್ನ ಹೆಸರು … (nanna hesaru …) — "my name is…," with no "is".

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Assemble the two atoms — and notice, as in Tamil, no word for "is."

The phrase, assembled.
nanna (my) + hesaru (name) + your name.
ನನ್ನ (nanna) ಹೆಸರು (hesaru) … = "my name …" becomes "my name is…"
Nanna hesaru Arun. becomes "My name is Arun."

Grammar Lens: the zero copula.
There is no verb. Kannada sets "my name" beside "Arun" and lets the "is" be understood — the zero copula of the Dravidian family (just like Tamil). Nanna hesaru Arun = literally "my name Arun."

Lesson 4 of 8.
ನೀನು / ನೀವು (nīnu / nīvu) — "you," familiar and respectful.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, for letter shapes on the page. You can listen to everything else now — leave the section called The letters in this word until you have stopped, and I will say so again when we reach it.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
"You" — and Kannada makes respect the same way Tamil and French do.

The letters in this word.
(Skim if you read Kannada.) ನೀ (nī) + ನು (nu) becomes nīnu; ನೀ + ವು (vu) becomes nīvu.

The word, taken apart.
ನೀನು (nīnu, familiar "you") from Proto-Dravidian nīn — the same native root as Tamil nī. ನೀವು (nīvu) is the respectful/plural "you."

Grammar Lens: respect by the plural.
nīnu (familiar) vs. nīvu (respectful, and literally plural) — the same politeness-by-plural as Tamil nīṅgaḷ and French vous: one respected person addressed as "you all." For a first meeting, nīvu.

Sounds you'll need.
ನೀ (n=i) + ನು (nu) becomes n=i nu; ನೀ + ವು (vu) becomes n=i vu.

Lesson 5 of 8.
ಏನು (ēnu) — "what".

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, for letter shapes on the page. You can listen to everything else now — leave the section called The letters in this word until you have stopped, and I will say so again when we reach it.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
The last piece of the question: "what."

The letters in this word.
(Skim if you read Kannada.) ಏ (independent long ē, word-initial) + ನು (nu) becomes ēnu.

The word, taken apart.
ಏನು (ēnu, "what") is native Dravidian, on the interrogative stem yā- / e- — the same Dravidian question-root as Tamil eṉṉa, heading Kannada's question family: ēnu (what), yāru (who), elli (where), yāke (why).

Sounds you'll need.
ಏ (independent long ē, word-initial) + ನು (nu) becomes ēnu.

Lesson 6 of 8.
ನಿಮ್ಮ ಹೆಸರು ಏನು? (nimma hesaru ēnu?) — "what's your name?"

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Assemble the question — your + name + what — still no "is."

The phrase, assembled.
ನಿಮ್ಮ (nimma, "your") = the possessive of nīvu ("you," respectful).
ನಿಮ್ಮ ಹೆಸರು (hesaru) ಏನು (ēnu)? = literally "your name what?" = "what's your name?"
Three words, no verb — the zero copula holds for questions too.

Grammar Lens: answer, and the familiar version.
Answer with the sentence you built: Nanna hesaru Arun. The familiar "your" is ninna (from nīnu): ninna hesaru ēnu?

Lesson 7 of 8.
ಸಂತೋಷ (santōṣa) — "joy," and "pleased to meet you".

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
The warm close — and, true to Kannada, a Sanskrit word where Tamil used a native one.

The phrase.
The full form is ನಿಮ್ಮನ್ನು ಭೇಟಿಯಾಗಿ ಸಂತೋಷ (nimmannu bhēṭiyāgi santōṣa) — "having met you, joy."

The word, taken apart.
ಸಂತೋಷ (santōṣa, "joy, contentment") is Sanskrit — sam- ("fully") + toṣa ("satisfaction") — not native. Where Tamil reaches for its home-grown magiḻcci, Kannada uses a Sanskrit word even for "pleased." The same split you saw over "hello" (namaskāra) and "thanks" (dhanyavāda) runs right through the introductions: Kannada borrows, Tamil keeps its own.

Lesson 8 of 8.
Chapter 2 — the introduction, whole.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Every atom built. Here's the exchange.

The dialogue.
[a table of 4 rows, read aloud]
ನನ್ನ ಹೆಸರು ಮೀರಾ. (nanna hesaru Mira) means My name is Mira.
ನಿಮ್ಮ ಹೆಸರು ಏನು? (nimma hesaru ēnu) means What's your name?
ನನ್ನ ಹೆಸರು ಅರುಣ್. (nanna hesaru Arun) means My name is Arun.
ಸಂತೋಷ. (santōṣa) means Pleased to meet you.
Every atom traced:
hesaru from Dravidian pesar (p becomes h; cousin of Tamil peyar, not name).
nanna from nānu ("I").
ēnu from Dravidian question-stem yā-/e-.
santōṣa from Sanskrit (Kannada borrows even for "pleased"; Tamil keeps native magiḻcci).
The Kannada habit: the zero copula (no "is"). And the family thread — native Dravidian grammar words, a Sanskrit word for "pleased."
