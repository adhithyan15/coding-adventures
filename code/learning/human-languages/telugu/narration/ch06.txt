Lesson 1 of 4.
౦ — the Telugu numeral for 0.

Before we start: this one needs your hands, so it is not a driving lesson. You will want a pen and something to write on and your eyes, for letter shapes on the page. You can listen to everything else now — leave the sections called Script you'll notice: ౦ and Guided Practice until you have stopped, and I will say so again when we reach it.

Warm-up.
[pause 1 second]
Before the new one: ౯ — what amount does it stand for? And one from further back: ౮?
[pause 2 seconds]
One numeral this time, and it is read the way every numeral is read: as an amount.

Script you'll notice: ౦.
౦ (0) is zero, and it closes the set.
It is a ring, and you have seen that ring before: ం, the sunna, is drawn the same way. On the page the difference is where it sits — the sunna rides after a letter inside a word, the zero stands in a row of numerals. Context, not shape, tells you which one you are looking at.
With ౦ (0) in hand the other nine become worth something, because Telugu numerals use place value exactly the way your own digits do. ౧ then ౦ (0) is ten. ౨ then ౦ (0) is twenty. Three digits make hundreds. Nothing new has to be learned for that — the system is one you already own, wearing different characters.

Writing: ౦ — copy what you see.
Put your pen on ౦ (0) and follow its line. Copy the shape you can see — slowly, and larger than it is printed.
This book does not yet tell you where to start the character or which way to travel. That is a real thing, taught with real variation from school to school, and it is not written down here until it can be written down with a source. Copying what is in front of you needs no such source, and it is how the shape gets into your hand in the meantime.

Lesson 2 of 4.
-కు (-ku) — "to, for".

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, because the lesson points at something written down. You can listen to all of it now and come back to the parts that need looking at once you have stopped.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Your first case ending. English puts "to" in front as its own word. Telugu sticks it on the back — and that difference is the biggest structural fact about the language.

You'll want to know: Adding it on.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
word: పేరు pēru — name. + -ku: పేరుకు pēruku. meaning: to/for the name.
word: పని pani — work. + -ku: పనికి paniki. meaning: for work.
Notice the suffix appears as -కు -ku or -కి -ki depending on the vowel before it. Same suffix, adjusted to fit — the seam still shows.
And with "I", which changes its body first:
నేను nēnu ("I") becomes నాకు nāku ("to me").
Nēnu becomes nā- before the ending. Nouns don't do this; they just take the suffix.

Grammar Lens: Why this matters.
[a table of 4 rows, read aloud]
pins down the case? Telugu -ku: yes — dative, always. a Latin ending like -īs: no — -īs is dative or ablative.
carries number? Telugu -ku: no — a separate suffix does. a Latin ending like -īs: yes — plural, baked in.
carries declension class? Telugu -ku: no. a Latin ending like -īs: yes — 1st/2nd only.
can you see the seam? Telugu -ku: yes: pēru + ku. a Latin ending like -īs: no — one fused lump.
Telugu stacks: each suffix means one thing and sits in a fixed order, so you can point at every piece of a long word. Latin fuses — -īs is case and plural and declension, indivisible, and it doesn't even settle which case. Telugu's -ku is never ambiguous.
That's what "agglutinative" means, and it's why Telugu words grow long without growing hard: they are built, not memorised.

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
What does -కు (-ku) mean? ("To" or "for.") Where does it go? (On the end of the noun.) What is "to me"? (నాకు nāku — nēnu shrinks to nā- first.) Why does it sometimes appear as -కి? You already say these, and every one of them has య (ya) somewhere inside it:

Lesson 4 of 4.
నాకు తెలుగు వచ్చు (nāku telugu vaccu) — "I know Telugu".

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Chapter 5 taught you how to say "I speak Telugu." Now say "I know Telugu." Telugu won't let you use nēnu — and the verb it uses instead will surprise you.

You'll want to know: The sentence.
నాకు తెలుగు వచ్చు. nāku telugu vaccu. literally: "to-me Telugu comes."
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
piece: నాకు nāku, "to me" — the dative from the last lesson.
piece: తెలుగు telugu, Telugu (Ch. 5).
piece: వచ్చు vaccu, "comes" — the very verb you learned in Ch. 4.
Two things at once. You are not the subject — nēnu is gone, moved into the dative, and what's left unmarked is telugu. And the verb is literally "to come": a language you know is a thing that comes to you.

Grammar Lens: Why: things that happen to you.
Telugu sorts experiences from actions. Speaking is something you do, so Ch. 5 used nēnu. But knowing, liking, wanting, being hungry — these arrive at you. Telugu marks that by putting the person in the dative.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
English frames it as: I know Telugu. Telugu frames it as: Telugu comes to me.
English frames it as: I like it. Telugu frames it as: it is pleasing to me.
English frames it as: I am cold. Telugu frames it as: cold is to me.
English keeps one fossil of exactly this: "methinks" — me is a dative, "it seems to me." Telugu made it the rule.

The word, taken apart - All four sisters do it.
[a table of 4 rows, read aloud]
language: Telugu. "I know [the language]": nāku telugu vaccu. the dative: -ku.
language: Tamil. "I know [the language]": enakku tamiḻ teriyum. the dative: -ukku.
language: Kannada. "I know [the language]": nanage kannaḍa gottu. the dative: -ge.
language: Malayalam. "I know [the language]": enikku malayāḷam aṟiyām. the dative: -ikku.
One construction across four languages, with four suffixes that are visibly the same suffix — -ku / -ukku / -ge / -ikku. The Dravidian family showing its bones, just as blanc/bianco/branco did for Romance.
