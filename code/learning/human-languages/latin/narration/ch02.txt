Latin, chapter 2: Numbers One to Ten.
2 lessons. Both can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 2.
ūnus, duo, trēs, quattuor, quīnque — one to five.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
You've seen these numbers before — worn down. Here they are new, before a thousand years of erosion set to work.

You'll want to know: The five.
[a table of 5 rows, read aloud]
numeral: I (1). Latin: ūnus. said: OO-nus.
numeral: II (2). Latin: duo. said: DOO-oh.
numeral: III (3). Latin: trēs. said: trayss.
numeral: IV (4). Latin: quattuor. said: KWAT-twor.
numeral: V (5). Latin: quīnque. said: KWEEN-kwe.
The Roman numerals I II III IV V are the tally you already half-know from clock faces — one, two, three strokes, then IV (one before five) and V (five itself). (The qu in quattuor/quīnque is said kw, and every Latin v is a w — so quīnque is KWEEN-kwe.)

The word, taken apart: The root of a whole family.
Latin is the well the Romance tracks keep drawing from. Line these up against their descendants and the erosion shows itself:
[a table of 5 rows, read aloud]
Latin: ūnus. becomes French: un. becomes Spanish: uno.
Latin: duo. becomes French: deux. becomes Spanish: dos.
Latin: trēs. becomes French: trois. becomes Spanish: tres.
Latin: quattuor. becomes French: quatre. becomes Spanish: cuatro.
Latin: quīnque. becomes French: cinq. becomes Spanish: cinco.
(A purist's note: French deux and Spanish dos actually descend from the accusative duōs, not the duo shown — as Romance words generally do.)
Quattuor becomes quatre becomes cuatro is the same wearing-down Hindi shows in catvāri becomes chār — two branches of one ancient word (PIE kʷetwóres), one softening in Europe, one in India. And they seed English too: ūnus in unit and union, duo in duo and dual, trēs in trio and triple, quīnque in quintet.

Grammar Lens: A grammar seed: the first three bend.
Here's something Latin does that its children mostly dropped: one, two and three change their endings to match what they count — ūnus/ūna/ūnum, duo/duae, trēs/tria — while four and up stay fixed. You don't need the full tables yet; just notice that ūnus is really ūn- with a changeable tail, and file away that Latin makes even its numbers agree.

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Count to five in Latin. (Ūnus, duo, trēs, quattuor, quīnque.) Which Latin word is the source of both French quatre and Spanish cuatro? (quattuor.) How is the qu in quīnque pronounced? (kw — KWEEN-kwe.) Which of the first five numbers change their endings to match what they count? (One, two, three — four and up stay fixed.) Next: six to ten, and the numbers hiding in the calendar.


Lesson 2 of 2.
sex, septem, octō, novem, decem — six to ten.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Four of these five are hiding in your calendar right now — with the wrong numbers on them. Here's why.

You'll want to know: The five.
[a table of 5 rows, read aloud]
numeral: VI (6). Latin: sex. said: sex.
numeral: VII (7). Latin: septem. said: SEP-tem.
numeral: VIII (8). Latin: octō. said: OK-toh.
numeral: IX (9). Latin: novem. said: NO-wem.
numeral: X (10). Latin: decem. said: DEK-em.
The numerals climb by adding to five — VI VII VIII (five-and-one, -two, -three) — then IX (one before ten) and X. (Remember v = w, so novem is NO-wem; and c is always hard k, so decem is DEK-em.)

You'll want to know: The numbers frozen in the calendar.
Say the last four out loud, then add the month-name ending -ber to each (where the ending itself comes from is uncertain):
septem + -ber becomes September.
octō + -ber becomes Octōber.
novem + -ber becomes November.
decem + -ber becomes December.
The first literally means "the seventh," yet it sits in ninth place, and the other three are two places off as well. The old Roman year began in March — so month one was March, and the seventh month was the one your calendar now counts ninth. When January and February were later pushed to the front, the names never moved. Rome renumbered the calendar and forgot to rename the months.

The word, taken apart: They seed English too.
sex becomes sextet, sextant (and sex- "six" in sexagesimal, base-60).
octō becomes octave, October — while its Greek twin oktṓ gives octagon and octopus.
decem becomes decimal, and — via duodecim "twelve" — dozen (its Greek cousin dekás "group of ten" adds decade).
All five are cousins of English six to ten (EtymDB 2.1).

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Count six to ten in Latin. (Sex, septem, octō, novem, decem.) Why does the month named for septem, "seven", fall ninth? (Rome's year began in March; two months were later added at the front and the names never changed.) How is novem said, and why? (NO-wem — Latin v is a w.) What everyday base-ten word comes straight from decem? (decimal.) You can now count to ten in the language every Romance number came from.
