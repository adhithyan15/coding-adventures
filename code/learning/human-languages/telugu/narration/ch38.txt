Telugu, chapter 38: The Heart, Two Ways.
2 lessons. You can do the first 1 of them in the car; after that you will want to have stopped.


Lesson 1 of 2.
గుండె (guṇḍe) — "heart," the everyday native word.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Four face words done, one of them an honest gap. This chapter gives you a fifth body word — and then, unusually, a second word for the very same thing.

You'll want to know: గుండె.
గుండె — guṇḍe — heart.
నా గుండె. — nā guṇḍe. — "My heart."

The word, taken apart - native, and matched in Kannada.
గుండె (guṇḍe) is inherited from Proto-Dravidian kuṇṭV, and it is cognate with Kannada's own native heart-word, ಗುಂಡಿಗೆ (guṇḍige) — plainly the same root, wearing a different Telugu and Kannada shape. Guṇḍe is the plain, physical, everyday word: the organ beating in your chest. The next lesson gives Telugu's other word for heart — not a synonym so much as a different register entirely.

Lesson 2 of 2.
హృదయం (hṛdayaṁ) — "heart," and a genuine cousin of the English word.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, for letter shapes on the page. You can listen to everything else now — leave the section called The letters in this word until you have stopped, and I will say so again when we reach it.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Guṇḍe is the heart beating in your chest. This word is the heart Telugu reaches for in a poem, a speech, or a love letter.

You'll want to know: హృదయం.
హృదయం — hṛdayaṁ — heart (literary, emotional register).
నా హృదయం నీది. — nā hṛdayaṁ nīdi. — "My heart is yours."

The letters in this word.
హృ is హ (ha) carrying the vocalic-r vowel sign ృ — the dependent form of the same vowel Chapter 14 gave you as the independent letter ఋ, standing alone at the front of ఋతువు. Same vowel, two shapes: one when it opens a word, another when it rides on a consonant. దయం closes with the familiar అనుస్వార ం.

The word, taken apart - a real cousin of "heart," not just a resemblance.
హృదయం (hṛdayaṁ) is Sanskrit हृदय, built on हृद् (hṛd), "heart." That Sanskrit root and the English word heart are not a coincidence of sound — both descend from the same reconstructed Proto-Indo-European root, ḱērd-. Latin's cor (genitive cordis, the source of English cordial and courage) and Greek's kardía (behind cardiac) come from that exact same root. Four languages, one ancient word.
Telugu keeps hṛdayaṁ beside native guṇḍe the same way Chapter 32 kept Sanskrit bhōjanaṁ beside native tiṇḍi: the plain, physical word stays Dravidian; the word for ceremony, poetry, and feeling is layered on top from Sanskrit.
