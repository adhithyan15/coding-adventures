# चाहिए — wanting a thing rather than an action

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[HI-LEX-CHAHNA-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say the verb for wanting, the one you met with **सकना**. Then try to
use it to want a **cup of tea** rather than to want to *do* something.

## You'll want to know: चाहिए
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[HI-LEX-C95-NEED-01]; assesses=[] -->

**चाहिए** — *is wanted, is needed*.

It is built from the same root as **चाहना**, and it does a job that verb
cannot.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[HI-LEX-C95-NEED-01, HI-LEX-CHAHNA-01] -->

| form | what follows it |
|---|---|
| चाहता / चाहती | another **verb** |
| चाहिए | a **thing** |

**चाहना** wants an action. It was taught alongside the rule that a Hindi verb
can swallow another verb whole, and that is exactly what it does: *I want **to
go***.

**चाहिए** wants a noun. *I want **tea***. English uses one word for both jobs
and hides the difference; Hindi uses two forms and makes it visible.

The other difference is that **चाहिए does not change**. It has no masculine and
feminine, no singular and plural — the same five letters whoever is speaking
and whatever is wanted. After a chapter of endings that agree with everything
in sight, that is a relief worth noticing.

## Grammar Lens: the ए on the end
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[HI-LEX-C95-NEED-01, HI-SCRIPT-RECOG-131] -->

The word ends in **ए**, the standing vowel drawn a few chapters back — not the
sitting mark **◌े**, because there is no consonant after **हि** for the vowel
to ride on.

That is the rule from the letter lesson doing its first real work: **which of
the two shapes a word uses is decided by whether a consonant is there to carry
the vowel**, and a word ending in a vowel sound has nothing behind it.
