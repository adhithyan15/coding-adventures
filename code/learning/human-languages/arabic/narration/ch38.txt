Arabic, chapter 38: Two at Once.
6 lessons. You can do the first 1 of them in the car; after that you will want to have stopped.


Lesson 1 of 6.
و (wa-) — and.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Chapter one taught the reply to the greeting and said of it: hear the little wa- at the front; it means and. That sentence taught nothing. This book has thirty-six chapters of vocabulary and has never joined two words.

You'll want to know: و.
الشاي والحليب — ash-shāy wa-l-ḥalīb — the tea and the milk.
الملح واللحم — al-milḥ wa-l-laḥm — the salt and the meat.
One letter, and it is written joined to the word that follows it, never with a space after it. That is why it looks like part of the next word and why it has been on the page in front of you since chapter one without ever being a word you owned.

The word, taken apart.
و (wa-) is the same conjunction Hebrew and Aramaic write with a single letter, in the same job, and it has held that job for three thousand years. It is by a wide margin the commonest word in written Arabic: Arabic prose strings clauses with و (wa-) where English would use a full stop, so a page of Arabic has far more ands in it than a page of English ever does.
Note that و (wa-) is also the letter wāw, the long ū inside كوب and يوم. One shape, two jobs, told apart by whether it stands alone at the head of a word.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
و (wa-) gives somebody both things. Nothing in this book has ever made anybody choose.
[your turn — recall: the joiner from the lesson before, and where it is written — wa-, joined on]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

You'll want to know: أو.
الشاي أو الحليب — ash-shāy aw al-ḥalīb — the tea or the milk.
Unlike و (wa-), this one stands as its own word with a space on each side. It sits where و (wa-) sits, between the two things, and nothing else in the sentence changes.
Look at the shape: أ — the hamza-carrying alif you know from أكل — followed by و (wa-), the letter that on its own means and. Arabic's two coordinators are one letter apart and mean opposite things.

The word, taken apart.
أو is Semitic ʾaw, the same word Hebrew keeps. It is the whole apparatus a host needs: two nouns, أو between them, and the question particle a later lesson in this book will add. Until this lesson the corpus could name coffee, tea, milk and sugar and could not offer any one of them against another.

Lesson 3 of 6.
لكن (lākin) — but.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, because the lesson points at something written down. You can listen to all of it now and come back to the parts that need looking at once you have stopped.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Two joiners so far. One adds, one chooses, and neither of them can disagree with anything.
[your turn — recall: the two items before this one — wa-, aw]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

You'll want to know: لكن.
أسمع لكن لا أكتب — asmaʿ lākin lā aktub — I hear, but I do not write.
And with the sentence chapter thirty-seven built, whose verb carries a letter the script ladder has not reached: asmaʿ lākin lā afham, I hear, but I do not understand.
الشاي حلو لكن الحليب لا — ash-shāy ḥulw lākin al-ḥalīb lā — the tea is sweet but the milk is not.
و (wa-) puts two clauses on the same side. لكن (lākin) turns the second one against the first, and it stands at the head of the clause it turns.

The word, taken apart.
Look at the first two letters: لا, the negator this book taught in chapter five, with an old particle behind it. Arabic's but has a not inside it.
English did the same thing by a different road. But is Old English be-ūtan, "be out, except" — and out is still visible in it if you look. Two unrelated languages built their adversative out of a word for leaving something aside.
In writing you will also meet لكنّ with a shadda, which takes a following noun in the accusative. The plain لكن (lākin) in this lesson joins clauses and asks nothing of what follows.

Lesson 4 of 6.
حسنًا (ḥasanan) — all right.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
نعم and لا answer a question. Neither of them says I am following you, and a conversation needs that more often than it needs an answer.
[your turn — recall: the joiner from the lesson before, and the negator inside it — lākin]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

You'll want to know: حسنًا.
حسنًا — ḥasanan — all right; fine; very well.
حسنًا، لكن … — ḥasanan, lākin … — all right, but …
The ending is one you have written twice already. شكرًا and ʿafwan wear the same ـًا, the two-stroke tanwīn over an alif that turns an Arabic adjective into an adverb. The root here is حَسَن, ḥasan, "good, beautiful." So ḥasanan is literally goodly.

Grammar Lens: agreeing, and half-agreeing.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
said: نعم. does: answers a question.
said: حسنًا (ḥasanan). does: accepts what was said.
said: حسنًا (ḥasanan)، لكن (lākin) … does: accepts, and then turns.
The third row is the shape almost every real disagreement takes. A learner who can only say نعم or لا has to accept or refuse whole; this is the first thing in the book that lets them do neither.

Lesson 6 of 6.
How are you, joined up — the wellbeing exchange for the second time, now joinable — the joining words of this chapter let the answer say two things at once, or one thing with a reservation.

Warm-up.
[pause 4 seconds]
Ask after a woman and answer as yourself, both parts. Then answer again using one of the joining words this chapter gave you.

The exchange.
[your turn — say: ask a man, then a woman — kayfa ḥāluka? … kayfa ḥāluki?]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: answer with both replies joined — bi-khayr wa-l-ḥamdu lillāh]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: answer with a reservation — bi-khayr, lākin …]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: accept what you hear — ḥasanan]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
For a long stretch this exchange could only be said one way, because there was nothing to join it with. This chapter is the first time the answer can carry two clauses.

Grammar Lens: what the question actually asks.
English asks after you. Arabic asks after your حال — your state.
حال ḥāl is ḥāʾ, alif, lām, with the deep throat ḥ, and its root ḥ–w–l means to turn, to change. So the question is not are you well but how stands the thing about you that shifts.
كيف is the question word, partner to ما, and the phrase bolts three owned pieces together: how + state + your. There is no verb in it at all, exactly as there is none in the name sentence.

How to answer: two replies, both literal.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
reply: بخير bi-khayr. literally: in goodness — بـ (in) plus خير (good).
reply: الحمد لله al-ḥamdu lillāh. literally: the praise is to God — root ḥ–m–d, to praise.
خير was hiding in the morning greeting long before it was named, and al-ḥamdu lillāh comes back more often in real speech than bi-khayr does. Neither is a polite noise: both say something, and both say it with a preposition glued to the front.

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 4 seconds]
Give the root behind حال and its sense. (ḥ–w–l, to turn or change.) Give the literal reading of بخير. (In goodness.) Give the literal reading of الحمد لله. (The praise is to God.) And name the question word that partners كيف. (ما.)
