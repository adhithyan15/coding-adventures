Italian, chapter 272: Would, Could, Should --- The Condizionale, and Six Lines.
4 lessons. You can do the first 3 of them in the car; after that you will want to have stopped.


Lesson 1 of 4.
potrei, vorrei, dovrei — the would of Italian.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say "I would like a coffee". (Vorrei un caffè.) Say "I should study". (Dovrei studiare.)
[pause 1 second]
Two forms of one tense, and you have known one of them since chapter eighty-six. This lesson gives the tense its name and its recipe.

Grammar Lens: the -ei family.
This is the condizionale, Italian's "would". Build it on the whole infinitive, minus its final -e, and add -ei for "I":
parlare, parlerei — I would speak (the -ar- turns to -er-) credere, crederei — I would believe dormire, dormirei — I would sleep.
The other persons change only the ending: parlerei, parleresti, parlerebbe, parleremmo, parlereste, parlerebbero.
A few common verbs squeeze their infinitive first, and these are the three you meet most:
potere, potrei — I could, volere, vorrei — I'd like, dovere, dovrei — I should.
Three jobs, all of them about softening. A polite request: Potrei avere un caffè? — Could I have a coffee? A wish: Vorrei viaggiare. And advice: Al tuo posto, studierei. — In your place, I'd study.

The word, taken apart: "I had to speak".
Latin had no would-tense. Spoken Latin made one by gluing a past of habēre, "to have", onto the infinitive: roughly "I had to speak", which slid into "I would speak". Italian chose the perfect, habuī, "I had", whose Italian descendant is ebbi; worn down on the end of a verb it became -ei, and ebbe, "he had", stayed nearly whole in parlerebbe. French and Spanish glued on a different past of the same verb, so their endings do not match. Neither EtymDB nor Lewis & Short covers a change this late; this is the usual explanation in histories of Romance, given as that. The habēre is the same one that became avere.

Lesson 2 of 4.
sarebbe — it would be.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Build "I would speak". (Parlerei.) Ask politely for a coffee with potere. (Potrei avere un caffè?)

You'll want to know: sarebbe.
sarebbe — "it would be", "he or she would be".
essere does not build its condizionale on esser-. It uses a stem of its own, sar-, and then the endings are the usual ones: sarei, saresti, sarebbe, saremmo, sareste, sarebbero. Sarebbe is the one you will use most, because it judges a situation rather than a person:
Sarebbe difficile. — It would be difficult. Sarebbe più comodo in treno. — It would be more comfortable by train.

Grammar Lens: sarebbe bello.
Sarebbe bello + infinitive — "it would be lovely to":
Sarebbe bello viaggiare insieme. — It would be lovely to travel together.
That is a wish with the person left out, softer than vorrei and less private than spero di. And it sits naturally after a real condition, as the judgement on what follows:
Se piove, sarebbe più comodo prendere l'autobus. — If it rains, it would be more comfortable to take the bus.
The se half stays in the plain present: it may well rain. The sarebbe half is your verdict on the branch.

The word, taken apart.
sarebbe is built on essere, inherited from Latin esse (EtymDB 2.1): the stem sar- is the one essere uses for what has not happened yet, and -ebbe is the worn Latin habuit, "he had", of the last lesson. So sarebbe began as something like "he had to be". Neither source records forms this late, and the history of sar- itself is not settled, so the stem is described here rather than traced.

Lesson 3 of 4.
Review — if this, then that would.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say "it would be difficult". (Sarebbe difficile.) Then "I could". (Potrei.)

What you've built.
Two chapters, and a whole way of talking about what has not happened yet. A real branch: se piove, resto a casa. A branch not yet named: dipende, or dipende da te. And the softened result, in the condizionale: potrei, dovrei, sarebbe.
— Andiamo al mare domani? — Dipende. Se c'è il sole, sarebbe bello. Se piove, potremmo andare al museo.
[pause 2 seconds]
Andiamo is "shall we go", and potremmo is "we could", the noi form of potrei. Both are the forms you already know how to build, worn by a new person.

Lesson 4 of 4.
Six lines about a journey — six connected lines to one reader about a journey — a scene in the imperfetto, events in order, a reason, and a last line that looks forward.

Before we start: this one needs your hands, so it is not a driving lesson. You will want a pen and something to write on and your eyes or your hands for the steps that ask you to read, point, write or gesture. You can listen to everything else now. I will say so again when we reach it, but leave this section until you have stopped: Wrap-up recall.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Chapter two hundred and sixty-two asked for four lines that held together. This one asks for six, and they tell a story.
[pause 1 second]
Pick one person who will read it: un amico, un'amica, someone in your family.

You'll want to know.
[pause 2 seconds]
A told story is not a list of events. It has a scene, in the imperfetto: era, c'era, faceva, ero contento. It has events, in the passato prossimo, put in order with prima, poi and alla fine. It has at least one reason, pointing back with perché or forward with quindi.
[pause 2 seconds]
And a good one ends by looking forward: spero di …, or sarebbe bello …, or a real condition, se …, with the present in the se half.
[pause 2 seconds]
This book's B1 writing paper asks for a connected text of 130 to 170 words. Six lines is the first step towards it, not the whole of it.

Writing — connected composition.
[pause 2 seconds]
On paper, write six lines to your reader about a journey, real or invented, one line for each of these:
the scene: when it was, and the weather;
where you were, who was with you, and how long you stayed, with per;
the first thing that happened, with prima;
the next thing, with poi, and a reason, with perché or quindi;
how it ended and how you felt, with alla fine and ero;
one line forward, with spero di, sarebbe bello or se.
[pause 3 seconds]
Use only words you already write. The whole exercise is choosing the right past for each line, and joining them.

[once you have stopped driving — this part needs your eyes or your hands; if you are driving, skip ahead to the next part]
Wrap-up recall.
[pause 2 seconds]
Now read your lines as you read the journey in chapter two hundred and sixty-four. Mark every verb S for scene or E for event. Lines one and five should be mostly S; three and four mostly E; in line two, a per stretch is E. A line about the weather marked E is the commonest slip: change ha piovuto back to pioveva.
[pause 2 seconds]
Then two spelling checks. A longer word stressed on its last vowel carries the accent there, perché, città, and so do a few short words you write all the time: è, più, sì. And every double consonant is held: aspettato, abbiamo, raccontava.
[pause 2 seconds]
Last, read line six alone. If it has a se, is the verb after it in the plain present? If it has sarebbe, is it your verdict on something that has not happened yet? Then it looks forward, and the story is finished.
