Spanish, chapter 8: Answering, and Handing It Back.
4 lessons. All 4 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 4.
¿cómo está usted? / ¿cómo estás? — choose the register.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
You can already ask a stranger ¿Cómo está usted? Now change only the relationship: ask the same question of a friend.

Grammar Lens: one question, two relationships.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
register: formal (stranger, elder, customer). question: ¿Cómo está usted?
register: informal (friend, peer, child). question: ¿Cómo estás?
The formal version uses the third-person ending está plus usted. The informal version uses second-person estás and normally omits tú. This is the same relationship contrast you drilled with se llama / te llamas.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: to a doctor you just met — "¿Cómo está usted?"]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: to your cousin — "¿Cómo estás?"]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: swap back and forth — "está usted / estás"]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[repeat that twice]
Ask both versions until the ending swap is automatic.

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Which version fits a stranger? (¿Cómo está usted?) Which fits a friend? (¿Cómo estás?) What two things change? (The verb ending and the pronoun.)


Lesson 2 of 4.
regular / más o menos — the honest "so-so".

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
You asked ¿cómo estás? Now you can answer three ways: great, middling, or a wave of the hand. You already have bien ("well"). Today, the middling answers — because nobody is bien every day.

You'll want to know first.
[pause 2 seconds]
Recall bien — "well." Root? (Latin bene, "well" — same as benefit, benevolent.) So estoy bien = "I'm well." Now the answer for when you're not quite bien.

The word, taken apart — two answers.
regular — as an answer to "how are you?", it means "so-so, average, not great." (A famous false-friend trap: it does not mean English "regular / normal / usual" here.) From Latin rēgula, a straight rod a builder used to check a line — the ruler. So regular is "on the rule, on the average line" becomes middling.
That rēgula rod is everywhere in English: rule, ruler, regulate, regular, regiment, and even rail (a straight rod). All the same straight stick.
más o menos — "more or less," the shrug-answer:
más = "more," from Latin magis ("more") — cousin of major, master, maximum.
menos = "less," from Latin minus — the very word English borrowed for minus, minor, minimum, minuscule.
So más o menos is literally "more or less" — the exact phrase, root for root, that English uses for the same shrug.

Grammar Lens: a small answer set.
You now have a scale to answer ¿cómo estás?:
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
(muy) bien means (very) well.
regular means so-so.
más o menos means more or less / meh.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: "regular" — reh-goo-LAR, soft tap on both r's]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: "más o menos" — a little shrug in the voice]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: answer "¿cómo estás?" three ways — bien / regular / más o menos]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
As an answer, does regular mean "normal" or "so-so"? (So-so — the false-friend trap.) What straight-rod Latin word is it from, and what English "rod" words share it? (rēgula becomes rule, ruler, regulate, rail.) Next: put the whole exchange together.


Lesson 3 of 4.
y / ¿y tú? — pass the question back.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
You can ask how someone is and answer. One tiny connector turns that exchange around so the other person gets a turn too.

The word, taken apart.
Y means and and sounds like Spanish i: ee. It continues Latin et, also “and.” English borrowed that Latin word intact inside et cetera, literally “and the rest.”

Grammar Lens: the shortest return question.
Put y before a person you already know:
¿Y tú? — And you? (informal).
¿Y usted? — And you? (formal).
Nothing else needs repeating. The little question hands the previous topic back to the other person.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: “Bien. ¿Y tú?” to a friend]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: “Regular. ¿Y usted?” to a stranger]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: “y” — one clear ee sound]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
What does y mean? (And.) How do you hand a question back to a friend? (¿Y tú?) To a stranger? (¿Y usted?) Which Latin word survives inside English et cetera? (Et, “and.”)


Lesson 4 of 4.
Asking how someone is — putting it together.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Now the question, its answer, and the register choice inside it. Nothing new in this lesson — only what you already have.

The exchange.
— ¿Cómo está usted? — Bien, gracias. ¿Y usted? — Regular.

What you've built.
You can ask how someone is, formally or not, answer honestly, and hand the question back.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: the whole exchange, both parts, without stopping]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: it again, faster, and let it sound like speech rather than recital]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[repeat that twice]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Run it once more from memory. If a word does not come, go back one lesson rather than pushing on — this chapter is short on purpose.
