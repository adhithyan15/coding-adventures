Persian, chapter 290: The Past, Seen Two Ways.
6 lessons. You can do the first 3 of them in the car; after that you will want to have stopped.


Lesson 1 of 6.
میروم (miravam) — the present tense, built from the stem you stored.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Give the pair for "to go": the infinitive, then its present stem. (Raftan, rav-.) Then the present stem of kardan. (Kon-.)
[pause 1 second]
Chapter six asked you to store those stems before there was a tense to run them on. This lesson is the tense.

Grammar Lens: three pieces, in one order.
A Persian present is three pieces: mi-, then the present stem, then an ending for the person.
mi- + rav + -am becomes میروم — miravam — I go, I'm going.
The endings are the six chapter seven named: -am, -i, -ad, -im, -id, -and. So: miravam, I go; miravi, you go; miravad, he or she goes; miravim, we go; miravid, you go, to several people or politely; miravand, they go.
The pronouns are man, to, u for "he" or "she", mâ for "we", shomâ and ânhâ. The ending already says who, so میروم (miravam) is a whole sentence.
Any stored stem works the same way: mikonam, I do; mikhânam, I read; midânam, I know. A compound verb keeps its noun in front, فکر میکنم, fekr mikonam, "I think".
One tense covers "I go", "I'm going" and often "I'll go": فردا میروم — fardâ miravam — I'm going tomorrow.
Printed Persian often sets می off with a narrow break. This book writes it joined to the verb, میروم (miravam), and it is read the same either way.

Grammar Lens: ne-, and the verb that refuses mi-.
"Not" is ne-, in front of everything: نمیروم, nemiravam, I'm not going; نمیدانم, nemidânam, I don't know.
Dâshtan, "to have", takes no mi- in this tense: دارم, dâram, I have, and dust dâram, I like. Its "not" is na-: nadâram, dust nadâram. A few compounds on dâshtan do take it, such as negah dâshtan, "to keep": negah midâram.

The word, taken apart.
Histories of Persian usually trace mi- to an older adverb, hamē, "continually", once a word of its own in front of the verb. EtymDB has no entry for the prefix, so this is the usual account rather than a sourced one.

Lesson 2 of 6.
رفتم (raftam) — the past, and the mark on an object you mean.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say "I'm going", then "I'm not going". (Miravam, nemiravam.)
[pause 1 second]
Now move it to yesterday. The past needs no new stem at all: the infinitive already holds it.

Grammar Lens: the past stem, and the same endings.
Take an infinitive and drop its -an: raftan gives رفت, raft, the past stem chapter six named. Add the endings you know, with one change: "he" or "she" takes no ending at all.
رفتم — raftam, I went, rafti, you went, raft, she went, raftim, we went, raftid, you went, raftand, they went.
Every verb gives up its past stem this way, -tan and -dan alike: budan, budam, I was, and bud, it was; khordan, khordam, I ate; didan, didam, I saw. "Not" here is na-: naraftam, I didn't go.
دیروز به خانه رفتم. — diruz be khâne raftam — Yesterday I went home.
Be is "to". The verb comes last, and the time usually comes first.

Grammar Lens: râ, for the one you mean.
فیلم را دیدم. — film râ didam — I saw the film.
را, râ, follows an object that is a particular one, the film both of you have in mind. Leave it out and the object is any film: film didam, "I watched a film". کتاب را خواندم, ketâb râ khândam: I read the book.

The word, taken apart.
Grammars of Persian usually trace the -t or -d at the end of a past stem to an old participle ending, so raft first meant "gone" and kard, "done". Old Persian kṛta, "done", is usually given as the older shape of kard. That is why the stem stands alone for "she went": raft is "gone" with nobody added.

Lesson 3 of 6.
گذشته (gozashte) — last year, last month, and the past itself.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say "I went", then "we went". (Raftam, raftim.) Then the one word for "last year". (Pârsâl.)
[pause 1 second]
Pârsâl works only for the year. For last month and last week, Persian has one word that fits them all.

You'll want to know: گذشته.
گذشته — gozashte — "last", of a stretch of time, and "the past".
It follows its noun with the ezafe, the same -e as in esm-e man:
سال گذشته به تهران رفتم. — sâl-e gozashte be Tehrân raftam — Last year I went to Tehran.
So mâh-e gozashte is "last month", and hafte-ye gozashte is "last week": after a final vowel the ezafe is said -ye, and in plain print it is often not written at all, هفته گذشته.
Standing alone it is a noun: در گذشته, dar gozashte, "in the past".
Say it in three beats, go-zash-te. Its second letter is one you have not written yet. The next lesson takes it out and puts it in your hand.

The word, taken apart.
gozashte is a past stem with -e on the end, the way Persian makes a participle: گذشت, gozasht, from gozashtan, "to pass", and then -e: "passed", the time that has gone by. Rafte, "gone", is built the same way from raft.
EtymDB takes gozashtan back to a Middle Persian verb, written wtštn' in Pahlavi letters, "to pass, to cross". It records no older parent, so no English cousin is claimed here.

[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

[once you have stopped driving — this part needs your eyes or your hands; if you are driving, skip ahead to the next part]
Script you'll notice: ذ.
ذ — zâl, the sound z.
It is the second letter of گذشته (gozashte), straight after گ. It is د with one dot above, and like د it takes the hand of the letter on its right and offers nothing to the left: after ذ (zâl) the pen lifts, and شته starts fresh.
You now hold three ways to write z: ز, ظ, and ذ (zâl). Persian says them the same. Most words with ذ (zâl) came from Arabic; گذشته (gozashte) is one of a few Persian words that kept it. The usual explanation is that early New Persian softened a d after a vowel towards the th of English this, and scribes wrote that sound with ذ (zâl).

Writing: ذ.
Put your pen on ذ (zâl) and follow its line. Copy the shape you can see — slowly, and larger than it is printed.
This book does not yet tell you where to start this letter or which way to travel. It is not written down here until it can be written down with a source. Copying what is in front of you needs no such source, and it is how the shape gets into your hand in the meantime.

Lesson 5 of 6.
میرفتم (miraftam) — the past that is still running.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say "I'm going", then "I went". (Miravam, raftam.)
[pause 1 second]
Raftam has an edge: the going happened, and it is over. Persian has a second past with no edge on it, and a story needs both.

Grammar Lens: mi- on the past stem.
Put the mi- of the present onto the past stem, and keep the past endings:
mi- + raft + -am becomes میرفتم — miraftam — I was going; I used to go.
So mikhândam, I was reading; mikhordam, I was eating; nemiraftam, I wasn't going. "She" again takes no ending: miraft, she was going.
It answers two English shapes. "Was ‑ing": the action was under way. "Used to": it happened again and again.
هر روز به مدرسه میرفتم. — har ruz be madrese miraftam — Every day I used to go to school.
Budan normally takes no mi- in the past. Bud is already the background: هوا سرد بود, havâ sard bud, it was cold.

Grammar Lens: the scene, and the events.
A told story splits its verbs in two. The mi- past paints the scene: what was going on, what things were like. The plain past drops the events into it: what happened next.
در حالی که کتاب میخواندم، تلفن زنگ زد. — dar hâli ke ketâb mikhândam, telefon zang zad — While I was reading a book, the phone rang.
The reading is scene, still running when the ring cuts into it. The ring is an event, over in a moment. Dar hâli ke, "while", is the joint between them.
هوا سرد بود و خورشید میتابید. — havâ sard bud va khorshid mitâbid — It was cold, and the sun was shining.
Two pieces of scene, and nothing has happened yet. That is how a story opens.

Review — the scene, and what cut into it.

Say "I read" for today, then for yesterday. (Mikhânam, khândam.) Then "I was reading". (Mikhândam.)

What you've built.
Four lessons, and three tenses from two stems. The present stem with mi-: miravam, I go. The past stem alone: raftam, I went, an event with an edge. The past stem with mi-: miraftam, I was going, the scene. And ne- or na- in front for "not".

Gozashte sets the time, and râ marks the object you mean:
