# el abecedario — twenty-seven letters, and one that English does not have

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-ORTH-C424-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] The last chapter gave you the marks that go between the letters. This
chapter is about the letters themselves — which you have been reading for four
hundred chapters and have never been asked to name.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-ORTH-C425-01]; assesses=[] -->

**el abecedario** — "the alphabet."

Look at the name: **a-be-ce-de**. It is the first four letters, read aloud and
glued together. Spanish also says **el alfabeto**, from the Greek *alpha* and
*beta* doing the identical trick in another language.

The Spanish alphabet has **twenty-seven** letters: the twenty-six of English,
plus **ñ**.

## Grammar Lens: the letters, and what each is called
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-ORTH-C425-01] -->

Knowing the shapes is not enough. To spell a name over a telephone you need the
**names**, and most of them are not what an English speaker would guess:

The names are not a list to memorise. A letter is named by **putting a vowel
beside its sound**, and there are only three patterns.

**The five vowels are called by their own sound**, and that is the whole rule
for them: *a, e, i, o, u*.

**Most consonants take an -e after**:

| letter | name |
|---|---|
| b, c, d | be, ce, de |
| g, p, t | ge, pe, te |
| v, y | uve, ye |

**Five take an e- before instead**:

| letter | name |
|---|---|
| f, l | efe, ele |
| m, n | eme, ene |
| s | ese |

**And the leftovers have names of their own**, which are the ones to learn
deliberately:

| letter | name |
|---|---|
| h | hache |
| j | jota |
| k | ka |
| q | cu |
| r | erre |
| w | uve doble |
| x | equis |
| z | zeta |
| ñ | eñe |

Nine irregular names out of twenty-seven letters is a small thing to carry.
