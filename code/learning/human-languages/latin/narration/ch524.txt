Latin, chapter 524: Plague and swelling.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
pestis, pestis — plague, pestilence; ruin.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for health, then the Latin for wholesome.

You'll want to know: pestis, pestis.
pestis, pestis — "plague, pestilence; ruin".
Pestis per urbem it. Plague is going through the city.
Feminine, third declension; accusative pestem. English pest.

The word, taken apart.
pestis is a native Latin noun; Lewis & Short suggests a link with perdō "to destroy", but the origin is not settled. English pest comes from it.

Lesson 2 of 5.
pestilentia, pestilentiae — an epidemic, pestilence.

pestilentia, pestilentiae — "an epidemic, pestilence".
Eō annō pestilentia magna fuit. That year there was a great epidemic.
Feminine, first declension, from pestilēns — plague-bearing. English pestilence.

The word, taken apart.
pestilentia is built on pestilēns "plague-bearing", from pestis "plague" (Lewis & Short; EtymDB). English pestilence comes from it.

Lesson 3 of 5.
contāgiō, contāgiōnis — infection, contagion; touch.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for plague, then the Latin for an epidemic.

You'll want to know: contāgiō, contāgiōnis.
contāgiō, contāgiōnis — "infection, contagion; touch".
Morbus contāgiōne trānsit. The disease passes by contact.
Feminine, third declension, from con- + the root of tangō — to touch. English contagion.

The word, taken apart.
contāgiō is built on con- and the root of tangō "to touch" (Lewis & Short): a touching together. English contagion and contagious come from it.

Lesson 4 of 5.
ulcus, ulceris — a sore, ulcer.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for an epidemic, then the Latin for infection.

You'll want to know: ulcus, ulceris.
ulcus, ulceris — "a sore, ulcer".
Medicus ulcus in crūre lavat. The doctor washes the sore on the leg.
Neuter, third declension. English ulcer.

The word, taken apart.
ulcus is inherited from a Proto-Indo-European word for "wound, sore", and Greek helkos is a cousin (EtymDB; Lewis & Short). English ulcer comes from it.

Lesson 5 of 5.
tumeō, tumēre — to swell, be swollen.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for infection, then the Latin for a sore.

You'll want to know: tumeō, tumēre.
tumeō, tumēre — "to swell, be swollen".
Post cāsum pēs eius tumet. After the fall his foot is swollen.
tumeō, tumēre, tumuī. English tumour comes from its noun tumor.

The word, taken apart.
tumeō is inherited from a Proto-Indo-European root meaning "to swell" (EtymDB); Lewis & Short also compares English thumb. English tumour comes from its noun tumor.
