# hablando — an action caught in the middle

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-HABLAR, ES-GRAMMAR-AR-PRESENT-1SG] -->

[PAUSE 2s] *Hablo* says that I speak. It does not say I am speaking **right
now**, this minute, with my mouth open.

[PAUSE 1s] Spanish has a separate form for that, and it is built from the
infinitive.

## Grammar Lens: swap the ending
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-GRAMMAR-GERUND-AR]; assesses=[ES-LEX-TRABAJAR] -->

> *habl-* **ar** → *habl-* ***ando***

| | |
|---|---|
| *hablar* | ***hablando*** |
| *trabajar* | ***trabajando*** |
| *estudiar* | ***estudiando*** |

[PAUSE 2s] Take the *-ar* off, put *-ando* on. There is nothing to agree with
and nothing to conjugate: **one form, for everybody**. After all the endings
this book has asked you to learn, that is a holiday.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-ETYMON-GERUND-NDUM, ES-GRAMMAR-GERUND-MEANING]; assesses=[] -->

Latin had a form for an action thought of as a **thing being done** — the
*gerund*, ending in *-ndum*. Spanish kept the ending almost untouched and threw
away everything else about it: Latin's gerund had case endings, and Spanish's
has none at all.

[PAUSE 2s] What survives is the meaning: not *to speak*, not *I speak*, but
**speaking** — the action underway, with no beginning and no end attached.

[PAUSE 2s] English built its *-ing* separately and lands in almost the same
place, which is why *hablando* feels immediately familiar. Almost. The two
part company later, and this book will show you where.
