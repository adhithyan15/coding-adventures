## You'll want to know: तिसरा
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[MR-LEX-TISRA]; assesses=[MR-LEX-NUMBERS-ONE-TO-FIVE] -->

> **तिसरा** — *tisrā* — **third**

> **तिसरं घर** — *tisraṁ ghar* — **the third house**

Put the two words you now have side by side against the numbers under them:

| how many | which one in the line |
|---|---|
| **दोन** (*don*) | **दुसरा** (*dusrā*) |
| **तीन** (*tīn*) | **तिसरा** (*tisrā*) |

The number is still visible in both. **दोन** leaves **दु-**, **तीन** leaves
**ति-**, and the same **-सरा** sits on the end of each.

## Grammar Lens: two is not yet a rule
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[MR-LEX-TISRA, MR-GRAMMAR-ORDINAL-SET] -->

It would be pleasant to say **-सरा** is how Marathi makes ordinals. It is not,
and this book will not claim it: **-सरा** appears on these two words and on no
others in the language. What the pair does prove is smaller and true — that the
which-one set is *related* to the how-many set, close enough for the eye to
catch it.

Keep the vowel change in view too. *don* has an **o** and *dusrā* has a **u**;
*tīn* has a long **ī** and *tisrā* a short **i**. The number is visible, not
untouched.
