# unos · unas — the plural of "one"

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-GRAMMAR-INDEFINITE-ARTICLE-MASCULINE, ES-ETYMON-UNUS] -->

[PAUSE 2s] *Un libro*, *una casa*. And you know where they came from: Latin
***unus***, the number **one**.

[PAUSE 1s] Which raises a question. What could a plural of *one* possibly mean?

## Grammar Lens: it stops meaning one
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-GRAMMAR-INDEFINITE-PLURAL]; assesses=[ES-GRAMMAR-NOUN-PLURAL-ES] -->

> ***unos*** *libros* — some books, a few books
> ***unas*** *casas* — some houses

[PAUSE 2s] The moment it goes plural it stops being a number and becomes a
**vague quantity**: a handful, several, a few. Not one, and not counted.

[PAUSE 2s] English does the same trick with *a* and *some* and never notices,
because it changes the word. Spanish keeps the word and changes only the
ending.

## Why it's said this way
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-GRAMMAR-INDEFINITE-PLURAL-AGAINST-BARE]; assesses=[ES-GRAMMAR-BARE-PLURAL-NOUN] -->

Now the useful part, because you already know the alternative.

| | |
|---|---|
| *Compro* ***libros***. | I buy books — books in general |
| *Compro* ***unos*** *libros*. | I buy **some** books — a few, this once |

[PAUSE 2s] The bare plural you learned long ago talks about the **kind** of
thing. *Unos* picks out a small, unspecified number of them.

[PAUSE 2s] So they are not interchangeable, and the difference is one you can
hear in English too — *I buy books* is a habit, *I buy some books* is an
occasion.

## Wrap-up Recall
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-GRAMMAR-INDEFINITE-PLURAL] -->

[PAUSE 2s] What does *unos* mean, given that *un* means one? (**Some** — a few,
uncounted.)

[PAUSE 2s] And what does the **bare** plural mean instead? (The **kind** of
thing, in general.)
