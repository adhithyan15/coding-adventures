Spanish, chapter 426: Capitals, Short Forms And Symbols.
8 lessons. You can do the first 3 of them in the car; after that you will want to have stopped.


Lesson 1 of 8.
las mayúsculas — and the four things Spanish refuses to capitalise.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Write today's day and month in English. Now write them in Spanish, and read on to find out what you almost certainly got wrong.

The word, taken apart.
la mayúscula — "the capital letter." La minúscula is the small one. Latin maiusculus, "somewhat larger," and minusculus, "somewhat smaller" — the two names describe nothing but size.
Spanish capitalises where you expect: the first word of a sentence, and proper names — people, cities, countries, rivers.

Grammar Lens: the four that English capitalises and Spanish does not.
This is the highest-value fact in the chapter, because an English-trained hand gets all four wrong and the marker sees every one:
[a table of 4 rows, read aloud]
days. English: Monday. Spanish: lunes.
months. English: January. Spanish: enero.
languages. English: Spanish. Spanish: español.
nationalities. English: I am Spanish. Spanish: soy español.
Add religions and titles: católico, and el rey, el presidente, doctor — all lower-case in running text.
The idea underneath is consistent. Spanish capitalises a name, not a category. España is a name; español is a description of a kind of person, and a description is an ordinary adjective.

Grammar Lens: the title of a book or a film.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
English, One Hundred Years of Solitude.
Spanish, Cien años de soledad.
Only the first word takes a capital. English capitalises most words in a title; Spanish treats a title as an ordinary sentence with a capital at the front.

[your turn — say: the single rule underneath all four (a name is capitalised, a category is not)]

Wrap-up Recall.

Does Spanish capitalise lunes? (No.) Does it capitalise España? (Yes.) What is the difference? (A name takes a capital; a category does not.)


Lesson 2 of 8.
la minúscula inicial — the capital that depends on what stands beside it.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say the principle from the last lesson: a name takes a capital, a category does not. This lesson gives you a case where the same word takes a capital in one sentence and not in another.

The word, taken apart.
Many Spanish surnames contain a small word: de, del, la, las. Whether it is capitalised depends on what stands in front of it:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
written: with the first name. form: Pedro de la Cruz.
written: without it. form: el señor De la Cruz.
With the forename present, the small word is lower-case. Standing alone at the head of the name, it takes the capital.

Grammar Lens: why the rule has that shape.
It is not arbitrary. De originally meant from — Pedro de la Cruz was Pedro from the Cross, a description of where he came from rather than a name. A description does not take a capital, which is the rule from the last lesson exactly.
Once the forename is gone, De la Cruz has become the whole name, and a name takes a capital.
This appears on forms. A field asking for apellidos alone is the second row; a field with nombre y apellidos together is the first.

[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: the same surname without the forename, and say what changed]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: what de originally meant, and why that explains the rule]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Is it Pedro De la Cruz or Pedro de la Cruz? (Lower-case de, because the forename is there.) And with no forename? (De la Cruz, capital.)


Lesson 3 of 8.
las abreviaturas — the short forms a letter opens with.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
You know that c/ on an address is calle. Say it. That slash was your first abbreviation; this lesson gives you the rest of the set.

The word, taken apart.
una abreviatura — "an abbreviation": a word written short, and always closed with a point.
The forms of address are the ones an A1 paper prints:
[a table of 6 rows, read aloud]
short: Sr. full: señor, Mr.
short: Sra. full: señora, Mrs.
short: Srta. full: señorita, Miss.
short: D. full: don, a courtesy title before a first name.
short: Dña. full: doña, its feminine.
short: Dr. full: doctor, blank.
The point is not decoration. Sr without it is an error, because the point is what marks the word as shortened.

Grammar Lens: don and señor are not the same word.
The distinction is one an English speaker has no equivalent for:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
señor. goes before: the surname. example: el Sr. García.
don. goes before: the first name. example: D. Pedro.
Don Pedro is respectful and warm; Señor García is respectful and formal. Putting them the wrong way round — don García — sounds wrong to a Spanish ear in the way Mr. Peter does to an English one.
Two more an address prints, both of which you can now read: c/ for calle and n.º for número.

Lesson 4 of 8.
las letras voladitas — the little letters that sit above the line.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, because the lesson points at something written down. You can listen to all of it now and come back to the parts that need looking at once you have stopped.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say the abbreviation for señora. Now say what you would write for señorita if you wanted the raised form, and read on.

The word, taken apart.
las letras voladitas — "the flown letters," raised above the line. Spanish uses exactly two of them, º and ª, and which one you use is decided by gender.
Their commonest job is the ordinal indicator:
[a table of 4 rows, read aloud]
written: 1.º. read: primero.
written: 1.ª. read: primera.
written: 2.º. read: segundo.
written: n.º. read: número.
English writes 1st and 2nd, changing the ending with the number. Spanish writes the same raised letter every time and changes it only for gender: º for a masculine word, ª for a feminine one.

Grammar Lens: the point goes before the raised letter.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
correct: 1.º. wrong: 1º.
correct: Sr.ª. wrong: Srª.
The Academy's recommendation puts the point first and the raised letter after, because the point is the abbreviation mark and the raised letter is the ending. You will see the pointless form printed everywhere; the one with the point is the one to write.
A warning about the shape. º is a raised letter o, not a degree sign, even though the two look nearly identical. A temperature uses the degree sign; an ordinal uses the letter.

Lesson 5 of 8.
las siglas — no points, and a plural made by doubling.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say the rule you learned for abbreviations: every one ends in a point. This lesson gives you a kind of short form where that rule is reversed.

The word, taken apart.
una sigla — a set of initials standing for a name. ONU, DNI, RENFE.
Unlike an abbreviation, a sigla takes no points at all:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
correct: DNI. wrong: D.N.I.
correct: ONU. wrong: O.N.U.
The two you will actually meet on a form are DNI — Documento Nacional de Identidad, the Spanish identity card — and UE, the European Union.

Grammar Lens: the plural, which is the strange part.
A sigla does not take an -s in writing. It is made plural by doubling its letters, with a space in the middle:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
singular: EU. plural: EE. UU. meaning: the United States.
singular: CC. plural: CC. OO. meaning: a trade union confederation.
EE. UU. is Estados Unidos — two words, so two doubled letters. Said aloud it is the full name; the doubling exists only on the page.
One more distinction the names record. A sigla is spelled out letter by letter (DNI is de-ene-i). An acrónimo is said as a word (RENFE, ONU). Which one a set of initials becomes depends on nothing but whether the result is pronounceable.

Lesson 6 of 8.
los símbolos — the third kind of short form, and the one with no point.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
An abbreviation ends in a point and a sigla does not. Say both rules. This lesson adds a third kind, and it sides with the sigla.

The word, taken apart.
un símbolo — a fixed sign for a unit, a currency or a direction. It is international, unchanging, and takes no point.
[a table of 4 rows, read aloud]
€ means the euro.
km means kilometre.
kg means kilogram.
h means hour.
km is never km. and never kms. A symbol has no plural and no point, because it is not a shortened Spanish word — it is the same sign in every language.
You have already read one in use: km/h, which you learned to say aloud as kilómetros por hora.

Grammar Lens: the compass, and where the euro goes.
The four points of the compass have symbols that match their Spanish names, and one does not match the English:
[a table of 4 rows, read aloud]
symbol: N. Spanish: norte. English: north.
symbol: S. Spanish: sur. English: south.
symbol: E. Spanish: este. English: east.
symbol: O. Spanish: oeste. English: west (W).
A Spanish map prints O where an English map prints W. On a reading paper that is a trap worth having seen once.
And the currency: Spanish writes the amount first and the symbol after, with a space — 20 €, not €20. English does the reverse.

Lesson 7 of 8.
tres formas cortas — and the point tells you which is which.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, because the lesson points at something written down. You can listen to all of it now and come back to the parts that need looking at once you have stopped.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Name the three kinds of short form before reading on, and say which one takes a point.

Grammar Lens: one test separates all three.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
kind: abreviatura. example: Sr., Dña., n.º. point?: yes.
kind: sigla. example: DNI, ONU, UE. point?: no.
kind: símbolo. example: €, km, N. point?: no.
The reason is worth more than the table. An abreviatura is a Spanish word cut short, and the point marks the cut. A sigla is not a cut word but a set of initials. A símbolo is not Spanish at all — it is an international sign that happens to appear in a Spanish sentence.
Only something that was cut needs a mark showing where.

Grammar Lens: the capitals, gathered.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
lower-case in Spanish: lunes, enero. capitalised: España, Madrid.
lower-case in Spanish: español, católico. capitalised: Pedro, García.
lower-case in Spanish: el rey, el doctor. capitalised: De la Cruz, with no forename.
One idea covers the whole left column: a category is not a name. Days, months, languages, nationalities, religions and titles all describe a kind of thing rather than naming a particular one.
And the raised letters: º for a masculine word, ª for a feminine one, with the point before — 1.º, 1.ª, Sr.ª.

Lesson 8 of 8.
un formulario completo — where all of it actually turns up.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, because the lesson points at something written down. You can listen to all of it now and come back to the parts that need looking at once you have stopped.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say what DNI stands for, and say whether it takes points.

Reading.
Read this form. Every short form in it has been taught:
Datos personales Nombre y apellidos: Pedro de la Cruz DNI: 12345678-A Dirección: c/ Mayor, n.º 5, 1.º Teléfono: 600 123 456 Correo electrónico: Nacionalidad: español.
Six things to notice, and every one is a rule rather than a detail: de la Cruz is lower-case because the forename is present; DNI carries no points; c/ is the street and n.º the number; 1.º is the floor, with the point before the raised letter; and español is lower-case because a nationality is a category.

Grammar Lens: the same data spoken aloud.
A form prints the short forms; a person says them in full:
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
on the form: c/ Mayor. said aloud: calle Mayor.
on the form: n.º 5. said aloud: número cinco.
on the form: 1.º. said aloud: primero.
A reading paper gives you the left column and a listening paper the right, which is why both have to be learned.
