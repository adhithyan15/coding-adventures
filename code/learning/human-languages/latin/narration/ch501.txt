Latin, chapter 501: Moods and feelings.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
commoveō, commovēre — to move deeply, disturb.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to envy, then the Latin for a rival.

You'll want to know: commoveō, commovēre.
commoveō, commovēre — "to move deeply, disturb".
Lacrimae fīliae patrem valdē commovent. His daughter's tears move the father deeply.
commoveō, commovēre, commōvī, commōtum. Of the body, to shake; of the mind, to stir or upset. English commotion.

The word, taken apart.
commoveō is built on con- and moveō "to move" (Lewis & Short; EtymDB). English commotion comes from the related noun commōtiō.

Lesson 2 of 5.
afficiō, afficere — to affect, fill with a feeling.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a rival, then the Latin for to move deeply.

You'll want to know: afficiō, afficere.
afficiō, afficere — "to affect, fill with a feeling".
Nūntius malus omnēs dolōre afficit. The bad news fills everyone with grief.
afficiō, afficere, affēcī, affectum. Aliquem laetitiā afficere — to fill someone with joy; in the passive, dolōre afficior — I am stricken with pain. English affect.

The word, taken apart.
afficiō is built on ad- and faciō "to do, make" (Lewis & Short): to do something to someone. English affect comes from it.

Lesson 3 of 5.
affectus, affectūs — a mood, state of mind.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to move deeply, then the Latin for to affect.

You'll want to know: affectus, affectūs.
affectus, affectūs — "a mood, state of mind".
Affectūs animī in vōce audīmus. We hear the moods of the mind in the voice.
Masculine, fourth declension, from afficiō. English affection comes from the related affectiō.

The word, taken apart.
affectus is the action noun of afficiō "to affect" (EtymDB; Lewis & Short): the state into which something puts the mind.

Lesson 4 of 5.

You'll want to know: tranquillus, tranquilla, tranquillum.
tranquillus, tranquilla, tranquillum — "calm, peaceful".
Post tempestātem mare tranquillum est. After the storm the sea is calm.
Of the weather and the sea, and of a quiet mind. English tranquil.

The word, taken apart.
tranquillus goes back to Proto-Italic (EtymDB), but its further origin is not settled. English tranquil comes from it.

Lesson 5 of 5.
sēcūrus, sēcūra, sēcūrum — free from care, untroubled.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a mood, then the Latin for calm.

You'll want to know: sēcūrus, sēcūra, sēcūrum.
sēcūrus, sēcūra, sēcūrum — "free from care, untroubled".
Post labōrem agricola sēcūrus dormit. After his work the farmer sleeps free from care.
sē- 'without' + cūra 'care'. In classical Latin it means carefree rather than safe. English secure.

The word, taken apart.
sēcūrus is built on sē- "without" and cūra "care" (Lewis & Short; EtymDB). English secure and sure come from it.
