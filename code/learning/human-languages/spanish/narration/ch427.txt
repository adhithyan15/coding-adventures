Spanish, chapter 427: People Named By What They Do.
8 lessons. You can do the first 6 of them in the car; after that you will want to have stopped.


Lesson 1 of 8.
el vendedor — the ending you already have, doing its real job.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say vender. Say trabajador. You have the verb and you have the ending. Put them together before reading on.

The word, taken apart.
el vendedor — "the seller, the shop assistant."
Take vender, drop the -er, add -dor. That is the whole operation, and you met the ending as an adjective in the character chapter. Here it does what it was built for: making a person out of an action.
[a table of 4 rows, read aloud]
verb: trabajar. the one who does it: el trabajador.
verb: vender. the one who does it: el vendedor.
verb: jugar. the one who does it: el jugador.
verb: ganar. the one who does it: el ganador.
The ending is productive, which is the property worth having. You are not learning a word; you are learning a machine that turns any verb you know into the person who does it.

Grammar Lens: where the ending puts the stress.
Every -dor noun stresses its last syllable: vende-DOR, trabaja-DOR, juga-DOR. None of them carries a written accent, because a word ending in -r is stressed on the last syllable by default and the accent marks only departures from the default.
The feminine adds -a and nothing else: la vendedora, la trabajadora.

Lesson 2 of 8.
el comprador — the other side of the counter.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say el vendedor. Say comprar. Build the person who does the second thing, using the machine from the first.

The word, taken apart.
el comprador — "the buyer."
Comprar minus -ar, plus -dor. No new rule, which is the point of teaching the pair together: one ending buys both halves of a transaction.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
side: sells. verb: vender. person: el vendedor.
side: buys. verb: comprar. person: el comprador.

Grammar Lens: the pair that names an economy.
These two words are how a form, a contract or a news item names the parties to a sale. They are not shop vocabulary — el vendedor in a shop is the assistant, but vendedor and comprador together are the roles, the way English says seller and buyer rather than shopkeeper and customer.
That distinction is why the source lists them under commerce rather than under shopping, and why knowing comprar was not enough to cover it.

Lesson 3 of 8.
el policía — where the article changes the meaning.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say el comprador and la compradora, where the article follows the person. The word in this lesson does something different, and it is worth noticing.

The word, taken apart.
el policía — "the police officer." Greek pólis, "the city," through Latin politia, "the administration of a state."
The root is unusually productive, and every word on it is about the city:
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
word: política. the city in it: the running of the city.
word: metrópolis. the city in it: the mother-city.
word: policía. the city in it: the keeping of the city's order.
So a police officer is named after the city, not after policing. The institution came first and the person was named from it.

Grammar Lens: the article does the distinguishing.
This is the fact a reading paper can test, and it catches people:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
el policía means a police officer, a man.
la policía means the police force — or a woman officer.
The word does not change. The article carries the whole difference, and la policía is ambiguous between the force and a female officer in a way that only context settles.
Compare it with the -dor pair from the last two lessons, where the ending moved and the article merely followed. Here the ending is fixed at -ía and the article is doing all the work.

Lesson 4 of 8.
el bombero — named after the pump, not the fire.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say el policía. The other emergency service has a name built a different way again — and it is named after a piece of equipment.

The word, taken apart.
el bombero — "the firefighter."
From la bomba, which in Spanish means a pump as well as a bomb. A bombero is the person who works the pump.
English names the same person after what they fight: fire-fighter, fire-man. Spanish names them after what they carry. Two languages looking at the same person and choosing opposite halves of the scene.
The -ero ending is the third person-making ending in this chapter, and it attaches to things rather than to verbs:
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
thing: la bomba — the pump. the person who works with it: el bombero.
thing: el pan — bread. the person who works with it: el panadero.
thing: la carta — the letter. the person who works with it: el cartero.
So Spanish has -dor for the one who does the action and -ero for the one who works with the thing.

Grammar Lens: three endings, three logics.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
ending: -dor. built from: a verb. example: vendedor.
ending: -ero. built from: a thing. example: bombero.
ending: -ía. built from: an institution. example: policía.
None of the three is guessable from the meaning; all three are readable once you know which is which. That is the honest position: you cannot predict which ending a profession takes, but you can take any profession apart.

Lesson 5 of 8.
el abogado — somebody called to your side.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say el bombero, named after his pump. The next profession is named after something nobody carries: a summons.

The word, taken apart.
el abogado — "the lawyer."
Latin advocatus: ad- to + vocatus, "called." Literally one called to your side — in a Roman court, the friend you summoned to speak for you.
English borrowed the same word twice and kept both:
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
word: advocate. the calling in it: one called to your side.
word: vocation. the calling in it: a calling, in the older sense.
word: convoke. the calling in it: to call together.
Vocare, "to call," is the root under all of them, and it is the same root as voz, the voice.
Spanish wore advocatus down to abogado while English kept the Latin shape intact, which is the ordinary difference between a word that was inherited and a word that was borrowed — the same distinction actor and trabajador demonstrated.

Grammar Lens: the profession without a visible ending.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
profession: vendedor. how the word was made: Spanish added -dor to a Spanish verb.
profession: bombero. how the word was made: Spanish added -ero to a Spanish noun.
profession: abogado. how the word was made: Latin made it, and Spanish inherited it whole.
Abogado has no ending you can remove to find a Spanish verb, because it was never built in Spanish. It arrived finished.
The feminine is the ordinary swap: la abogada.

Lesson 6 of 8.
la empresa — a thing taken on.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say el vendedor and el comprador. You can name both parties to a sale. Say where the seller works, and notice you have no word for it.

The word, taken apart.
la empresa — "the company, the firm."
From Latin in- + prehendere, "to take hold of." An empresa is literally a thing taken on — an undertaking.
English took the same idea through French and got enterprise, from entre-.
prendre, the same two pieces in the other Romance language. The words are cousins built to the same plan.
The difference is what each language did with it afterwards. English enterprise mostly means the venture; Spanish empresa means both the venture and the firm that carries it out. For an A1 form asking where you work, empresa is the everyday word — it is what English calls the company.
Prehendere is generous, and two of its descendants are already useful to you: aprender, to learn, is to take hold of something; and sorprender, to surprise, is to take somebody from above.

Grammar Lens: a workplace rather than a worker.
The chapter has named five people by what they do. La empresa is the one word in it that names a place and an organisation instead:
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
word: el vendedor. what it names: a person.
word: el abogado. what it names: a person.
word: la empresa. what it names: the organisation they work for.
That is why a form has two separate lines, profesión and empresa, and why answering one does not answer the other.

Lesson 7 of 8.
cinco personas y un lugar — sorted by how each name was built.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, because the lesson points at something written down. You can listen to all of it now and come back to the parts that need looking at once you have stopped.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Name as many of the six as you can before reading the table.

Grammar Lens: four ways to name a person.
[a table of 5 rows, read aloud]
word: el vendedor. built from: the verb vender, plus -dor.
word: el comprador. built from: the verb comprar, plus -dor.
word: el bombero. built from: the noun bomba, plus -ero.
word: el policía. built from: the institution, la policía.
word: el abogado. built from: nothing in Spanish — inherited whole from Latin.
Four different methods for five words, which is the honest summary: Spanish does not have one way of naming a profession. What it has is a small set of methods, and knowing them lets you take a profession apart even when you could not have predicted it.
The -dor pair is the only one that is productive for you: give it any verb you know and it gives you back the person — which is how trabajar gave trabajador in the character chapter, before you had any use for the ending.

Grammar Lens: which ones change, and how.
[a table of 4 rows, read aloud]
masculine: el vendedor. feminine: la vendedora.
masculine: el bombero. feminine: la bombera.
masculine: el abogado. feminine: la abogada.
masculine: el policía. feminine: la policía — and the word also means the force.
Three behave ordinarily. Policía is the exception worth remembering, because the article is carrying a distinction no ending marks.
And la empresa is not a person at all — it is where several of these people work, which is why a form asks for it on its own line.

Lesson 8 of 8.
¿A qué se dedica? — the question about work.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say el abogado and la empresa. One answers what somebody does and one answers where. A conversation about work needs both.

Grammar Lens: naming a profession takes no article.
This is the one grammatical trap in the answer, and English speakers fall into it every time:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
correct, Soy abogado.
wrong, Soy un abogado.
English says I am a lawyer. Spanish drops the article when the sentence states nothing but the profession, because the bare noun is working as a description rather than picking out one individual.
The article comes back as soon as you add anything to it: Soy un abogado trabajador — "I am a hard-working lawyer." Adding the adjective turns the description into a particular kind of thing, and a particular thing takes an article.

Reading.
Read aloud. Every word has been taught:
— ¿A qué se dedica? — Soy abogado. Trabajo en una empresa grande. — ¿Y su hermana? — Es policía. Mi hermano es bombero.
Three professions, no article on any of them, and una empresa taking one because it is a thing rather than a description.
