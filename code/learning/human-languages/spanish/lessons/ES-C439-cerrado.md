# cerrado — the word on the glass

## You'll want to know: cerrado
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-C439-VIA-03]; assesses=[ES-LEX-CERRAR-04, ES-LEX-PUERTA] -->

**cerrado** — closed.

*CERRADO POR VACACIONES* — closed for holidays. *La piscina está cerrada los
lunes* — the pool is closed on Mondays. It agrees like any adjective:
*cerrado*, *cerrada*, *cerrados*, *cerradas*, matching the thing that is shut.

It also stretches past doors. *Una curva cerrada* is a tight bend; *un acento
cerrado* is a thick accent you struggle to follow. The common thread is **not
letting anything through**.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C439-VIA-03, ES-ETYMON-CLAUDERE-06] -->

**cerrar** plus **-ado**, the ending that turns an *-ar* verb into its past
participle. Nothing exotic: *hablar* gives *hablado*, *cerrar* gives *cerrado*.

Underneath sits Latin **claudere**, to shut, which you met under *cerrar*. It is
still visible in **la clausura**, **excluir** and English **closure**, **clause**
and **claustrophobia** — a fear named for a shut room.

## Grammar Lens: two participles, two shapes
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C439-VIA-03, ES-GRAMMAR-ABIERTO-03] -->

**abrir** gave you **abierto**, which does not follow the pattern. **cerrar**
gives **cerrado**, which does. The pair sits on opposite sides of the same shop
door, and their shapes do not match.

| verb | participle |
|---|---|
| cerrar | cerrado — regular |
| abrir | **abierto** — irregular |

The regular ones you build; the irregular ones you learn one at a time, and
there are only about a dozen that matter. Spanish put the irregularity on the
oldest and most-used verbs, which is the usual bargain a language makes: the
words you say constantly are the ones that resist tidying.
