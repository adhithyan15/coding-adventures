Spanish, chapter 459: El inventario.
5 lessons. You can do the first 3 of them in the car; after that you will want to have stopped.


Lesson 1 of 5.
la sección — a piece cut off.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say cortar and el almacén. A big store has been cut into parts, and each one has a name.

You'll want to know: la sección.
la sección — the section, the department.
La sección de deportes — the sports department. ¿En qué sección está? — which part of the shop is it in? La sección de lácteos — the dairy aisle.
Beyond shops: a section of a form, of a newspaper, of a report. Anywhere a whole has been divided and the parts labelled.
Plural secciones, and the accent comes off, as it does for every -ción.

Grammar Lens: sección, parte, departamento.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
what it names: any piece of a whole. the word: la parte.
what it names: a piece somebody divided off on purpose. the word: la sección.
what it names: a unit of an organisation. the word: el departamento.
Una parte del libro could be any stretch of it. Una sección del libro is one somebody marked out and probably numbered. And a departamento has staff.
The middle one is the useful new distinction: sección always implies a dividing hand, which is exactly what its root says.

The word, taken apart.
Latin secare, to cut — the participle sectus gave sectio, a cutting.
So a sección is a cut piece, and the family is full of cutting: el insecto is an animal cut into segments, which is what you can see on an ant.
A word to keep out of the family, because it looks as though it belongs: la sierra is a saw, and also a mountain range, since a ridge of peaks looks like a saw's teeth. That picture is right, but the word is Latin serra and has nothing to do with secare. Two words about cutting, unrelated.
English has section, sector, dissect, segment and insect off the same root — and insect is the one worth remembering, because nobody expects a bug to be named after a cut.
Cortar, which you already have, comes from a different Latin word — curtus, shortened — so the two are not relatives, and Spanish kept both: the everyday cutting and the bookish dividing.

Lesson 2 of 5.
el inventario — what we found.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say venir and el almacén. A very old verb is about to turn up in a very dull word.

You'll want to know: el inventario.
el inventario — the inventory, and the stocktake itself.
Cerrado por inventario.
Hacer inventario — to do a stocktake. Está en el inventario — it's on the list.
That sign goes up on a shop door for a morning once or twice a year, and it is the reason this word is worth having before you need it.

Grammar Lens: cerrado por — the cause on a door.
You have met por plus a bare noun giving a cause on a notice. Shop doors are where it lives:
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
sign: cerrado por inventario. why they are shut: stocktaking.
sign: cerrado por obras. why they are shut: building work.
sign: cerrado por vacaciones. why they are shut: holidays.
No article, no verb, no explanation. The frame is cerrado por + the reason, and a Spanish reader takes the whole thing in at a glance from across a street — which is what a sign is for.

The word, taken apart.
Latin invenire: in- plus venire, to come — so to come upon, and from there to find.
An inventario is therefore a list of what was found, which is exactly what a stocktake produces: somebody walks the shelves and writes down what is actually there, as opposed to what the records claim.
The same invenire gave inventar, to invent — and that is the same idea seen from the other side. To invent something is to come upon it. Spanish and English both treat discovery and invention as one act, and the word for a warehouse list is the proof.
Venir is underneath all of it, and you have had it since the early lessons.

Lesson 3 of 5.
habitual — the way it usually is.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say he hablado and siempre. The he in the first is about to turn up in an adjective.

You'll want to know: habitual.
habitual — usual, regular, customary.
El horario habitual — the usual hours. Nuestro proveedor habitual — our regular supplier. Lo habitual es avisar — the usual thing is to let people know.
Habitualmente is the adverb, and el cliente habitual is a regular. The opposite is poco habitual — unusual — rather than any single word.

Grammar Lens: habitual, normal, siempre.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
what you mean: this is the pattern, by habit. the word: habitual.
what you mean: this is ordinary, not strange. the word: normal.
what you mean: without exception. the word: siempre.
El horario habitual is the one they usually keep — and this week they may not. Es normal means nobody should be surprised. Siempre abren a las nueve allows no exceptions at all.
So habitual sits between the other two: stronger than "not strange", weaker than "always". A notice using it is telling you what to expect while leaving itself room.

The word, taken apart.
Latin habēre, to have, to hold — by way of habitus, "the way one holds oneself".
You own that verb already: habēre is the haber of he hablado, worn down by centuries of use into an auxiliary so small it barely looks like a word. Set the two side by side and the two roads are visible again:
[a table of 1 row, read aloud]
worn down by speech: he hablado. lifted from the page: habitual.
A habit is something you hold, which is why English says you have a habit rather than doing one. And el hábito in Spanish is both a habit and a monk's robe — the clothing you hold yourself in, from the same habitus.
The h is silent, as it is anywhere in Spanish that it stands on its own.

Lesson 4 of 5.
(la sección, el inventario, habitual) — from cold.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, because the lesson points at something written down. You can listen to all of it now and come back to the parts that need looking at once you have stopped.

Warm-up.
[pause 3 seconds]
Close the lessons before this one. Say all three: the section, the stocktake, usual.

Grammar Lens: two everyday verbs, hidden in office words.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
office word: el inventario. the everyday verb inside it: venir — in-venire, to come upon.
office word: habitual. the everyday verb inside it: haber — habēre, to hold.
Both verbs are among the first things anybody learns in Spanish, and both are so worn down that nothing on the surface connects them to these words. An inventory is a list of what was found; something habitual is a thing held on to.
This is what happens when a language takes the same Latin word twice. The spoken copy shrinks until it is almost nothing — he, vengo — while the written copy keeps its whole Latin shape.

Grammar Lens: the notice frames, collected.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
frame: cerrado por + bare noun. example: cerrado por inventario.
frame: fuera de + bare noun. example: fuera de plazo.
frame: por + bare noun, as a cause. example: por obras.
No article after any of them. Spanish signs run on these three shapes, and once you can see the frame you can read a notice you have never met by filling in one word.

Lesson 5 of 5.
(cerrado por inventario) — read it, then do it yourself.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say el inventario and habitual. The door below uses one to excuse breaking the other.

Guided Practice.
A sheet taped to a shop door. Read it, then answer.
CERRADO POR INVENTARIO.
Solo esta mañana. Abrimos a las 16:00.
El horario habitual se recupera mañana.
La sección de deportes permanece abierta por la puerta lateral.
[pause 3 seconds]
[your turn — say: how long the shop is shut, and when it opens]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: what is open anyway, and how to get in]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: when things go back to normal]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Why it's said this way.
El horario habitual se recupera mañana is the line worth taking apart. Habitual rather than normal, because the shop is saying this is the pattern we keep, not this is not strange. And se recupera rather than recuperamos: the timetable comes back by itself, nobody is thanked for it.
Permanece abierta agrees with la sección, feminine — a small check that catches people out, because deportes is masculine and plural and sits right next to the verb. Spanish agrees with the head noun, not with the nearest one.
And the heading is the frame you now know: cerrado por plus a bare noun. A reader takes it in from the pavement without reading the rest.

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Now your turn, out loud and then in writing. Four lines for a shop door: say you are closed for stocktaking, say when you reopen, say the usual hours come back tomorrow, and say which section stays open.
Then check yourself: did you use the cerrado por frame with no article, and did the adjective agree with la sección rather than with deportes?
