# jubilado — the participle that became a person

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C431-PEDIDO-01, ES-LEX-TRABAJO] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say *el pedido*. Say *el trabajo*. You learned that a participle can
harden into a noun. Here it hardens into a **person**.

## You'll want to know: jubilado
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-C435-PAPEL-05]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C431-PEDIDO-01] -->

**jubilado / jubilada** — retired, and also *a retired person*.

**jubilarse** — to retire. *Se jubila en marzo* — she retires in March.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C435-PAPEL-05, ES-LEX-C431-PEDIDO-01] -->

Same move as **el pedido**, one chapter apart:

| verb | participle | used as |
|---|---|---|
| pedir | pedido | a thing |
| jubilar | jubilado | a person |

A Spanish participle can stand as an adjective *or* a noun with no further
change, which is why *un jubilado* needs no word for *person* in it.

**And the root is not what you would expect.** Latin *iubilare* meant to
**shout for joy** — it is the same word under *jubilee* and *jubilant*. Retiring
in Spanish is, etymologically, letting out a cheer.

Whether anybody feels that on the day is another matter, but the word was built
by people who thought the end of work was worth shouting about.
