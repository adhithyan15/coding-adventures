# দুঃখিত (dukkhito) — "sorry," and a prefix that reaches Greek

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[BN-LEX-C13-DOYAKORE-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Last lesson's kindness gets a request said politely. This lesson
is what you say when the kindness came too late.

## You'll want to know: দুঃখিত
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[BN-LEX-C13-DUKKHITO-01]; assesses=[] -->

> **দুঃখিত** — *dukkhito* — **sorry**

আমি দুঃখিত (*āmi dukkhito*) — I'm sorry.

## The letters in this word
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[] -->

**দুঃখিত** is **দ** (*dô*) with the vowel sign **ু** (*u*), then **ঃ** — a new
mark, the **visarga**, two small stacked dots that add a light breath after
the vowel — then **খ** (*kh*) with the vowel sign **ি** (*i*), then **ত**
(*t*, silent at the end). Spelled *duḥkhita*, said *dukkhito*: the breath
mark softens toward the consonant that follows it rather than staying a
separate sound.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[] -->

**দুঃখিত** is built on Sanskrit **দুঃখ** (*duḥkha*), "sorrow, suffering" —
the word Buddhism made famous as *dukkha*. Take it apart and one half is
certain: **দুঃ/দুস্** (*dus-*) means "bad, difficult," from PIE **\*dus-**,
the very prefix inside Greek **dys-**: **dystopia**, **dysfunctional**. The
other half, **খ** (*kha*), is where the certainty ends. Sanskrit's own
grammatical tradition explains it as "an axle-hole" — a *duḥkha* journey is a
**bad axle-hole**, a rough ride, set against **সুখ** (*sukha*), "a good
axle-hole," comfort. It is a vivid story, and it is repeated everywhere. It
is also, by the account of modern Sanskritists including Mayrhofer, not a
story to trust: the axle-hole reading may be a later folk explanation
grafted onto a word whose real root is lost. দুঃ's cousin is real; খ's is a
story, reported and not settled.
