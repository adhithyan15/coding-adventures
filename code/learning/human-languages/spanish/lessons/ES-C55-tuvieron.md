# tuvieron

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-GRAMMAR-STRONG-PRETERITE-SINGULAR-PATTERN, ES-GRAMMAR-TENER-PRETERITE-SINGULAR] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say the three strong pasts you know. (*Tuve, hice, estuve*.) Where
does the stress fall? (On the **stem** — *TU-ve*, *HI-ce*.)

[PAUSE 1s] That is what makes them strong. Now watch what the plural does to it.

## Grammar Lens: the stem gives the stress back
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-GRAMMAR-STRONG-PRETERITE-PLURAL]; assesses=[ES-GRAMMAR-ER-IR-PRETERITE-PLURAL] -->

> **Strong stem + *-ieron*.**

| singular | plural |
|---|---|
| *tuve* | **tuvieron** |
| *hice* | **hicieron** |
| *estuve* | **estuvieron** |

The ending is the same *-ieron* you learned for *comer* and *vivir*. The strong
stem does not change — *tuv-*, *hic-*, *estuv-* — it simply takes the ordinary
plural ending.

[PAUSE 2s] And the stress moves: *TU-ve*, but *tu-VIE-ron*. The stem holds it in
*tuve* and *tuvo* and hands it back here, because *-ieron* is two syllables and
takes it.

Note that *tuviste* never had it — *tu-VIS-te* is stressed on the ending like any
ordinary past. **The stem only ever held the stress in two of the forms**, which
is exactly how much irregularity Spanish was willing to pay for.

No written accent on any of them — they end in *-n*, so the default is already
right, exactly as with *hablaron*.
