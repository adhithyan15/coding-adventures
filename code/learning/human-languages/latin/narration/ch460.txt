Latin, chapter 460: Crowd, Crowded, To throng, To go back and forth, Near the city.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
turba, turbae — a crowd, a throng; an uproar.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for the sacred boundary, then the Latin for to build up.

You'll want to know: turba, turbae.
turba, turbae — "a crowd, a throng; an uproar".
In viā turba magna est, et raeda lentē it. There is a big crowd in the street, and the carriage goes slowly.
First declension, feminine: turba, turbae f. First disorder and noise, then the crowd that makes it. English disturb and turbulent belong to the same family.

The word, taken apart.
turba is usually linked with Greek tyrbē 'turmoil, disorder' (EtymDB; Lewis & Short); whether it was borrowed or is a cousin is not settled. English disturb and turbulent come from words built on it.

Lesson 2 of 5.
frequēns, frequentis — crowded, full of people; frequent.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to build up, then the Latin for a crowd.

You'll want to know: frequēns, frequentis.
frequēns, frequentis — "crowded, full of people; frequent".
Forum hodiē frequēns est, quod mercātus est. The forum is crowded today, because it is market day.
An adjective of one ending: frequēns, genitive frequentis. First 'packed close', then 'often'. English frequent comes from it.

The word, taken apart.
frequēns is usually traced to PIE bʰrek- 'to cram together' (EtymDB; Lewis & Short compare farciō 'to stuff'). English frequent comes from it.

You'll want to know: frequentō, frequentāre.
frequentō, frequentāre — "to throng, to crowd into; to visit often".
Cīvēs thermās post merīdiem frequentant. The citizens throng the baths in the afternoon.
frequentō, frequentāre, frequentāvī, frequentātum, with an accusative. English frequent as a verb comes from it.

The word, taken apart.
frequentō is built on frequēns, frequentis 'crowded, frequent' (EtymDB; Lewis & Short).

Lesson 4 of 5.
commeō, commeāre — to go back and forth, to come and go.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for crowded, then the Latin for to throng.

You'll want to know: commeō, commeāre.
commeō, commeāre — "to go back and forth, to come and go".
Mercātōrēs inter portum et forum commeant. Merchants go back and forth between the harbour and the forum.
commeō, commeāre, commeāvī, commeātum. The noun commeātus is the coming and going, then supplies and leave.

The word, taken apart.
commeō is built from con- and meō 'to go, to pass' (EtymDB; Lewis & Short).

Lesson 5 of 5.
suburbānus, suburbāna, suburbānum — near the city, suburban.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to throng, then the Latin for to go back and forth.

You'll want to know: suburbānus, suburbāna, suburbānum.
suburbānus, suburbāna, suburbānum — "near the city, suburban".
Dīves vīllam suburbānam habet et saepe ibi cēnat. The rich man has a villa near the city and often dines there.
An adjective of three endings, from sub 'near, under' and urbs 'city'. Suburbānum, a villa near the city. English suburban comes from it.

The word, taken apart.
suburbānus is built from sub 'under, close to' and urbānus 'of the city', from urbs (Lewis & Short). English suburban comes from it.
