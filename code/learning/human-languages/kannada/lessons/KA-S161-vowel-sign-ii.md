# ◌ೀ — the long one, on the sign you already read

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[KA-SCRIPT-RECOG-160] -->

[PAUSE 2s] You say *nīnu* and *nīvu* every time you address somebody. The mark that makes that *i* long is the one you have never been shown.

## Script you'll notice: ◌ೀ
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[KA-SCRIPT-RECOG-161]; assesses=[] -->

**◌ೀ** — *ī*.

It is a **vowel sign** — it cannot stand alone, it rides on a consonant and changes the vowel that consonant carries.

Set it beside the short one you have read since chapter four:

| short | long |
|---|---|
| ಿ — *i* | ೀ — *ī* |

**This is the most-used character in this book that nothing has taught.** It is
in the word for *you*, in the colour blue, and in the polite verb ending you use
whenever you ask somebody how they are.

You already say these:

- **ನೀನು / ನೀವು** *nīnu / nīvu* — you, familiar and respectful
- **ನೀಲಿ** *nīli* — blue
- **ನೀವು ಹೇಗಿದ್ದೀರಾ?** *nīvu hēgiddīrā?* — how are you? — and the ending carries it twice

## Writing: ◌ೀ
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[KA-SCRIPT-RECOG-161] -->

Put your pen on ◌ೀ and follow its line. Copy the shape you can see — slowly,
and larger than it is printed.

> This book does not yet tell you **where to start the character or which way to
> travel**. That is a real thing, taught with real variation from school to
> school, and it is not written down here until it can be written down with a
> source. Copying what is in front of you needs no such source, and it is how the
> shape gets into your hand in the meantime.
