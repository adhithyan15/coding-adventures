# el punto — the mark you have read ten thousand times and never been told about

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Look at the last sentence you read. It ended in a mark. Say what
that mark is called in Spanish before reading on — and notice that this book
has printed it on every page without once naming it.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-ORTH-C424-01]; assesses=[] -->

**el punto** — "the full stop," or in American English, the period.

Spanish gives the same mark a different name according to what follows it, and
those names appear in exam instructions, so they are worth having:

| what comes next | name | what it closes |
|---|---|---|
| another sentence, same paragraph | **punto y seguido** | a sentence |
| a new paragraph | **punto y aparte** | a paragraph |
| nothing | **punto final** | the whole text |

The mark on the page is identical in all three. What changes is the size of the
thing being finished — a sentence, a block, or a text. *Seguido* means
"continued," *aparte* means "separate," and *final* means what it looks like.

## Grammar Lens: spacing, and the one place the mark is left off
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-ORTH-C424-01] -->

Two mechanical habits that a written paper is marked on:

| | correct | wrong |
|---|---|---|
| spacing | `Hola. Adiós.` | `Hola .Adiós.` |
| after a title | `Mi familia` | `Mi familia.` |

No space before the point, one space after it. And a **title or a heading takes
no point at all** — which is why every heading in this book ends bare.
