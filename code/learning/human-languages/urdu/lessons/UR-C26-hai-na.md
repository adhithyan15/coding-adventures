# ہے نا؟ (hai nā?) — isn't it?

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[UR-GRAMMAR-KYA-POLAR, UR-GRAMMAR-NA-NA] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Ask a yes-or-no question with **کیا**. Then say the negator that
stands in front of a refused noun.

## You'll want to know: ہے نا؟
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[UR-FUNC-HAI-NA]; assesses=[UR-LEX-YAHAN, UR-LEX-KAMRA] -->

> **آپ یہاں ہیں، ہے نا؟** — *āp yahāṅ haiṅ, hai nā?* — **you are here, aren't
> you?**

> **یہ آپ کا کمرہ ہے، ہے نا؟** — **this is your room, isn't it?**

Both halves have been in this book for twenty-five chapters. **ہے** arrived in
chapter two and **نہ** in chapter nineteen, and nobody has ever put them
together. It is not a new word; it is an assembly.

## Grammar Lens: two ways to ask, and they mean different things
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[UR-FUNC-HAI-NA, UR-GRAMMAR-KYA-POLAR, UR-LEX-JI-HAN, UR-LEX-THIK] -->

| how you ask | what it says |
|---|---|
| **کیا** … *āp ṭhīk haiṅ?* | I do not know. Tell me. |
| *āp ṭhīk haiṅ*, **ہے نا؟** | I think so. Agree with me. |

Chapter nineteen's **کیا** opens a genuine question. The tag makes a statement
and asks the listener to confirm it, and a conversation runs on the second far
more than on the first.

The reply is the *jī hāṅ* of chapter one. (*Ṭhīk* and *jī* stay in romanization:
between them they need three letters the script ladder has not reached.)
