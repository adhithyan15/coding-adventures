Italian, chapter 264: First, Then, In the End --- A Story Told in Order.
4 lessons. You can do the first 2 of them in the car; after that you will want to have stopped.


Lesson 1 of 4.
prima, poi, alla fine — the events, in order.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Chapter twenty-eight put a dinner in order: prima the first course, dopo the second. Say "before" and "after". (Prima, dopo.)
[pause 1 second]
Now the same words go to work on a story. The scene is painted; the events need a running order.

Grammar Lens: three places in a story.
Prima ho comprato il biglietto. — First I bought the ticket. Poi ho aspettato il treno. — Then I waited for the train. Alla fine sono tornato a casa. — In the end I went back home.
Poi is the plain "then, next". It stands between two events and points from the first to the second, and you can use it as many times as the story has events: poi … poi … poi.
Dopo does a little more. It can stand alone, "afterwards", but it can also take a noun: dopo il caffè, "after the coffee". Poi cannot.
Alla fine is "in the end": the last event, and usually the outcome. You have infine, "finally", too; infine closes a list, alla fine closes a story. C'era molto traffico, ma alla fine sono tornato a casa — there was a lot of traffic, but in the end I got home.
Notice what these words do not touch: the scene. Faceva freddo needs no poi. Order belongs to the events.

The word, taken apart: one root, twice.
poi comes from Latin post, "after, behind", through a Vulgar Latin pos that had already dropped the t (EtymDB 2.1). And you met post before: dopo is dē + post, two Latin words welded together. So poi and dopo are the same root, once bare and once with dē in front. English keeps post in postpone and postscript.
fine, "end", in alla fine, is Latin fīnis, "boundary, end" — inherited (EtymDB 2.1). English final, finish and define share it.

Lesson 2 of 4.
raccontare — to tell a story, which is to count it out.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Three pieces are in your hands: the imperfetto to hold the scene still, prima, poi and alla fine to order the events, and perché to explain one of them. This lesson adds no fourth piece. It puts the three together, under the verb that names the act.

Grammar Lens: the backbone of a told story.
raccontare, "to tell (a story)", is a plain -are verb you have had since chapter one hundred and thirty-six. Here is what it tells, once it has both pasts to work with:
Era sabato. Faceva caldo e c'era il sole. Prima sono andato al mercato. Poi ho incontrato un amico, e abbiamo parlato molto. Alla fine sono tornato a casa, perché ero stanco. It was Saturday. It was hot and the sun was out. First I went to the market. Then I met a friend, and we talked a lot. In the end I went home, because I was tired.
Follow the two pasts as they divide the work. Era, faceva, c'era: imperfetto, the scene, held still. Sono andato, ho incontrato, abbiamo parlato, sono tornato: passato prossimo, the events dropped in. Prima, poi and alla fine put the events in order; perché explains the last one, and its reason, ero stanco, is scene again. Every word of it is one this book has taught you.

The word, taken apart: counting it out.
raccontare is built on contare, "to count", which you met in chapter two hundred and six. Contare is inherited from Latin computāre, "to reckon up" (EtymDB 2.1), and computāre is com-, "together", plus putāre, "to settle a reckoning" (Lewis & Short). The ra- in front is Italian's re- plus a-: to count something out for someone. EtymDB 2.1 has no row for raccontare itself, so that last step is the usual reading of its parts.
English has the same picture. To recount a story is to count it out (neither source traces that English word), and tell once meant "to count" (EtymDB 2.1) — a bank teller still does.

[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
In your story, which past held the sunshine and which one took you to the market? (C'era — imperfetto; sono andato — passato prossimo.) Which words put the events in order? (Prima, poi, alla fine.) What verb is inside raccontare? (Contare, "to count", from Latin computāre.)


Lesson 3 of 4.
(reading) — a journey, told in both pasts.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, to read the printed text yourself. The questions are about what you read, so save the whole lesson for when you have stopped.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Chapter thirty-six gave you forty-seven words in a row, all in the present. This passage is twice as long, and it happened yesterday.
[pause 1 second]
Every word in it was taught before this page.

[once you have stopped driving — this part needs your eyes or your hands; if you are driving, skip ahead to the next part]
Reading.
Ieri mattina faceva freddo e pioveva. Ero alla stazione, e il mio amico era lì. C'erano molte persone, e il treno era in ritardo. Prima abbiamo aspettato al binario. Poi abbiamo preso un caffè, perché eravamo stanchi. Mentre parlavamo, hanno annunciato il treno, e siamo saliti subito. Il viaggio era lungo, ma non era noioso: il mio amico raccontava la storia di suo padre, e io ascoltavo. Fuori pioveva ancora, ma in treno faceva caldo. Alla fine, la sera, eravamo al mare. Il mare era bello, e c'era la luna. Ero stanco, ma ero contento.
[pause 3 seconds]
Read it once without stopping. Do not translate as you go; let the sentences arrive.
[pause 3 seconds]
Then read it a second time and sort every verb into one of two piles: the scene, or what happened.

Why it's said this way.
[pause 2 seconds]
The events pile is short: abbiamo aspettato, abbiamo preso, hanno annunciato, siamo saliti. Four things happened, and prima and poi put the first two in order.
[pause 2 seconds]
Everything else is scene: the cold, the rain, the crowd, the late train, the moon. A story is mostly background, and the imperfetto marks every piece of it.
[pause 2 seconds]
Mentre parlavamo is still running when the announcement cuts into it. And raccontava is scene too: the telling went on while the train moved. Ha raccontato would have made it one finished event.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: the passage aloud, once, without stopping]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[question — say your answer, then pause 15 seconds]
How many events in the passato prossimo are in the passage?
[question — say your answer, then pause 12 seconds]
What cut into mentre parlavamo?
[question — say your answer, then pause 10 seconds]
Where were they in the end?

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 2 seconds]
How many words in that passage were new? (None.)
[pause 2 seconds]
Why is raccontava in the imperfetto? (The telling went on while the train moved: it is scene.)
[pause 2 seconds]
Which word opened the line where a scene and an event met? (Mentre.)


Lesson 4 of 4.
Review — tell it back.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say "first", "then" and "in the end". (Prima, poi, alla fine.)

What you've built.
You have read a story. Now you tell one, which is the same skill turned round: a reader sorts verbs into scene and event, and a teller has to choose them.
[pause 2 seconds]
The journey from the reading, in five cues. Answer each one in Italian, out loud, in a full sentence. Choose the past as you go: is this cue the scene, or something that happened?
