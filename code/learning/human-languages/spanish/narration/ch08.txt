Lesson 1 of 5.
¿Cuántos años tienes? — "how old are you?"

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
With tener and your numbers, you can ask and give age — and Spanish does it in a way English doesn't: you don't be a number of years old, you have them.

The phrase, taken apart.
¿Cuántos años tienes? = "How old are you?" literally: "How many years do you have?"
cuántos = "how many," from Latin quantus, "how much/great" becomes English quantity, quantum, quantify. (It's the qu- question family — qué, cómo, dónde, cuántos.) It agrees: cuántos años (masc. pl.).
años = "years," from Latin annus — and look, the ñ is back (the doubled-nn of annus becomes añ, your writing lesson). annus also gives English annual, anniversary, biennial.
tienes = "you have" (the tener you just learned).
Age is had, not been.
This is a real difference to internalize: Spanish counts age with tener ("have"), not ser/estar ("be").
— ¿Cuántos años tienes? — Tengo veinte años. ("I have twenty years.")
Saying "soy veinte" ("I am twenty") is simply wrong in Spanish. You possess your years; you don't equal them. (French and Italian do the same — j'ai vingt ans, ho vent'anni, all "I have.")

[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: "¿Cuántos años tienes?" — KWAN-tos AN-yos TYE-nes]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: answer — "Tengo ___ años" with your own number]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: never "soy ___"; always "tengo ___ años" — age is had]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
What does ¿Cuántos años tienes? literally ask? ("How many years do you have?") Which verb gives age in Spanish — ser or tener? (tener — you have your years.) What letter does años bring back, and from what? (The ñ, from Latin annus's doubled nn.) Next: put counting and age together in practice.


Lesson 2 of 5.
uno, dos, tres, cuatro, cinco — counting to five.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Numbers are the most reused words in any language — and Spanish counts almost exactly like Latin, which means you already half-know them from English. Here are 1 to 5, each with the English words it planted.

Sounds you'll need.
diphthong-ue — cuatro = KWA-tro (the cu- is a kw glide).
r-tap — soft taps in tres, cuatro.

The numbers, taken apart.
[a table of 5 rows, read aloud]
Spanish: uno (1). from Latin: ūnus. English cousins: unit, union, unique, unanimous, universe (and a/an, one).
Spanish: dos (2). from Latin: duo. English cousins: duo, dual, duet, double, duplex.
Spanish: tres (3). from Latin: trēs. English cousins: trio, triple, trinity, trident, triangle.
Spanish: cuatro (4). from Latin: quattuor. English cousins: quart, quarter, quadrant, square (Latin qu- becomes Spanish cu-).
Spanish: cinco (5). from Latin: quīnque. English cousins: quintet, quintessence, quintuplet (Latin qu- becomes Spanish c-).
So cinco and quintet are the same number — Latin quīnque just softened its qu- to a Spanish c-. Count the family on your fingers: unit, duo, trio, quart, quint.

Grammar Lens: uno becomes un / una.
uno shrinks before a noun: un (masc.) / una (fem.) — and these double as "a/an" (Spanish makes no difference between "one" and "a"): un café = "a coffee / one coffee." The others (dos, tres…) don't change.

[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: each with its English cousin — "uno/unit, dos/duo, tres/trio, cuatro/ quart, cinco/quint"]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: "un café, una cerveza" — uno becomes un/una before a noun]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Give 1–5 in Spanish. (Uno, dos, tres, cuatro, cinco.) What English word is cinco the cousin of, and why does it look different? (Quintet — Latin quīnque's qu- became Spanish c-.) What two things does un/una mean? ("One" and "a/an.") Next: 6–10.


Lesson 3 of 5.
seis, siete, ocho, nueve, diez — and the calendar's secret.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
The rest of the way to ten — and these four hide a fact that explains the English calendar: September through December are just Latin for 7, 8, 9, 10.

Sounds you'll need.
diphthong-ie — siete = SYE-teh, nueve = NWE-veh (the ie/ue glide, a Spanish signature).

The numbers, taken apart.
[a table of 5 rows, read aloud]
Spanish: seis (6). from Latin: sex. English cousins: sextet, sextant, semester (six months).
Spanish: siete (7). from Latin: septem. English cousins: September (the 7th month!), septet, septuagenarian.
Spanish: ocho (8). from Latin: octō. English cousins: October (8th), octopus, octave, octagon.
Spanish: nueve (9). from Latin: novem. English cousins: November (9th), novena.
Spanish: diez (10). from Latin: decem. English cousins: December (10th), decimal, decade, dime, decimate.
Why are the "7th–10th" months numbered 7–10 when they sit at 9–12? Because the old Roman year began in March — so September really was the 7th month. Julius and Augustus later inserted July and August (named for themselves), shoving the counting-months out of place, but the Latin numbers stuck. Every time you say December, you're saying Roman "ten."

Grammar Lens: building the teens (a fusion).
Past ten, Spanish fuses "ten and X" into one word:
diez y seis ("ten and six") becomes dieciséis (16).
diez y siete becomes diecisiete (17), and so on to 19.
English did the identical thing: six + ten becomes sixteen. So dieciséis and sixteen are built the same way, from the same two numbers.

[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: "diez y seis becomes dieciséis" — the fusion, like sixteen]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Give 6–10. (Seis, siete, ocho, nueve, diez.) Why is December Latin for "ten"? (The Roman year started in March, so the 10th month was December.) How do you make 16, and what English word matches? (diez y seis becomes dieciséis; sixteen.) Next: the verb for having — tener.

Lesson 5 of 5.
tener — "to have," your first irregular verb.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, because the lesson points at something written down. You can listen to all of it now and come back to the parts that need looking at once you have stopped.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Every verb so far has been regular — hablo, como, vivo, all tidy. tener ("to have") is your first irregular one, and it breaks the rule in the two ways Spanish verbs most often do. Meet the exceptions now; you'll see them again and again.

Sounds you'll need.
diphthong-ie — the stem grows a -ie-: tienes = TYE-nes.

The word, taken apart.
tener comes from Latin tenēre, "to hold, to keep, to grasp." Having is, at root, holding. That root has a firm grip on English:
tenant (one who holds land), tenure, tenacious (holding on), tenable, tenet (a held belief).
with prefixes: contain ("hold together"), retain ("hold back"), maintain ("hold in hand"), sustain ("hold up"), detain, obtain.

Grammar Lens: the two irregularities.
tener misbehaves in exactly the two classic Spanish ways:
The yo form is odd: not "teno" but tengo (a -g- appears). Several common verbs do this (hago, pongo, salgo, vengo).
The stem vowel breaks e becomes ie in the stressed forms — a stem-changer:
[a table of 5 rows, read aloud]
yo, tengo, (the -go form).
tú, tienes, (e becomes ie).
él/ella/usted, tiene, (e becomes ie).
nosotros, tenemos, (back to e — unstressed).
ellos/ustedes, tienen, (e becomes ie).
Notice tenemos keeps the plain e — the vowel only cracks to ie when it's stressed. That "boot" pattern (all forms but nosotros/vosotros) is the single most common irregularity in Spanish; tener is your model for it.

Guided Practice.

[your turn — say: "tener" — te-NER]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: "tengo, tienes, tiene, tenemos, tienen" — hear the ie crack in, and drop out for tenemos]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: "tener" then English "tenant, retain, contain" — the hold family]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
What Latin verb is tener from, and its English cousins? (tenēre, "to hold" — tenant, retain, contain.) Its two irregularities? (The -go yo-form tengo; the e becomes ie stem-change tienes/tiene/tienen.) Which form keeps the plain e? (tenemos — it's unstressed.) Next: use it to give your age.
