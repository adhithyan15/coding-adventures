You'll want to know: छठा.
छठा (chaṭhā) — "sixth".
छठा दिन (chaṭhā din), छठी किताब (chaṭhī kitāb).
The last lesson gave you a rule one lesson ago and this is where it fails. छह is six, and the sixth is not chhahvā̃. It is छठा (chaṭhā), with a retroflex ठ that comes from nowhere in the cardinal.

The word, taken apart: why this one resisted.
Sanskrit had षष्ठ (ṣaṣṭha) for sixth, and Hindi inherited it directly, worn down to chaṭhā. It never went through the -वाँ machinery because it was already there when the machinery was built.
Which is the ordinary shape of this kind of hole: a rule spreads through a language and the words that are said most often resist it longest, because they are already known and nobody has to work them out. English does the same thing — went is not goed for exactly this reason.
So the ordinals are five inherited words and one ending: pahlā, dūsrā, tīsrā, cauthā, chaṭhā, and -वाँ for everything else. That is the whole system, and the rest of this chapter is the ending doing its work.

सातवाँ (sātvā̃) — "seventh".
सातवाँ दिन (sātvā̃ din), सातवीं किताब (sātvī̃ kitāb).
सात stands whole and -वाँ goes on. After the hole at six the ending runs without interruption, and it will keep running for the rest of the number line.
Notice what you are now doing: you are not learning a word, you are applying something. Say sāt and the seventh follows from it without a second act of memory. That is what a rule buys, and it is why the four irregular ones were worth meeting first — they were the price, and it has been paid.

आठ + -वाँ. The retroflex ठ of the cardinal is kept exactly as it is — the ending arrives after it and changes nothing on its way.
Hold chaṭhā and āṭhvā̃ side by side and hear the difference. Both have a retroflex ठ; in the sixth it belongs to the ordinal and not to the cardinal, and in the eighth it belongs to the cardinal and the ordinal borrowed nothing.

You'll want to know: नौवाँ.
नौवाँ (nauvā̃) — "ninth".
नौवाँ दिन (nauvā̃ din), नौवीं किताब (nauvī̃ kitāb).
नौ ends in a vowel where sāt and āṭh ended in consonants, and -वाँ attaches anyway. That is worth one sentence of attention: an ending that does not care what the word before it ends in is a suffix, not a reshaping — it is added, not fused.
Compare cauthā, where cār had to change shape to accept its ending. That is a fused form. This is not. Same language, two different ages of word-making, and you can hear which is which.

You'll want to know: दसवाँ.
दसवाँ (dasvā̃) — "tenth".
दसवाँ दिन (dasvā̃ din), दसवीं किताब (dasvī̃ kitāb).
That is the ten. Say them in order — pahlā, dūsrā, tīsrā, cauthā, pā̃cvā̃, chaṭhā, sātvā̃, āṭhvā̃, nauvā̃, dasvā̃ — and count what you actually had to learn: five words and one ending.
And ten is not a boundary. ग्यारहवाँ is eleventh, बीसवाँ twentieth, सौवाँ hundredth. There is no second list above ten and no third above a hundred; the ending you learned at five reaches every number you will ever say.
