Lesson 1 of 4.
نام (nâm) — name, the word Persian never borrowed.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say زبان, starting on its letter ز. Then say the very first sentence this track ever built, اسمِ من... است. Both words in that sentence for "name" are about to get company.

You'll want to know first — one word.
نام — nâm — name.
Three letters, all already yours: ن ا م — n, long â, m.

The word, taken apart — the inherited half of a pair.
نام (nâm) continues Old Persian nāma, from Indo-European h₁nómn, "name" — one of the most widely shared words in the whole family: English kept it as name, Greek as ónoma ( becomes onomastics), Italian as nome, and Sanskrit as nāman. اسم esm, the word this track taught first, is a separate, later Arabic loan for the identical idea — so Persian now holds two words for "name," inherited and borrowed, sitting side by side the way خوب and وقت already showed the track's vocabulary layers working.

دل (del) — heart.

You'll want to know first — one word.
دل — del — heart.
Two letters, both already yours: د ل — d, l.

The word, taken apart — one of the family's oldest survivors.
دل (del) continues Middle Persian dil, from the ancient Indo-European word for heart, ḱḗr — the same root English wore down to heart, Armenian kept as sirt, and Sanskrit built into hṛdaya. Persian uses دل (del) far beyond anatomy, the way English leans on heart for courage and feeling rather than only the organ — a later chapter's word for "want" will lean on it too.

Lesson 3 of 4.
در (dar) — door.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say دل (del), and the root it shares with heart. This lesson's word shares its own root with an equally ordinary English word.

You'll want to know first — one word.
در — dar — door.
Two letters, both already yours: د ر — d, r.

The word, taken apart — a textbook cognate.
در (dar) continues Old Persian duvar-, "door, gate," from Indo-European dʰwer-. Few Indo-European words are this well attested across every branch of the family at once: English door, German Tür, Latin foris ( becomes foreign, "outside the door"), Greek thýra, and Sanskrit dvāra all continue the identical ancient word. در (dar) is Persian's contribution to a cognate set most Indo-European languages still carry.

Lesson 4 of 4.
کتاب (ketâb) — book, and the name exchange this chapter reopens.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say در (dar), and the four languages that share its root. This chapter's last word breaks that pattern completely.

You'll want to know first — one word.
کتاب — ketâb — book.
Four letters, all already yours: ک ت ا ب — k, t, long â, b.

Grammar Lens: a possessed object, the pattern one more time.
کتابِ من، لطفاً. — ketâb-e man, lotfan. — My book, please.
The same ezafe -e from اسمِ من, کلیدِ من, and دخترِ من links a fourth kind of noun to its owner. Four different words, one grammar habit.

The word, taken apart — where the layers run the other way.
کتاب (ketâb) is not inherited Persian at all: it is borrowed whole from Arabic kitāb, "book," built on the three-consonant root ك ت ب (k-t-b), "to write." Persian's four new words this chapter show its vocabulary layers running in both directions at once — نام (nâm) is inherited where اسم, taught first, is Arabic; کتاب (ketâb) is Arabic where Persian has no single inherited word of its own for the idea. دل (del) and در (dar) are older still, each carrying an Indo-European root beside a whole set of English cousins.
