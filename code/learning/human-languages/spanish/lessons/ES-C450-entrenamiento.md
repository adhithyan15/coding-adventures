# el entrenamiento — the session

up somewhere you would not expect it.

## You'll want to know: el entrenamiento
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-C450-SUELO-05]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C450-SUELO-04] -->

**el entrenamiento** — training, and a single training session.

*Tengo entrenamiento los martes* — I have training on Tuesdays. *El
entrenamiento es en el césped de atrás* — training is on the back pitch. And
the people: **el entrenador**, the coach; **entrenar**, to train; **entrenarse**,
to train yourself.

Away from sport it keeps working: *un entrenamiento de dos días* for a course
at work.

## Grammar Lens: -miento names the process
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C450-SUELO-05, ES-LEX-CORRER-10] -->

You have met **-anza** turning a verb into a noun. **-miento** does the same
job, and the two split the language between them:

| verb | noun |
|---|---|
| entrenar | el entrena**miento** |
| mover | el movi**miento** |
| conocer | el conoci**miento** |

Every **-miento** noun is masculine, as every **-anza** noun is feminine, so
the ending hands you the article both times.

Which verb takes which is not predictable and has to be met word by word —
*esperanza* but *movimiento*, with nothing in the verbs to tell you. What you
can rely on is the gender, and that is worth having.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C450-SUELO-05, ES-LEX-TREN] -->

French **entraîner**, to drag along — and inside it the same *traîner* that
gave Spanish **el tren**.

Hold the two together. A **tren** is the set of carriages *dragged* behind the
engine. To **entrenar** somebody is to *drag them along* with you until they
can keep up. The coach is out in front and the squad is the train.

English took the identical picture from the identical French word, which is
why *train* in English means both the thing on rails and what a coach does to
a team. Spanish split the two forms — *tren* and *entrenar* — and so kept the
relationship visible in a way English hides.
