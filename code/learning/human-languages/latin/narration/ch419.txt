Latin, chapter 419: Paying It Back.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
persolvō, persolvere — to pay in full.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for interest, then the Latin for borrowed.

You'll want to know: persolvō, persolvere.
persolvō, persolvere — "to pay in full".
Tandem omne aes aliēnum persolvit. At last he paid off his whole debt.
persolvō, persolvere, persolvī, persolūtum, per- through + solvō: to loosen the whole debt.

The word, taken apart.
persolvō is built from per- "through, completely" and solvō "to loosen, to pay" (Lewis & Short).

Lesson 2 of 5.
ūsūra, ūsūrae — interest charged; use.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for borrowed, then the Latin for to pay in full.

You'll want to know: ūsūra, ūsūrae.
ūsūra, ūsūrae — "interest charged; use".
Ūsūra gravis erat. The interest was heavy.
ūsūra, ūsūrae f., built on ūtor, to use: payment for the use of money, often plural. English usury.

The word, taken apart.
ūsūra is built on ūtor, ūsus "to use" (EtymDB; Lewis & Short): payment for the use of money. English usury comes from it.

Lesson 3 of 5.
pignus, pignoris — a pledge, a security.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to pay in full, then the Latin for interest charged.

You'll want to know: pignus, pignoris.
pignus, pignoris — "a pledge, a security".
Ānulum prō pignore dedit. He gave a ring as a pledge.
pignus, pignoris n. A thing handed over as security for a debt, and any pledge of love or loyalty.

The word, taken apart.
pignus comes from the root of pangō "to fasten, to fix" (Lewis & Short), the root also in pacīscor "to make a bargain": a thing that fastens a bargain.

Lesson 4 of 5.
syngrapha, syngraphae — a written bond, a promissory note.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for interest charged, then the Latin for a pledge.

You'll want to know: syngrapha, syngraphae.
syngrapha, syngraphae — "a written bond, a promissory note".
Syngrapham manū suā scrīpsit. He wrote out the bond in his own hand.
syngrapha, syngraphae f., borrowed from Greek syngraphē, a written contract signed by both sides.

The word, taken apart.
syngrapha was borrowed from Greek syngraphē "a written contract" (Lewis & Short), built from syn "together" and graphein "to write". English syngraph comes from it (EtymDB).

Lesson 5 of 5.
fēnerātor, fēnerātōris — a moneylender.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a pledge, then the Latin for a written bond.

You'll want to know: fēnerātor, fēnerātōris.
fēnerātor, fēnerātōris — "a moneylender".
Fēnerātor ā pauperibus pecūniam repetit. The moneylender demands his money back from the poor.
fēnerātor, fēnerātōris m., also spelled faenerātor, built on fēnus: a man who lends at interest.

The word, taken apart.
fēnerātor is built on fēneror "to lend at interest" (Lewis & Short), which comes from fēnus "interest".
