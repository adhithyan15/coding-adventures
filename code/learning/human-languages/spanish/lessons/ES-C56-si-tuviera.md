# si tuviera

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-GRAMMAR-SI-REAL-CONDITION, ES-GRAMMAR-IMPERFECT-SUBJUNCTIVE-DERIVATION] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say the real one. (*Si tengo tiempo, hablo español*.) Now the
supposing form of *tener*. (*Tuviera*.)

[PAUSE 1s] Put them together and you can talk about a day you are not having.

## Grammar Lens: the world that is not the case
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-GRAMMAR-SI-UNREAL-CONDITION]; assesses=[ES-GRAMMAR-AR-CONDITIONAL-SINGULAR, ES-GRAMMAR-CONDITIONAL-HYPOTHETICAL] -->

> **si + supposing form, then the *-ría* form.**

| | |
|---|---|
| *Si tengo tiempo, hablo español.* | and I might well have it |
| *Si tuviera tiempo, hablaría español.* | **but I do not** |

The second sentence says two things at once: what would happen, **and** that it
is not happening. That is carried entirely by the verb forms — no extra word is
needed to say *but I don't*.

[PAUSE 2s] Both halves change together. *Si* takes the supposing form; the
result half takes the *-ría* form you learned for *would*. Neither half can be
left in the present.

## Why it's said this way
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-GRAMMAR-SI-UNREAL-CONDITION] -->

**And *si* never takes the present subjunctive.** Not *si tenga* — that
combination simply does not occur in Spanish. The supposing form after *si* is
always the *-ra* one.

[PAUSE 2s] It is worth knowing why the pairing feels lopsided. The *si* half is
not asserting anything, so it uses the form Spanish reserves for the
unasserted — and the result half **is** making a claim, just a claim about a
world that does not exist, which is what *-ría* is for. Two different jobs, two
different forms, in one sentence.
