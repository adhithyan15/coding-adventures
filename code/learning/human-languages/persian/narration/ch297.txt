Persian, chapter 297: Better Than, And Years Ago.
5 lessons. You can do the first 2 of them in the car; after that you will want to have stopped.


Lesson 1 of 5.
بزرگتر از (bozorgtar az) — bigger than, and the biggest.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say "big", "small" and "better". (Bozorg, kuchek, behtar.)
[pause 1 second]
Behtar already holds the pattern. This lesson opens it up for every adjective you know.

Grammar Lens: -tar, and az for "than".
Add -tar to the adjective, and put az, "from", in front of the thing you compare against:
این اتاق از آن اتاق بزرگتر است. — in otâq az ân otâq bozorgtar ast — This room is bigger than that room.
قطار از اتوبوس زودتر میرسد. — qatâr az otobus zudtar miresad — The train arrives earlier than the bus.
So kuchektar, smaller; zudtar, earlier; dirtar, later. The az phrase usually sits before the -tar word; bozorgtar az ân otâq is heard as well. Behtar is the same pattern on beh, "good", a word that lives today mostly inside behtar and behtarin.

Grammar Lens: -tarin, in front of the noun.
For "the most", add -tarin and move the adjective in front of its noun, with no ezafe between them:
بهترین غذا — behtarin ghazâ — the best food.
این بهترین رستوران شهر است. — in behtarin resturân-e shahr ast — This is the best restaurant in town.
An ordinary adjective follows its noun with the ezafe, ghazâ-ye khub. The -tarin one jumps ahead.

The word, taken apart.
EtymDB 2.1 traces the suffix -tar back to an Indo-European suffix that set one of two things against the other. The same suffix is usually seen at the end of English other, one of two, though EtymDB does not draw that link itself. -tarin is -tar with a further -in.

Lesson 2 of 5.
مثل (mesl) — like, as.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say "this room is bigger than that room". (In otâq az ân otâq bozorgtar ast.) Then "the best food". (Behtarin ghazâ.)
[pause 1 second]
-tar says how two things differ. Describing a place needs a word for how they are the same.

You'll want to know: مثل.
مثل — mesl — "like, as".
این شهر مثل تهران است. — in shahr mesl-e Tehrân ast — This city is like Tehran.
It takes the ezafe, mesl-e, and then the thing it matches: mesl-e khâne, like home; mesl-e ghazâ-ye mâdar, like mother's cooking.
One use to keep whole: مثل همیشه, mesl-e hamishe, "as usual", word for word "like always":
مثل همیشه دیر رسید. — mesl-e hamishe dir resid — As usual, he arrived late.
Say it in one beat, mesl, with a clear s. Its second letter is new: the next lesson puts it in your hand.

The word, taken apart.
mesl is borrowed from Arabic mithl, "like" (EtymDB 2.1), on the Arabic root m-th-l, usually glossed "to be like". Arabic says its middle letter as the th of English thin. Persian says it s and keeps the Arabic letter in the spelling, the way it kept ذ in ghazâ and says it z.

[once you have stopped driving — this part needs your eyes or your hands; if you are driving, skip ahead to the next part]
Script you'll notice: ث.
ث — se, the sound s.
It is the middle letter of مثل (mesl), joined on both sides, ـثـ. Its body is the body of ت, from chapter fifteen, but it carries three dots above instead of two. In the middle of a word that body shrinks to a single small tooth, and only the dots tell ث (se) from ت.
You now hold three ways to write s: س, ص and ث (se). As with the four z letters, Persian says them alike, and the spelling remembers which came from where. Nearly all words with ث (se) came from Arabic.

Writing: ث.
Put your pen on ث (se) and follow its line. Copy the shape you can see — slowly, and larger than it is printed.
This book does not yet tell you where to start this letter or which way to travel. It is not written down here until it can be written down with a source. Copying what is in front of you needs no such source, and it is how the shape gets into your hand in the meantime.

Lesson 4 of 5.
سه سال است که (se sâl ast ke) — for three years now, and three years ago.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say "ago", then "a length of time". (Pish, moddat.) Then "like Tehran". (Mesl-e Tehrân.)
[pause 1 second]
An experience has a length. Persian measures it two ways, and the verb changes with them.

Grammar Lens:... ast ke, with the present.
For a stretch that is still going on, name it, add ast ke, "it is that", and keep the verb in the present:
سه سال است که در تهران زندگی میکنم. — se sâl ast ke dar Tehrân zendegi mikonam — I have lived in Tehran for three years.
Word for word, "it is three years that I live in Tehran". English switches to "have lived"; Persian stays in the present, because the living has not stopped. Zendegi kardan is "to live", zendegi, "life", on kardan.
دو هفته است که اینجا کار میکنم. — do hafte ast ke injâ kâr mikonam — I've been working here for two weeks.
To ask: چه مدت است که اینجا کار میکنید؟, che moddat ast ke injâ kâr mikonid?, "how long have you been working here?".

Grammar Lens: pish, with the plain past.
For a point in the past, the time goes first with pish after it, and the verb is the plain past:
سه سال پیش به تهران آمدم. — se sâl pish be Tehrân âmadam — I came to Tehran three years ago.
And a stretch that is over takes barâ-ye, "for", with the plain past: barâ-ye do hafte be Tehrân raftam, "I went to Tehran for two weeks".
So the first two fit together: se sâl pish âmadam. se sâl ast ke injâ zendegi mikonam.

Review — two places, and how long.

What you've built.
Two chapters, and an experience can now be measured. -tar az sets one thing against another, -tarin crowns one, and mesl says two are alike. Pish fixes a moment in the past;... ast ke with the present measures a stretch that is still running.

Someone who moved, describing it:
do sâl pish be in shahr âmadam. do sâl ast ke injâ zendegi mikonam. in shahr mesl-e Tehrân ast, vali kuchektar ast. ghazâ-ye injâ behtar ast! avval nârâhat budam, vali ba'd khoshhâl shodam.
The last line goes back to chapter two hundred and ninety-six: a mood that lasted, budam, and a change that arrived, shodam.
