Spanish, chapter 428: Five Last Pieces.
7 lessons. You can do the first 5 of them in the car; after that you will want to have stopped.


Lesson 1 of 7.
la arroba — Spanish has a word where English has a noise.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say el correo electrónico. Now try to say your e-mail address aloud in Spanish, and stop at the symbol in the middle.

The word, taken apart.
la arroba — the @ sign.
English calls it at, which is a preposition pressed into service. Spanish has a noun for it, and the noun is much older than e-mail.
An arroba was a unit of weight — about eleven and a half kilos — from Arabic ar-rubʿ, "the quarter," because it was a quarter of a quintal. Merchants wrote it with a looped a, and that shorthand is the symbol on your keyboard.
So when a Spanish speaker reads out an address, they are naming a medieval unit of weight:
ana arroba ejemplo punto es.
You have the other two pieces already: punto for the dot, from the punctuation chapter, and guion if the address carries one.

Grammar Lens: reading an address aloud.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
symbol: @. said aloud: arroba.
symbol: . said aloud: punto.
symbol: -. said aloud: guion.
A listening paper dictates an address using exactly these words, and the whole task is unreadable without the first one. Note that the symbol is feminine: la arroba, because the unit of weight was.

Lesson 2 of 7.
el guion bajo — the hyphen that sits on the floor.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say el guion, the short mark that joins. Say la arroba. One symbol remains before an address can be read out complete.

The word, taken apart.
el guion bajo — the underscore, _.
The name is transparent and worth admiring for it: guion is the hyphen you already know, and bajo means low. It is the low hyphen — the same mark, dropped to the baseline.
English names it under-score, from the typewriter habit of scoring a line under text to underline it. Spanish names it by where it sits relative to the mark it resembles. Two languages, two entirely reasonable descriptions of the same key.

Grammar Lens: the address, complete.
Every symbol an e-mail address can contain now has a Spanish name:
[a table of 4 rows, read aloud]
symbol: @. said aloud: arroba.
symbol: . said aloud: punto.
symbol: -. said aloud: guion.
symbol: _. said aloud: guion bajo.
ana_lopez arroba ejemplo punto es.
Read that aloud and you have completed the task the source calls dictating an e-mail address. It needed four words, three of which you already had.

Lesson 3 of 7.
¡Felicidades! — the word you say, not the word you describe with.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say el guion bajo. The symbols are finished; what follows is what you say to people. Say feliz, then try to congratulate somebody with it — and notice the adjective will not do it.

The word, taken apart.
¡Felicidades! — "congratulations."
It is felicidad, "happiness," in the plural — literally happinesses. Spanish wishes several at once, which is what a congratulation is: not a description of a state but a handful of good wishes thrown at somebody.
The relationship to feliz is visible: feliz becomes felicidad becomes felicidades. An adjective, the noun made from it, and that noun pluralised into an act.

Grammar Lens: which one on which occasion.
Spanish has two congratulation words and they are not interchangeable:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
word: ¡Felicidades! for: a birthday, a saint's day, a wedding — an occasion.
word: ¡Felicitaciones! for: an exam passed, a job won — an achievement.
Felicidades is the one an A1 paper wants, because A1 life is birthdays rather than promotions. In much of Latin America felicitaciones is the commoner of the two; in Spain felicidades covers nearly everything.
And note the marks: an exclamation in Spanish opens with ¡ and closes with!, the same doubling you learned for questions.

Lesson 4 of 7.
¡Salud! — a noun doing a job.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say la salud, health. Say ¡Felicidades! You have one noun that describes and one that acts. This lesson turns the first into the second.

The word, taken apart.
¡Salud! — "cheers," raising a glass.
It is the same word you already have, with no article and an exclamation around it. Stripped of la, it stops describing a condition and becomes a wish: health to you.
That is what almost every toast in Europe is. English cheers wishes cheerfulness; Irish sláinte is literally health; and ¡Salud! is the Spanish member of the same family. Raising a glass is wishing somebody well, and most languages say so plainly.

Grammar Lens: the same word, two occasions.
¡Salud! does a second job that catches English speakers out:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
raising a glass means cheers.
after somebody sneezes means bless you.
English uses two unrelated expressions for those. Spanish uses one word for both, and the reason is the same in each case: you are wishing the person health, whether because they are about to drink or because they have sneezed.
Some speakers say ¡Jesús! after a sneeze instead, and in a formal toast you may hear the fuller ¡A tu salud! — "to your health." The bare word covers both occasions and is never wrong.

Lesson 5 of 7.
el animal — a thing with breath in it.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say ¡Salud! — a word you already had, put to a new use. Now name three creatures you can say in Spanish, and try to say the word that covers all three at once.

The word, taken apart.
el animal — "the animal."
Latin animal, from anima, "breath, soul." An animal is literally a thing that has breath in it — which is how Latin drew the line between the living and the not-living, at the lungs.
Anima is generous, and every word on it is about that breath:
[a table of 4 rows, read aloud]
word: animal. the breath in it: a thing that breathes.
word: ánimo. the breath in it: spirit, courage.
word: desanimado. the breath in it: discouraged — the breath gone out.
word: reanimar. the breath in it: to revive — to put the breath back.
English took the same root for animate, unanimous (of one anima, one mind) and magnanimous (of a great one).

Grammar Lens: the word that covers the others.
This is a superordinate — a word one level up from the ones it covers. The corpus has taught particular creatures for hundreds of chapters without ever teaching the category they belong to.
That gap matters more than it looks. A superordinate is what lets you talk about something you cannot name: un animal grande works when you do not know the word for the creature in front of you, and a reading paper will use it exactly that way, in a notice or a sign that must cover every creature at once.
The stress is on the last syllable — a-ni-MAL — and there is no written accent, because a word ending in -l is stressed there by default.

Lesson 6 of 7.
cinco piezas sueltas — five things that finish five different jobs.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, because the lesson points at something written down. You can listen to all of it now and come back to the parts that need looking at once you have stopped.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Name the five before reading the table. They have less in common than usual, which is the point.

Grammar Lens: what each one completes.
[a table of 5 rows, read aloud]
word: la arroba. finishes: reading an address aloud.
word: el guion bajo. finishes: the same.
word: ¡Felicidades! finishes: congratulating somebody.
word: ¡Salud! finishes: raising a glass, and answering a sneeze.
word: el animal. finishes: naming a creature you cannot name.
The address needed only these two: punto and guion were already yours from the punctuation chapter.
Each was the last missing piece of something the corpus could otherwise nearly do. That is what makes them worth a chapter together despite having nothing else in common: the address needed two more words, the social phrases were absent although the adjectives behind them were taught, and a dozen creatures had no category.

Grammar Lens: two of them are nouns doing other work.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
word: salud. as a noun: health. as an act: cheers, bless you.
word: felicidad. as a noun: happiness. as an act: —.
word: felicidades. as a noun: —. as an act: congratulations.
¡Salud! drops its article and becomes a wish. ¡Felicidades! takes a plural and becomes an act. Neither is a new word in the ordinary sense; both are an existing word put to a different use, which is why the adjectives behind them were never enough on their own.

Lesson 7 of 7.
una invitación — reading it, and answering it.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say the two things you would say at a birthday. One congratulates and one raises a glass.

Reading.
Read this invitation aloud, saying every symbol by name:
Cumpleaños de Ana Sábado, a las ocho. Confirma en ana_lopez arroba ejemplo punto es.
The address line is written in words here because that is how it is said. On a screen it would read ana_lopez@ejemplo.es, and saying it aloud requires the four names: guion bajo, arroba, punto, and the letters themselves.

Grammar Lens: answering it.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
moment: arriving. what you say: ¡Felicidades!
moment: the first glass. what you say: ¡Salud!
moment: somebody sneezes. what you say: ¡Salud!
Two words cover the whole evening, and one of them covers two of the three moments.
If the invitation had been to a farm rather than a flat, you would also have the word for whatever was in the field: un animal.
