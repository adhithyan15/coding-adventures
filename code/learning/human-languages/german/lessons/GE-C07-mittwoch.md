# Mittwoch — the day with no god

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GE-GRAMMAR-GOTT-PLUS-TAG-01, GE-LEX-TAG-02] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Four days in and the shape has held every time. Here is the first
one that breaks it, and you can hear the break before you know the reason: this
word does not end in *Tag* at all.

## You'll want to know: Mittwoch
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[GE-LEX-MITTWOCH-01]; assesses=[GE-GRAMMAR-GOTT-PLUS-TAG-01] -->

> **Mittwoch** — "Wednesday," and literally "**mid-week**."

No god, no *Tag*. *Mitt-* is the middle, *Woche* is the week, and the day is
named for its position rather than for anybody.

English did **not** do this. *Wednesday* is **Woden's day** — the chief god,
sitting exactly where the pattern says he should. So English kept the Germanic
name and German threw it away, which is the opposite of what the rest of this
chapter would lead you to expect.

## Sounds you'll need
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GE-SOUND-NACHT-CH-01, GE-LEX-MITTWOCH-01] -->

- `ch-ach` — the ending is the raspy back-of-the-throat sound you first met in
  *Nacht*. Same sound, same spelling, and it closes this word too:
  *MIT-vokh*.

The *w* in the middle is a German *w*, so it is a *v*.

## Why it's said this way
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GE-LEX-MITTWOCH-01, GE-GRAMMAR-GOTT-PLUS-TAG-01] -->

The medieval Church disliked naming a day of its week for the chief pagan god.
So German dropped Wodan and put a plain description in his place, and the
replacement stuck.

That is a decision you can date and attribute, sitting inside an everyday word.
Most of the etymology in this book is drift — sounds wearing down over
centuries with nobody deciding anything. This one is somebody choosing, and the
choice is still in the language a thousand years later.

English, further from that authority, kept its god.
