Sanskrit, chapter 30: Welcoming a Guest.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
द्वारम् (dvāram) — a door, a gate.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new word: what did dhanyaḥ mean, and what was inside dhanyavādaḥ?

You'll want to know: द्वारम्.
द्वारम् (dvāram) — "a door, a gate".
A guest is met at the द्वारम् (dvāram) — the door, the gate.
This is one of the securest cousins in the book. Latin foris and forēs, "door" — whence forum, the space outside the door, and foreign, what is out of doors. Greek thura. English door, German Tür, Russian dver. The word for the opening of a house survived every migration intact, which is what you would expect of a word every generation says every day. Indian place names keep it too: Haridwar is Hari's gate.

What you've built.
A door, and a word that reached every branch of the family.

Lesson 2 of 5.
आसनम् (āsanam) — a seat.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new word: what did dvāram mean?

You'll want to know: आसनम्.
आसनम् (āsanam) — "a seat".
Having met the guest at the door, you offer an आसनम् (āsanam) — a seat. The root means "to sit", and the ending makes it the thing you sit on.
English borrowed the word without the furniture: an āsana in yoga is a posture, a way of being seated. Both senses live in the one Sanskrit word, and the ordinary one is the older. Neuter -अम्, like द्वारम् (dvāram) before it.

What you've built.
The second step of a welcome, and a third borrowed word restored.

Lesson 3 of 5.
पुष्पम् (puṣpam) — a flower.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new word: what did āsanam mean, and what did dvāram mean?

You'll want to know: पुष्पम्.
पुष्पम् (puṣpam) — "a flower".
पुष्पम् (puṣpam) is a flower. Its root means "to bloom, to thrive, to nourish", so the word names the opening rather than the petals.
You already have फलम्, the fruit, and now the flower that precedes it — and in Sanskrit the pair puṣpa-phala travels together as a set phrase, flower-and-fruit, the way English says bricks and mortar. Neuter -अम्, like फलम् itself.

What you've built.
A flower to set beside the fruit you already had.

Lesson 4 of 5.
माला (mālā) — a garland.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new word: what did puṣpam mean?

You'll want to know: माला.
माला (mālā) — "a garland".
Threaded, the flowers become a माला (mālā) — a garland, put over the head of a guest of honour, and also the string of beads counted through in prayer. English borrowed the second sense as mala.
Its own history is unsettled: no agreed cousin outside India, like केशः and अलम् before it. Feminine, ending in -आ, like यात्रा and माता.

What you've built.
A third word with no proven travels, and a garland made of the last one.

Lesson 5 of 5.
अञ्जलिः (añjaliḥ) — the two hands cupped together.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the last word of this book: what did mālā mean, and what did dvāram mean?

You'll want to know: अञ्जलिः.
अञ्जलिः (añjaliḥ) — "the two hands cupped together".
अञ्जलिः (añjaliḥ) is the two hands brought together, palms cupped — the gesture your hands make when you say नमस्ते.
The root means "to anoint, to smear", and the cupped hands were first of all a vessel: the shape you make to carry water, or an offering. The gesture of greeting began as the shape of giving something away. So the book ends where it began, on the first word you learned, with the hands finally named.

What you've built.
Five steps of a welcome — door, seat, flower, garland, and the hands themselves.
