# ट्रेन — a word that travelled with the thing

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[HI-LEX-C90-ROUT-02, HI-LEX-C69-GOING-03] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say *by bus*. Say *station*. You have had a station for a while
without a second way of reaching it.

## You'll want to know: ट्रेन
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[HI-LEX-C94-SPAN-04]; assesses=[] -->

**ट्रेन से** — by train.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[HI-LEX-C94-SPAN-04, HI-LEX-C90-ROUT-02] -->

Both of your vehicles are borrowed words, and they are borrowed the same way:

| Hindi | from | by … |
|---|---|---|
| बस | English *bus* | बस से |
| ट्रेन | English *train* | ट्रेन से |

The **से** that makes them *by bus* and *by train* is the one already taught
for means — the same word that says *from*, and that this chapter has been
using for the start of a span.

Look at the first letter. **ट** is the retroflex one, made with the tongue
curled back — and it is used here because English *t* in *train* sounds, to a
Hindi ear, much more like **ट** than like the dental **त**. That is the regular
habit for English loanwords, and it is why **ट्रेन** and not **त्रेन**.

## Grammar Lens: a word Hindi also has its own name for
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[HI-LEX-C94-SPAN-04, HI-LEX-C69-GOING-03] -->

Hindi does have an inherited word for a railway train, and an A1 reader will
still meet **ट्रेन** far more often — on a board, in a timetable, in the middle
of an ordinary sentence about getting to work.

Learn the borrowed one first because it is the one that is said. The other is
worth recognising later, when reading formal notices is the task.
