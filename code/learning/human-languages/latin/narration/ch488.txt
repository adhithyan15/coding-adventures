Lesson 1 of 7.
brūma, brūmae — midwinter, the winter solstice, the shortest day.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to blow through, then the Latin for clear weather.

You'll want to know: brūma, brūmae.
brūma, brūmae — "midwinter, the winter solstice, the shortest day".
Brūmā diēs brevissimus est et nox longissima. At midwinter the day is shortest and the night longest.
First declension, feminine: brūma, brūmae f. Shortened from brevissima (diēs), the shortest day. English brumal 'wintry' comes from it.

The word, taken apart.
brūma comes from brevima, an old form of brevissima 'shortest', as in brevissima diēs 'the shortest day' (EtymDB; Lewis & Short); it is built on brevis 'short'.

Lesson 2 of 7.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for clear weather, then the Latin for midwinter.

You'll want to know: sōlstitium, sōlstitiī.
sōlstitium, sōlstitiī — "the summer solstice; midsummer heat".
Sōlstitiō diēs longissimus est. At the summer solstice the day is longest.
Second declension, neuter: sōlstitium, sōlstitiī n. For Romans mostly the summer one; midwinter was brūma. English solstice comes from it.

The word, taken apart.
sōlstitium is built from sōl 'sun' and sistō 'to stand still' (EtymDB; Lewis & Short): the time when the sun seems to stand still. English solstice comes from it.

Lesson 3 of 7.
canīcula, canīculae — the Dog Star; a little dog.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for midwinter, then the Latin for the summer solstice.

You'll want to know: canīcula, canīculae.
canīcula, canīculae — "the Dog Star; a little dog".
Ubi canīcula ardet, agricolae in umbrā quiēscunt. When the Dog Star blazes, the farmers rest in the shade.
First declension, feminine: canīcula, canīculae f., a diminutive of canis 'dog'. Its rising in high summer brought the hottest days, the dog days.

The word, taken apart.
canīcula is a diminutive of canis 'dog' with -cula (EtymDB; Lewis & Short): a little dog, and the star in the mouth of the Great Dog. English canicular comes from it.

Lesson 4 of 7.
aestīvus, aestīva, aestīvum — of summer, summer.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for the summer solstice, then the Latin for the Dog Star.

You'll want to know: aestīvus, aestīva, aestīvum.
aestīvus, aestīva, aestīvum — "of summer, summer".
Dīves vīllam aestīvam in montibus habet. The rich man has a summer house in the mountains.
An adjective of three endings, from aestās 'summer'. Aestīva (n. pl.), a summer camp. English aestivate comes from the same root.

The word, taken apart.
aestīvus is built on aestās 'summer' with the suffix -īvus (EtymDB; Lewis & Short), and aestās goes back to PIE h₂eydʰ- 'to burn'.

Lesson 5 of 7.
caleō, calēre — to be warm, to be hot.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for the Dog Star, then the Latin for of summer.

You'll want to know: caleō, calēre.
caleō, calēre — "to be warm, to be hot".
Aestāte saxa sub sōle calent. In summer the stones are hot under the sun.
caleō, calēre, caluī, second conjugation. Calidus 'hot' and calor 'heat' come from it; English calorie comes from calor.

The word, taken apart.
caleō is inherited from Proto-Italic kalēō 'to be hot', from PIE ḱelh₁- 'to heat' (EtymDB). calidus 'hot' and calor 'heat', the source of English calorie, are built on it (Lewis & Short).
