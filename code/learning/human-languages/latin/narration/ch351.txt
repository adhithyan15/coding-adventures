Latin, chapter 351: Concīdere et terere.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
concīdō, concīdere — to chop up, to cut to pieces.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to wipe, then the Latin for a small knife.

You'll want to know: concīdō, concīdere.
concīdō, concīdere — "to chop up, to cut to pieces".
Coquus cēpās et ālium concīdit. The cook chopped up the onions and garlic.
concīdō, concīdere, concīdī, concīsum, long ī: con- + caedō. Do not confuse concidō, 'to collapse'. English concise.

The word, taken apart.
concīdō is built on caedō, "to cut", with con- (EtymDB): to cut up small. English concise, cut short, comes from its participle.

Lesson 2 of 5.
secō, secāre — to cut, to slice.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a small knife, then the Latin for to chop up.

You'll want to know: secō, secāre.
secō, secāre — "to cut, to slice".
Pater pānem cultellō secuit. Father sliced the bread with a little knife.
secō, secāre, secuī, sectum. English section and insect, a creature cut into parts, come from it.

The word, taken apart.
secō is inherited from the Proto-Indo-European root sek-, "to cut" (EtymDB). English section, sector and insect come from it.

Lesson 3 of 5.
mortārium, mortāriī — a mortar (a bowl for pounding).

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to chop up, then the Latin for to cut.

You'll want to know: mortārium, mortāriī.
mortārium, mortāriī — "a mortar (a bowl for pounding)".
Coquus herbās in mortārium posuit. The cook put the herbs into the mortar.
Neuter. A heavy bowl of stone or clay. English mortar, for building too, comes from it.

The word, taken apart.
mortārium is a Latin word whose origin is not settled (Lewis & Short). English mortar, both the bowl and the builder mix, comes from it.

Lesson 4 of 5.
pistillum, pistillī — a pestle.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to cut, then the Latin for a mortar.

You'll want to know: pistillum, pistillī.
pistillum, pistillī — "a pestle".
Pistillum ligneum erat et grave. The pestle was wooden and heavy.
Neuter, from a verb meaning 'to pound'. English pestle comes from it.

The word, taken apart.
pistillum is built on pīnsō, "to pound" (EtymDB), with a small-tool ending. English pestle comes from it through French, and pistil directly.

Lesson 5 of 5.
terō, terere — to grind, to rub.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a mortar, then the Latin for a pestle.

You'll want to know: terō, terere.
terō, terere — "to grind, to rub".
Ancilla piper pistillō terēbat. The maid was grinding pepper with the pestle.
terō, terere, trīvī, trītum. English trite, worn out by rubbing.

The word, taken apart.
terō is an inherited verb; Lewis & Short compare Greek teirō, "to wear away". English trite comes from its participle trītus.
