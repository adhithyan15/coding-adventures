Lesson 1 of 4.
کان (kān) — "ear," where the body words start.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say merā khāndān — my family, closing the last chapter. This chapter turns from the people around you to the wellbeing check itself: four words for the body, starting with the one you use to hear the question.

You'll want to know first — one word.
کان — kān — ear.
میرا کان۔ — merā kān. — "my ear."
کان (kān) is masculine, so merā keeps the form you already know from بھائی and خاندان.

The word, taken apart — honestly, not fully traced.
kān continues Sanskrit karṇa by way of Prakrit and Apabhramsa kaṇṇa. That much is solid. Where karṇa itself comes from is not: the trail runs out inside Indo-Aryan, with only a loose, unconfirmed suggestion linking it to Persian کر (kar), "deaf." No English cousin, no settled Indo-European root — the same honest dead end پڑھنا's paṭh- hit two chapters ago.
Notice the pattern from last chapter still holds: کان (kān) is inherited, straight from Sanskrit, the same road as بھائی and بہن. خاندان, the word that closed that chapter, was Persian instead — the body's own words stay native even where their history goes quiet.

Lesson 2 of 4.
ناک (nāk) — "nose," the same three letters as کان (kān), backwards.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, for letter shapes on the page. You can listen to everything else now — leave the section called The letters in this word until you have stopped, and I will say so again when we reach it.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say merā kān — my ear, masculine, unchanged merā. This word shares every letter with it.

You'll want to know first — one word.
ناک — nāk — nose.
میری ناک۔ — merī nāk. — "my nose."
ناک (nāk) is feminine — merī, not merā. Grammatical gender does not follow shape or sound; it has to be learned noun by noun.

The letters in this word.
کان (kān) is ک ا ن — k, ā, n. ناک (nāk) is ن ا ک — n, ā, k. The same three letters, in exactly the opposite order. Ear and nose, spelled as mirror images of each other — a coincidence of Urdu's script, not of its history; the two words are not related.

The word, taken apart — a real cousin, and English agrees.
nāk continues Sauraseni Prakrit ṇakka, from Proto-Indo-Iranian náHs, from Proto-Indo-European néh₂s, "nose." English nose comes from that very root. Where کان (kān)'s ancestry ran out unanswered — recall karṇa's own origin was left unsettled last lesson — ناک's does not: nāk and nose are genuine cousins, joining بھائی and brother as this book's settled Indo-European matches.

Lesson 3 of 4.
آنکھ (āṅkh) — "eye," and a fifth letter learns to breathe.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, for letter shapes on the page. You can listen to everything else now — leave the section called The letters in this word until you have stopped, and I will say so again when we reach it.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say merī nāk — my nose, feminine. The next word on the face is feminine too, and it puts the two-eyed ھ on a letter it has never touched.

You'll want to know first — one word.
آنکھ — āṅkh — eye.
میری آنکھ۔ — merī āṅkh. — "my eye."

The letters in this word.
From the right edge: آ, alif carrying long ā (the same mark you already read in آپ), then ن, then ک k, then ھ.
کھ extends the two-eyed he to a fifth base letter. It has already aspirated ج in samajh-, ٹ in ṭhīk, ڑ in paṛh-, and چ in pūchh-; here it does the same job to ک, giving kh.
The ن in this word is not pronounced as its own consonant. It marks the آ before it as nasal — the vowel said through the nose, with no separate n sound following it. Say āṅkh by nasalizing the ā, not by adding a clean n.

The word, taken apart — a third real cousin.
āṅkh continues Sanskrit ákṣi, from Proto-Indo-European h₃ekʷ-, "eye, to see." English eye descends from that same root. This chapter has now found two settled Indo-European cousins in a row — ناک (nāk)/nose, آنکھ/eye — after کان (kān)'s honest dead end.

Lesson 4 of 4.
منہ (muṅh) — "mouth," and the نون آنکھ (āṅkh) already used without explaining.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, for letter shapes on the page. You can listen to everything else now — leave the section called The letters in this word until you have stopped, and I will say so again when we reach it.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Three face words: kān, nāk, āṅkh — recall that کان and ناک shared the same three letters, reversed. One more word closes the chapter, and it finally explains something آنکھ already did.

You'll want to know first — one word.
منہ — muṅh — mouth (also: face).
میرا منہ۔ — merā muṅh. — "my mouth."
منہ (muṅh) is masculine.

The letters in this word.
From the right edge: م m, then ن, then ہ, the round he you already write in ہوں and ہونا — a real, spoken h, not the two-eyed aspirator.
That middle ن does the same job it did in آنکھ (āṅkh), unglossed at the time: it marks the vowel before it as nasal, not a separate n sound. Say muṅh by nasalizing the u, with no clean n in between.
Keep this apart from میں maiṅ and ہوں hūṅ, whose nasal ending is written with a different letter entirely: ں, nūn ghunna, a dedicated nasalization mark. منہ (muṅh) and آنکھ (āṅkh) nasalize with a plain ن doing double duty; میں and ہوں nasalize with ں, a letter built for exactly that job and nothing else. Same sound, two different letters, depending on which word you are in.

The word, taken apart — a word possibly behind myth.
muṅh continues Sanskrit múkha, "mouth, face." One proposal, from the Sanskrit etymologist Manfred Mayrhofer, ties it to a Proto-Indo-European gesture-root mu-, "pressed lips" — the same root that would sit behind Greek mŷthos ("word," behind English myth) and German Maul, "mouth." Mayrhofer himself calls a non-Indo-European origin less likely but does not rule it out. Hold this one the way you held بہن's link to bhaj- — a real proposal, not a verdict.
