# करने के लिए — in order to

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[HI-CONCEPT-C05-KARNA-01, HI-LEX-C93-REL-06] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say the infinitive *to do*. Now say *for the family*. To put the
first inside the second, one of them has to bend — and by now you can guess
which.

## You'll want to know: करने के लिए
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[HI-LEX-C93-REL-07]; assesses=[] -->

**मैं काम करने के लिए जाता हूँ।** — I go in order to work.

**करना** became **करने**. Nothing else moved.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[HI-LEX-C93-REL-07, HI-LEX-C91-NOUN-06] -->

Look at the ending that changed, and then look at where you have seen that
change before:

| kind of word | plain | before a postposition |
|---|---|---|
| possessive | मेरा | मेरे |
| demonstrative possessive | उसका | उसके |
| own | अपना | अपने |
| noun | बच्चा | बच्चे |
| **infinitive** | **करना** | **करने** |

Five kinds of word, one ending, one trigger. The infinitive is the **last** of
the five, and it needs no new rule at all: **-ना** ends in **-आ**, so it bends
exactly as **बच्चा** does.

The reason it can bend like a noun is that in Hindi it **is** one. **करना** is
both *to do* and *the doing*, which is why it could already sit where a noun
sits. A word that behaves like a noun bends like a noun.

## Grammar Lens: where the purpose goes
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[HI-LEX-C93-REL-07, HI-LEX-C90-ROUT-04] -->

| slot | contents |
|---|---|
| 1 | who |
| 2 | the purpose: **काम करने के लिए** |
| 3 | the main verb, last |

English puts the purpose **after** the verb — *I go to work*. Hindi puts it
**before** — because the verb goes last, and that has not changed since the
first sentence you read.

So the order reverses, and the reversal is the only thing to practise. The
pieces themselves are all old.
