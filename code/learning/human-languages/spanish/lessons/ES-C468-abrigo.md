# el abrigo — the coat

## You'll want to know: el abrigo
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-C468-ABRIGO-03]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C468-ABRIGO-01] -->

**el abrigo** — the coat, the overcoat.

> A tres grados no salgas sin **abrigo**.

**Abrigar** is the verb: *abrígate*, wrap up warm. And *una manta que abriga
mucho* is a blanket that keeps you very warm — the verb is about the warmth,
not the wrapping.

A *chaqueta* is a jacket. An *abrigo* is the long one you put on over
everything else when it is properly cold.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C468-ABRIGO-03, ES-LEX-C468-ABRIGO-02] -->

This word is not named for its shape. It is named for what it does.

Behind *abrigar* is Latin **apricari**, to **bask in the sun**. *Apricus* was
the adjective: open to the sun, warm.

Then Spanish did something quietly odd with it. A word for a place the sun
gets **into** became a word for a place the weather cannot, and the coat is
named for the second one. *El abrigo* is not "a long coat", it is **a
shelter** — *un abrigo rocoso* is a hollow in the rock face out of the
weather, and *al abrigo de* is **in the shelter of**.

| Spanish | the shelter |
|---|---|
| el abrigo | the one you wear |
| al abrigo de | the one you stand behind |

Watch that second one: what follows *de* is what shelters you, not what you
are hiding from.

One warning while *apricus* is in front of you: English **apricot** looks like
a relative and is not one. That word came through Arabic and Portuguese from
Latin *praecoquum*, the early-ripening fruit, and the resemblance to *apricus*
is a coincidence people have been reading meaning into for centuries.

So the garment is named for the sunny corner it replaces.
