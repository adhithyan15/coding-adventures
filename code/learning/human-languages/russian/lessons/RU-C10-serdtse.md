# сердце — "heart," the surest cousin in this book

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[RU-LEX-GLAZ, RU-LEX-ROT] -->

[PAUSE 2s] One more letter, one silent consonant, and then the tightest
Indo-European cognate set this book will show you.

## You'll want to know first — сердце
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[RU-LEX-SERDTSE]; assesses=[RU-GRAMMAR-NOUN-GENDER-ENDING, RU-LEX-GLAZ] -->

> **сердце** — *sérdtse* — **heart**. **Neuter.**

Ends in **-це**, a spelling variant of **-о/-е** forced by the letter before
it — still Chapter 6's neuter row. Stress on the front: *SYÉRT-se*. And
listen closely: the **д** is not pronounced at all. Four consonants in a row
would be unsayable, so Russian drops the middle one in speech — write
*сердце*, say *sérts-e*.

## The letters in this word
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[RU-SCRIPT-TSE]; assesses=[] -->

*(Skim if you read Cyrillic.)* One new letter: **ц**.

- **ц** = **"ts,"** the sound at the end of English *cat**s***. No Latin
  look-alike to trip over — it simply hasn't been in your way until now.

**с, е, р, д, е** you already have.

## The word, taken apart — barely disguised in any of its four languages
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[RU-ETYMON-SERDTSE-HEART]; assesses=[RU-LEX-LYUBIT, RU-ETYMON-LYUBIT-LOVE] -->

**сердце** is Indo-European \**ḱerd-*, and few roots in this book have
travelled so far with so little disguise. English **heart** is the same
word. Latin **cor**, genitive **cordis**, gave English **cordial**
("heartfelt"), **courage** (from the heart), and — by a long and surprising
road through "to learn by heart" — **record**. Greek **kardía** gave
**cardiac** and **cardiology**. Say *sérdtse*, *heart*, *cor*, *kardía* in a
row and the family resemblance needs no proof.

It is fitting that the book's most secure cousin is also the organ
Chapter 5 gave a verb to: **любить**, with its inserted **л**, is what the
*сердце* does.
