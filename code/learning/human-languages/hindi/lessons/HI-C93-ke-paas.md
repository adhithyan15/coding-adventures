# घर के पास — the word that was hiding in *I have*

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[HI-LEX-C84-POSS-05, HI-LEX-C93-REL-02] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say *I have* in Hindi. Now look at the two words you said and ask
what the second one actually means, because so far you have never been told.

## You'll want to know: के पास
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[HI-LEX-C93-REL-03]; assesses=[] -->

**दुकान घर के पास है।** — The shop is near the house.

**पास** means *near*. It has meant *near* the whole time.

## The two words
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[HI-LEX-C93-REL-03, HI-LEX-C84-POSS-05] -->

| phrase | word for word | the job it does |
|---|---|---|
| मेरे पास | near me | I **have** |
| घर के पास | near the house | it is **nearby** |

The same three slots, and the same slot-3 word. What changed is only what sits
in slot 1 — a person or a place.

So Hindi says *having* by saying **nearness**, and the physical sense you are
meeting now is the original one. A language with no verb for *have* reached for
the nearest true thing: whatever is beside you is, for practical purposes,
yours. The possessive chapter taught you the borrowed meaning first because it
is the one a beginner needs first. This is the meaning it was borrowed from.

## Grammar Lens: a place answer
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[HI-LEX-C93-REL-03, HI-LEX-C56-ROAD-04] -->

You have been able to **ask** where something is for sixty chapters. This is
the first shape that lets you **answer** with anything more exact than a room:

| question | answer |
|---|---|
| दुकान कहाँ है? | घर के पास। |

An A1 listening paper does this constantly, because *near* is how people
actually give directions — not in metres, but relative to a landmark both
speakers already know.
