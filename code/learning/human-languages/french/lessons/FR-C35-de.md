# de — "of," and the word already hiding in *du*

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[FR-GRAM-DU-PARTITIF-07, FR-LEX-A-PREP-01, FR-LEX-PAIN-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say **du pain**. You were told two words are inside it. Name them.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[FR-LEX-DE-PREP-01]; assesses=[FR-GRAM-DU-PARTITIF-07, FR-GRAMMAR-ELISION-ARTICLE-02, FR-LEX-CAFE-01, FR-LEX-FRERE-06] -->

> **de** — of, from.

> **le café de Paris** — the café of Paris. · **le frère de Marie** — Marie's
> brother.

That second line is worth a moment. French owns nothing with an apostrophe: it
says **the brother of Marie**, and it has said so since Latin. English is the odd
one here — the possessive **'s** is a Germanic survival that Latin's daughters
never had, so *Marie's brother* is the outlier and *le frère de Marie* is the
ordinary European shape.

Before a vowel it elides, exactly as *le* does:

> **le livre d'Anne** — Anne's book.

And you have been saying **de** since the food chapter without meeting it alone:
*du pain* is **de** + **le**, and *de l'eau* is **de** with nothing to contract.
