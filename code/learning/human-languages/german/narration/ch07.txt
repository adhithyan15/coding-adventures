German, chapter 7: Four, Five, and the Article Hiding in Eins.
4 lessons. All 4 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 4.
vier — "four," written with a v and said with an f.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
The fourth number is the easiest one yet — as long as you do not trust the first letter.

You'll want to know: vier.
vier — "four."

Sounds you'll need.
v-as-f — German v is said as an f. It is not a v sound; that job belongs to w.
ie-long-ee — ie is a long ee, the mirror of ei.
So vier is feer. Put the two vowel rules side by side and the pair is settled for good:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
Spelling: ei. Sound: eye. Example: eins, zwei, drei.
Spelling: ie. Sound: ee. Example: vier, wieder.
Both times, the second letter is the one you say.

The word, taken apart.
vier is English four, and once you know the v is an f you can hear it: feer, four. Latin's cousin is quattuor, which is where Spanish cuatro and French quatre come from — so this is a number where English sides with German and not with the Romance languages at all.
The v-said-as-f is not special to this word. German writes v in a handful of its oldest words — vier, Vater, von, voll — and says every one of them with an f, which is exactly the letter English uses in four, father, from, full.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: "vier" — feer, the v said as an f]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: "eins, zwei, drei, vier"]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: the vowel pair — "ei" as eye, "ie" as ee]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
What is "four"? (Vier, said feer.) How is German v said? (As an f.) Which letter carries the v sound instead? (W.) And how do you tell ei from ie? (Say the second letter.)


Lesson 2 of 4.
fünf — "five," and the law you can hear.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
The fifth number closes the first hand — and it is the clearest example of a sound law anywhere in the language.

You'll want to know: fünf.
fünf — "five."

Sounds you'll need.
umlaut-ue — ü is an ee said with your lips rounded for an oo. Say ee, hold the tongue exactly where it is, and push your lips forward.
So fünf is roughly fewnf, in one syllable. This is the umlaut doing what you learned it does: it takes the back vowel u and drags it to the front of the mouth.

The word, taken apart.
fünf is English five, and the two of them are the sound law from the last lesson caught in the act.
The older word for five began with a p. Latin kept it — quinque still has one in the middle — and Germanic turned every one of those ps into an f. So English got five and German got fünf, both starting with the letter that was once a p:
old p becomes Germanic f: five / fünf, against Latin's quinque.
Say fünf and five one after the other. They are the same word, and the whole distance between them and the Latin one is that single turn.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: "fünf" — say ee, then round your lips without moving your tongue]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: "fünf" then "five" — one word, one law apart]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: "eins, zwei, drei, vier, fünf"]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
What is "five"? (Fünf.) How do you make ü? (Say ee and round your lips.) Which sound turned into the f of five and fünf? (An older p — the one Latin kept.)


Lesson 3 of 4.
eins bis fünf — counting to five.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Five words, one at a time. Counting is something else: it is the five of them running together without a pause to think. That is what this lesson is for, and nothing in it is new.

You'll want to know: the run.
eins, zwei, drei, vier, fünf.
Said straight through: eyns, tsvy, dry, feer, fewnf.
Every one is the English number with a German coat on:
[a table of 5 rows, read aloud]
German: eins. English: one. What changed: nothing — English hid it in the spelling.
German: zwei. English: two. What changed: the tw- German still says.
German: drei. English: three. What changed: th flattened to d.
German: vier. English: four. What changed: the v is said as an f.
German: fünf. English: five. What changed: an older p, now an f in both.
Not one of them is borrowed from Latin. All five are what an English speaker already half knows.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: "eins, zwei, drei, vier, fünf" — straight through, no gaps]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: it again, faster]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: it backwards — "fünf, vier, drei, zwei, eins"]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[repeat that twice]
"eins, zwei, drei, vier, fünf".

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Count to five. (Eins, zwei, drei, vier, fünf.) Now backwards. (Fünf, vier, drei, zwei, eins.) Which of the five is borrowed from Latin? (None of them.)


Lesson 4 of 4.
ein und eine — when "one" becomes "a".

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Eins is what you say while counting. Put it in front of a naming word and it changes shape — and English does the very same trick, so this costs almost nothing.

You'll want to know: ein / eine.
ein / eine — "a," "an," and also "one" when it sits in front of a naming word.
Which of the two you use follows the same three-way split you already know from der, die, das:
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
The word takes: der. So "a" is: ein.
The word takes: das. So "a" is: ein.
The word takes: die. So "a" is: eine.
Two of the three groups share one form, so there is really only one thing to remember: die words take the extra -e. On the two nouns inside guten Tag and gute Nacht:
der Tag becomes ein Tag, "a day", die Nacht becomes eine Nacht, "a night".

Grammar Lens: the counting -s drops.
Counting form: eins. In front of a naming word: ein / eine. The -s belongs to counting and to nothing else.
English does the identical thing, and hides it just as well. One in front of a naming word wore down to a and an — an apple really is one apple, said quickly for a thousand years. So both languages have a special worn-down shape of "one", and both use it as the word for "a".
The other numbers do not do this. Zwei, drei, vier and fünf look exactly the same whether you are counting or putting them in front of something. Only the first number has two shapes, in German as in English.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: "eins" — then "ein", dropping the s]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: "der Tag / ein Tag", "die Nacht / eine Nacht"]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: "one apple", then "an apple" — English doing the same thing]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
What happens to eins in front of a naming word? (It drops the -s: ein.) Which group takes eine? (The die group.) Which English words are the same worn-down "one"? (A and an.) Do the other numbers change? (No — only the first.)
