Malayalam, chapter 78: Whose It Is.
3 lessons. The first lesson already needs your eyes or your hands, so save this one for when you have stopped.


Lesson 1 of 3.
അധ്യാപകന്റെ (adhyāpakanṟe) — "the teacher's".

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, because the lesson points at something written down. You can listen to all of it now and come back to the parts that need looking at once you have stopped.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say my. Then say the word for teacher.
[pause 2 seconds]
You have owned the first of those since the second chapter, and you have never been able to do to any other word what it does.

Grammar Lens: the ending was there all along.
എന്റെ was handed to you whole, as a single word meaning my. Look at its end:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
എന്റെ, my.
അധ്യാപകന്റെ (adhyāpakanṟe), the teacher's.
-ന്റെ is a suffix, and it makes whatever it lands on the owner. That is the one thing this lesson adds, and it turns a word you memorised into a machine you can run.
അധ്യാപകന്റെ പുസ്തകം adhyāpakanṟe pustakaṁ — "the teacher's book".
The owner comes first, wearing the ending; the thing owned follows bare. English does the same with 's and the opposite with of. Malayalam only has the first order.

The word, taken apart.
The script lesson for എന്റെ already showed you ന്റെ on its own, as a conjunct to read. It was a piece of writing then. It is a piece of grammar now, and it is the same piece.
That is worth expecting from this language: something introduced as a shape often turns out later to be a part.

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
What does -ന്റെ do to a noun? (Makes it the owner.) How do you say the teacher's book? (അധ്യാപകന്റെ (adhyāpakanṟe) പുസ്തകം.) And which comes first, the owner or the thing? (The owner.)


Lesson 2 of 3.
അമ്മയുടെ (ammayuṭe) — "mother's".

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say the teacher's book. Then take the ending off and say the teacher again on their own.

Grammar Lens: the same job, a different ending.
അമ്മയുടെ പുസ്തകം ammayuṭe pustakaṁ — "mother's book".
Not -ന്റെ. -ഉടെ, and a യ appears in front of it.
A noun that ends in a vowel takes -യുടെ. അമ്മ ends in a vowel, so that is the ending it gets. അധ്യാപകൻ ends in a consonant, so it took the other one.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
noun ends in: a consonant. ending: -ന്റെ.
noun ends in: a vowel. ending: -യുടെ.
You do not choose between them. The noun in front does, and it decides by its last sound rather than by anything you mean. So the question to ask is never which ending do I want — it is what does this word end in.

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Which ending does a vowel-final noun take? (-യുടെ.) How do you say mother's book? (അമ്മയുടെ (ammayuṭe) പുസ്തകം.) And who picks the ending — you, or the noun? (The noun.)


Lesson 3 of 3.
(whose it is) (ārute enna ōrmma) — from cold.

Warm-up.
[pause 3 seconds]
Close the lessons before this one. Say my, and then say what its last piece does when you put it on somebody else.

Grammar Lens: two endings, one job, and the noun decides.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
owner: അധ്യാപകന്റെ (adhyāpakanṟe). ending: -ന്റെ. because it ends in: a consonant.
owner: അമ്മയുടെ (ammayuṭe). ending: -യുടെ. because it ends in: a vowel.
One job — whose it is — and the choice is made by the last sound of the noun, not by you.
Then the phrase is built the same way every time: owner first with the ending, thing second and bare. adhyāpakanṟe pustakaṁ, ammayuṭe pustakaṁ.

What you've built.
A third possessor, and every one after it.
എന്റെ and നിന്റെ were handed over whole — my and your, two words to memorise. Naming the ending inside them turns a closed pair into an open rule: any noun this book has taught you can now own something.
That is the difference between a word and a piece of grammar, and it is worth looking for: when two words you were given separately end the same way, the ending is usually doing the work.
