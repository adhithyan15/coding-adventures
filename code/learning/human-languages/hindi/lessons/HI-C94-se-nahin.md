# nahīṁ kī jagah — how much the negative takes back

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[HI-LEX-C90-ROUT-02, HI-LEX-C94-SPAN-04] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say *by bus* and *by train*. Now imagine somebody has guessed the
first one and you need to give them the second.

## You'll want to know: X से नहीं, Y से
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[HI-LEX-C94-SPAN-05]; assesses=[] -->

**बस से नहीं, ट्रेन से।** — Not by bus — by train.

**नहीं** stands between the two options, and the one it rejects is the one
**before** it.

## The two words
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[HI-LEX-C94-SPAN-05, HI-CONCEPT-C07-NAHIN-01] -->

Read the frame as three beats:

| beat | contents |
|---|---|
| 1 | the wrong option, with its own **से** |
| 2 | **नहीं** |
| 3 | the right option, with its own **से** |

**Each option keeps its marker.** That is the part to watch, because English
drops the second one — *not by bus, by train* has one *by*, and Hindi has two
**से**. A sentence with only one **से** is not this frame.

## Grammar Lens: what नहीं is negating
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[HI-LEX-C94-SPAN-05, HI-CONCEPT-C07-NAHIN-01] -->

In every sentence so far, **नहीं** has stood in front of the verb and denied
the whole thing. Here it stands after a noun-plus-postposition and denies **only
that phrase**.

| what is denied | example |
|---|---|
| the whole sentence | मैं नहीं जाता |
| one option in it | बस से नहीं, ट्रेन से |

So the position of **नहीं** tells a reader how much of the sentence is being
taken back — and getting it wrong turns a correction into a refusal. The English
speaker's instinct is to reach for a word like *but*; **this frame has no word
in the middle beyond the negative itself**, and the comma carries the pause.
