# naulum, naulī — the fare

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[LA-LEX-C286-NAVIS-01, LA-LEX-C156-ACT156-05] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say "I go on board the ship". (*In nāvem ascendō.*)

[PAUSE 1s] Romans had no tickets. A passenger found a ship going his way
and paid its master a fare.

## You'll want to know: naulum, naulī
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[LA-LEX-C286-NAULUM-01]; assesses=[LA-LEX-C164-LEG164-03, LA-LEX-C190-RES190-04] -->

**naulum, naulī** — "a fare, passage money". It is neuter, like *pretium*,
"a price", which is the word for what it costs.

> **Naulum solvō.** — I pay the fare.
> **Decem dēnāriōs solvī.** — I paid ten denarii.

[PAUSE 2s] *Solvō* does both jobs of this chapter: it unties a ship from the
shore, and it pays what you owe.

[PAUSE 1s] The man you pay is the **magister nāvis**, "the master of the
ship": the same *magister* as a teacher, the one in charge.

## The word, taken apart: naulum
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[LA-LEX-C53-SCHOOL-02] -->

**naulum** was borrowed from Greek *naulon*, "passage money" (Lewis &
Short), a word usually connected with *naus*, "ship", the Greek cousin of
*nāvis*. The satirist Juvenal uses it, and so do the Roman law books, for
the money owed to a ship; the law books also name the *magister nāvis*,
the ship's master (Lewis & Short).
