Latin, chapter 284: What You Should Do, and Why.
3 lessons. All 3 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 3.
dēbēs — what you should do.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
You own dēbeō, "I have to, I must; I owe", and quiēscō, "to rest". Say "I have to work". (Labōrāre dēbeō.)
[pause 1 second]
Turn the same verb on a friend, and it becomes advice.

Grammar Lens: dēbēs + infinitive.
Aeger es; quiēscere dēbēs. — You are ill; you should rest. Nōn dēbēs tantum labōrāre. — You shouldn't work so much.
[pause 2 seconds]
Dēbēs is the second person, "you must", and Latin uses it for what English softens into "you should". The infinitive carries the advice, and nōn in front turns it round.
[pause 1 second]
oportet, "it is right, one ought", says the same thing without pointing at anyone, and it takes the accusative and infinitive you met with putō:
Oportet tē quiēscere. — You ought to rest.

Grammar Lens: dēbēbās, you should have.
English moves the past onto the infinitive: "you should have come". Latin moves it onto dēbeō instead, and keeps the plain infinitive:
Herī venīre dēbēbās. — You should have come yesterday.
[pause 2 seconds]
Dēbēbās is the imperfect, "you were bound to", and the sentence carries the regret: the coming was owed, and it did not happen.

The word, taken apart: dēbeō.
dēbeō is contracted from dē, "away from", and habeō, "to have" (Lewis & Short), as its own lesson said: to have something that belongs to someone else. A debt, dēbitum, is what you hold and must give back; advice with dēbēs treats the right action as something owed.

Lesson 2 of 3.
cūr? — quia, quod, nam: explaining.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Ask "why?". (Cūr?) Answer with "because". (Quia.)
[pause 1 second]
You also own explicō, "to explain", and causa, "a reason".

Grammar Lens: the answer to cūr.
Cūr herī nōn vēnistī? — Why didn't you come yesterday? Nōn vēnī, quia aeger eram. — I didn't come, because I was ill.
[pause 2 seconds]
Both pasts: the not-coming is an event, nōn vēnī; the illness is the scene behind it, aeger eram. After quia the verb is an ordinary one, in whatever tense the facts need.
[pause 2 seconds]
nam, "for", hangs the reason on afterwards, as a second sentence: Nōn vēnī. Nam aeger eram. And explicō names what you are doing: Causam explicō, I am explaining the reason.

You'll want to know: quod, because.
The four lines you wrote with joiners asked you for quia, because quod has two jobs. Now you meet its second one as a reader:
Gaudeō quod vēnistī. — I am glad that you came, glad because you came.
[pause 2 seconds]
After a verb of feeling, gaudeō, doleō, quod gives the cause. Where a noun stands before it and quod points back to it, it is usually "which"; where no noun waits for it, it is usually "because" or "that". Writing, quia is still the safe choice; reading, expect both.

The word, taken apart: explicō.
explicō is ex, "out", with plicō, "to fold" (EtymDB 2.1), as its own lesson said: to explain is to unfold. A Roman book was a volūmen, a roll, and Cicero writes volūmen explicāre for opening one (Lewis & Short). An explanation opens out what was rolled up inside the question.

Lesson 3 of 3.
Review — what you should do, and why.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say "you should rest". (Quiēscere dēbēs.) Then "because you are tired". (Quia fessus es.)

What you've built.
Two chapters, and you can give reasons. An opinion: putō with the accusative and infinitive, meā sententiā, mihi vidētur. Advice: dēbēs and an infinitive, and dēbēbās for what should have happened. A reason: quia, nam, and the quod that follows a feeling. A conclusion: ergō.
Meā sententiā, quiēscere dēbēs, quia aeger es. Herī labōrāre nōn dēbēbās. — In my opinion you should rest, because you are ill. You shouldn't have worked yesterday.
[pause 2 seconds]
Four pieces in two sentences: the opinion, the advice, the reason, and the regret.
