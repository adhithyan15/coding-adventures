# ita / nōn — "yes" and "no"

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[LA-PHONO-GRATIAS-01, LA-ETYMON-GRATIAS-02, LA-GRAMMAR-GRATIAS-03] -->

[PAUSE 2s] A surprise: Latin had no plain word for "yes." Here is what Romans
said instead.

## Sounds you'll need
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[LA-PHONO-ITA-NON-01]; assesses=[] -->

*Ita* = **IH-tah**; *nōn* = **nōne**, with a long ō.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[LA-ETYMON-ITA-NON-02]; assesses=[] -->

Classical Latin had **no simple "yes."** To agree you said *ita* ("thus, so"),
*sīc* ("so, in this way"), *certē* ("certainly"), or you just **repeated the
verb** — asked "are you coming?", you answered "I'm coming." It was *sīc* that
hardened into the Romance "yes": Spanish and Italian **sí / sì**, French *si*
(the emphatic yes). "No" is easier: *nōn* ("not"), from older *ne-oenum* "not one
thing" — the ancient negative **ne-** that also gives English **no, not, none,
never**, and which you will meet again as Sanskrit *na* and Hindi *nahīṇ*.

## Why it's said this way
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[LA-LEX-ITA-NON-03]; assesses=[] -->

A language that carries so much in its verb endings barely needs a "yes" word —
the verb *is* the answer. This is a real window into how Latin thinks: agreement
lives in the sentence, not in a little particle bolted on.
