# cortar — and what a cut road means

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C365-COUNT-24, ES-LEX-C294-HOME-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say **corto** and **el cuchillo**. One is a shape, one is a tool, and
the same idea is inside both. Name the verb.

## You'll want to know: cortar
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-C439-VIA-04]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C294-HOME-01] -->

**cortar** — to cut.

*Corta el pan* — cut the bread. *Me he cortado* — I have cut myself. *Se ha
cortado la llamada* — the call has dropped, which Spanish sees as a line cut
rather than a call lost.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C439-VIA-04, ES-LEX-C365-COUNT-24] -->

**corto** and **cortar** are the same Latin word, one taken as a description and
one as an action. *Curtus* meant shortened, cut off.

| word | the same idea as |
|---|---|
| corto | the result of the cut |
| cortar | the act of cutting |

English keeps *curtus* in **curt**, a reply cut short, and in **curtail**. The
scissors in a barber's window and the adjective for a short skirt are relatives.

## Why it's said this way
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C439-VIA-04, ES-LEX-C439-VIA-03] -->

Now the meaning that catches travellers out. On a road or a rail line,
**cortado** does not mean damaged. It means **closed**.

> **VÍA CORTADA POR OBRAS**

A **vía cortada** is a line out of service. A **calle cortada** is a street shut
to traffic, often for a market or a race, with nothing wrong with it at all.

Set it beside **cerrado**, which you met a moment ago, because the two are not
interchangeable. A shop is **cerrado**: someone shut it and will open it again.
A road is **cortada**: the through-route has been severed, and you must go round.
Spanish picks the verb by *how* the way is blocked, and a sign that says
*cortada* is telling you there is no waiting for it to reopen today.
