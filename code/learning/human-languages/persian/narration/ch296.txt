Persian, chapter 296: Glad, Tired, Hopeful.
5 lessons. You can do the first 2 of them in the car; after that you will want to have stopped.


Lesson 1 of 5.
خوشحال بودم (khoshhâl budam) — I was happy, and I became happy.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say "happy", "tired", "upset". (Khoshhâl, khaste, nârâhat.) Then "to become". (Shodan.)
[pause 1 second]
You can say what happened on a trip. Describing it means saying how it felt, and Persian gives a feeling two pasts as well.

Grammar Lens: the feeling as scene.
The adjective, then budan in the past:
خوشحال بودم. — khoshhâl budam — I was happy.
Khaste budam, I was tired; nârâhat budim, we were upset; shâd bud, she was cheerful. The adjective stays the same for every person, and the ending rides on bud-. This is the feeling as background, there for a while.

Grammar Lens: the feeling as event.
Swap budan for shodan, "to become", and the feeling arrives at a moment:
خسته شدم. — khaste shodam — I got tired.
وقتی قطار را از دست دادم، ناراحت شدم. — vaqti qatâr râ az dast dâdam, nârâhat shodam — When I missed the train, I got upset.
The cause can come with az, "from", and the infinitive works as a noun:
از دیدن شما خوشحال شدم. — az didan-e shomâ khoshhâl shodam — I was glad to see you.
Word for word, "from the seeing of you I became happy". It is a thing people say at the end of a first meeting.
So khoshhâl budam is the mood of the whole day, and khoshhâl shodam is the moment the news arrived.

The word, taken apart.
Shodan is usually traced to Middle Persian šudan, "to go". Becoming is going into a state: khaste shodam is "I went tired". Khoshhâl is khosh, "good", with hâl, "state", the same hâl as in hâl-e shomâ chetor ast?

Lesson 2 of 5.
غذا (ghazâ) — food, and how it was.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say "I was happy", then "I got tired". (Khoshhâl budam, khaste shodam.) Then "delicious". (Khoshmaze.)
[pause 1 second]
Talk about a trip, and sooner or later the food comes up.

You'll want to know: غذا.
غذا — ghazâ — "food", and "a meal".
غذای این رستوران خیلی خوشمزه بود. — ghazâ-ye in resturân kheyli khoshmaze bud — The food at this restaurant was very tasty.
After its long â the ezafe is -ye, and here it is written, as ی: غذای. Ghazâ khordan is "to eat, to have a meal": ghazâ khordim, we ate.
Say gha-zâ, with the gh far back, like a soft gargle. Its first letter is new, and the next lesson puts it in your hand. The second is ذ, which you wrote in chapter two hundred and ninety.

The word, taken apart.
ghazâ is borrowed from Arabic ghidhāʾ, "nourishment, food" (EtymDB 2.1). Arabic says its middle letter as the th of English this. Persian kept the letter ذ in the spelling and says it z, which is why ghazâ sounds nothing like the Arabic it is spelled from.

Script you'll notice: غ.
غ — gheyn, the sound gh, made far back, like a soft gargle.
It opens غذا (ghazâ), in its opening shape, غـ, joined to ذ. It is ع with one dot above, and it changes shape the same way: an open hook at the start, a small closed loop in the middle, a tail at the end.
In Tehran speech غ (gheyn) and ق sound alike: ghazâ and qatâr open on the same sound. Spelling keeps them apart, and so does this book's romanization: ق is written q, as in qatâr, and غ (gheyn) is written gh, as in ghazâ. Each word has to be learned with its own letter.

Writing: غ.
Put your pen on غ (gheyn) and follow its line. Copy the shape you can see — slowly, and larger than it is printed.
This book does not yet tell you where to start this letter or which way to travel. It is not written down here until it can be written down with a source. Copying what is in front of you needs no such source, and it is how the shape gets into your hand in the meantime.

Lesson 4 of 5.
امیدوارم که (omidvâram ke) — I hope that, I'd like to, I dream of.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say "to hope", then "I want to go". (Omidvâr budan, mikhâham beravam.) Then "I'm well", in one word. (Khubam.)
[pause 1 second]
Describing an experience runs forward as well as back: what you hope for next, and what you would like to become.

Grammar Lens: omidvâram ke, and the subjunctive.
Omidvâr takes the -am of khubam: امیدوارم, omidvâram, "I am hopeful", "I hope". What you hope for follows ke in the subjunctive, because it has not happened:
امیدوارم که سال دیگر به تهران بروم. — omidvâram ke sâl-e digar be Tehrân beravam — I hope to go to Tehran next year.
Chapter one hundred and thirty-eight showed omidvâram hâlat khub bâshad, "I hope you are well". The bâshad there is this subjunctive, and the ke has been left out, as speech often does.

Grammar Lens: what you'd like, and what you dream of.
Dust dâram, "I like", with a subjunctive after it is "I'd like to":
دوست دارم مهندس بشوم. — dust dâram mohandes beshavam — I'd like to become an engineer.
Beshavam is shodan in the subjunctive, on its stem shav-; in speech it is shortened to besham. Ârezu, "a wish, a dream", works the same way with dâram:
آرزو دارم که یک روز خلبان بشوم. — ârezu dâram ke yek ruz khalabân beshavam — My dream is to become a pilot one day.
So three strengths: mikhâham, I want; dust dâram, I'd like; ârezu dâram, I dream of. All three take the subjunctive.

Lesson 5 of 5.
Review — how it felt, and what you hope for.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say "I was tired", then "I got tired". (Khaste budam, khaste shodam.)

What you've built.
Three lessons, and an experience now has its colour. Budam gives the feeling of a whole stretch; shodam gives the moment it arrived. Ghazâ brings in the meal, and omidvâram ke, dust dâram and ârezu dâram carry the hope forward, each with the subjunctive after it.
[pause 2 seconds]
Back to the bad morning at the station, told as an experience:
qatâr râ az dast dâdam va nârâhat shodam. ba'd mas'ul-e istgâh bilit-e fardâ râ be man dâd, barâ-ye hamin khoshhâl shodam. ghazâ-ye istgâh khoshmaze bud. omidvâram ke fardâ dir naresam.
The last verb, naresam, is the subjunctive with na-: "that I don't arrive".
