## You'll want to know: el dormitorio
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-C455-CUARTO-02]; assesses=[ES-LEX-DORMIR-04] -->

**el dormitorio** — the bedroom.

*Un piso de dos dormitorios* — a two-bedroom flat, the wording in every
advert. *El dormitorio da al patio* — the bedroom looks onto the courtyard.

**La habitación** also covers it in speech — *mi habitación* is what a child
says — but a property listing counts *dormitorios*, because *habitación* means
any room at all.

## Grammar Lens: the vowel that has been breaking all along
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C455-CUARTO-02, ES-GRAMMAR-STEM-O-UE-06] -->

You met **duermo** early on, as a verb that changes its stem. It is the same
single rule you have now seen in a noun, an adjective and a conjugation:

| plain o | stressed, so ue |
|---|---|
| d**o**rmir, d**o**rmitorio | d**ue**rmo |
| r**o**dar | la r**ue**da |
| m**o**strar | m**ue**stro |

**Dormitorio** keeps the plain **o** because the stress lands on the **-to-**,
three syllables along. Say *dormitorio* and *duermo* one after the other and
you can hear the vowel change as the stress moves.

Nothing here is irregular. One vowel, one condition, and it has been running
under the language since the first weeks of it.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C455-CUARTO-02, ES-LEX-C455-CUARTO-01] -->

Latin **dormitorium**, a sleeping place — from **dormire**, to sleep.

**-orio** names the place where something is done, and once you have it you
can read a lot of signs:

| word | the place for |
|---|---|
| el dormit**orio** | sleeping |
| el laborat**orio** | working |
| el velat**orio** | keeping watch over the dead |

English took *dormitorium* whole as **dormitory**, and shortened it to *dorm*.
It also has **dormant**, lying asleep, and the **dormouse**, named for how much
of the year it spends doing exactly that.
