## You'll want to know: la hierba
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-C450-SUELO-02]; assesses=[ES-LEX-PLANTA] -->

**la hierba** — grass, and more widely any low soft plant: a herb, a weed.

*Hay hierba entre las piedras* — there is grass growing between the stones.
*Las malas hierbas* — the weeds, literally the bad grasses. *Una infusión de
hierbas* — a herbal tea.

You will also see it written **yerba**, mostly in the Americas, and *yerba
mate* is the usual spelling for the drink. Both are the same word said the
same way.

## Grammar Lens: the h that is not pronounced and not decorative
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C450-SUELO-02, ES-LEX-VERDE-01] -->

Latin *herba* had an h that had already gone silent by Roman times. Spanish
kept the letter and added it back where Latin had none:

| Latin | Spanish | the h |
|---|---|---|
| herba | la **h**ierba | inherited |
| ovum | el **h**uevo | added |
| ossum | el **h**ueso | added |

Look at what the added ones have in common: they all begin **ue**. Scribes put
an *h* in front because a word starting with *u* was read as a *v*, and
*uevo* would have been read *vevo*. The *h* is a fence around the vowel.

*Hierba* begins *ie* rather than *ue*, so it did not need the fence. It
kept what Latin had already handed it. The letter is silent in all of them.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C450-SUELO-02] -->

Latin **herba**, grass or herb. English borrowed the same word twice: once
through French as **herb**, and once as the botanical **herbaceous**. Spanish
has the learned form too, in *herbicida* and *herbívoro*, where the h sits in
front of an unbroken *e* because those words came from books rather than from
speech.

That is the tell. *Hierba* has the broken vowel because ordinary speakers wore
it down over centuries; *herbívoro* has not, because it was lifted from Latin
whole and handed to a scientist.
