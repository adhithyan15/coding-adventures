# morgen — "tomorrow," the same word doing a second job

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GE-LEX-MORGEN-02, GE-ETYMON-MORGEN-03] -->

[PAUSE 2s] No new word this time — a word you have had since chapter one, doing
something you have not seen it do.

## You'll want to know: morgen
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[GE-LEX-MORGEN-TOMORROW-01]; assesses=[GE-LEX-MORGEN-02] -->

> **morgen** — "tomorrow."

Same spelling as the *Morgen* of *Guten Morgen*, same sound: *MOR-gen*, with the
German **r** made at the back of the throat.

## Grammar Lens: the capital letter splits the sense
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[GE-GRAMMAR-CAPITAL-SPLITS-SENSE-02]; assesses=[GE-LEX-MORGEN-TOMORROW-01, GE-LEX-DER-DIE-DAS-02] -->

German capitalises every noun. That rule usually just makes text look busy —
here it does real work:

| Written | Part of speech | Means |
|---|---|---|
| *der* **M**orgen | noun | the morning |
| **m**orgen | adverb | tomorrow |

*Guten Morgen!* is "good **morning**." *Bis morgen* is "until **tomorrow**."
The capital is the only thing telling them apart on the page — and once you know
that, German's noun-capitalisation stops being decoration and starts being
punctuation you can read.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GE-LEX-MORGEN-TOMORROW-01, GE-ETYMON-MORGEN-03] -->

This morning-becomes-tomorrow move is not a German quirk. All three languages
make "tomorrow" out of "morning":

| Language | Morning | Tomorrow |
|---|---|---|
| German | *Morgen* | *morgen* |
| Spanish | *mañana* | *mañana* |
| French | *matin* | *demain* |

And English did it too, then half forgot: **morrow** meant *morning*, and
**tomorrow** is literally *to (the) morrow*. So *bis morgen* is "see you on the
morrow," almost word for word.
