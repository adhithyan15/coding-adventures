# iter, itineris — a journey

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[LA-GRAMMAR-C285-DIRECTIO-01, LA-LEX-EO-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say "go straight on". (*Rēctā viā ī.*) Then "we go". (*Īmus.*)

## You'll want to know: iter, itineris
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[LA-LEX-C285-ITER-01]; assesses=[LA-LEX-C285-VIA-01, LA-LEX-C93-MAGNUS-03] -->

**iter, itineris** — "a journey; a route, a way". It is neuter, and the
genitive shows the stem every other form is built on: **itiner-**.

> **Iter longum erat.** — The journey was long.
> **In itinere** — on the way, on the journey

[PAUSE 1s] *Via* is the road under your feet; *iter* is the going along
it, from the start to the arrival.

## You'll want to know: iter faciō
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[LA-LEX-C285-ITER-FACIO-02]; assesses=[LA-GRAMMAR-C281-PERFECTUM-01, LA-GRAMMAR-C281-ERAM-01, LA-LEX-C155-ACT155-01, LA-GRAMMAR-C59-PLACE-03] -->

To travel, a Roman **made a journey**: **iter faciō**. Caesar writes it of
whole armies marching through a province.

> **Rōmam iter faciō.** — I am travelling to Rome.
> **Herī iter fēcī.** — Yesterday I made a journey.

*Rōmam* needs no *ad*: a city's name points the way by itself, as *domum*
does. *Fēcī* is the perfect of *faciō*, a stem learned on its own.

## The word, taken apart: iter
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[LA-ETYMON-EO-02] -->

**iter** is built on *īre*, "to go" (Lewis & Short), the one-vowel verb
whose lesson traced it to Proto-Indo-European *\*h₁ey-*: a journey is a
going. Its stem *itiner-* gives English *itinerary*, a plan of the route,
and *itinerant*, someone who lives on the road.
