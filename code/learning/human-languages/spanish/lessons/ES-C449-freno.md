## You'll want to know: el freno
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-C449-TALLER-02]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C363-WHERE-45] -->

**el freno** — the brake.

*Los frenos no van* — the brakes aren't working. *El freno de mano* — the
handbrake. *Frenar* — to brake: *frena antes de la curva*, brake before the
bend.

Spanish uses the plural far more than English does. A bicycle has *frenos*,
and asking about *el freno* on a bike would sound as though you knew which one
was at fault.

## Grammar Lens: the noun and the verb in one step
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C449-TALLER-02, ES-LEX-C449-TALLER-01] -->

**el freno** gives **frenar** by adding nothing but *-ar*, and this pair is
one instance of a pattern you can lean on:

| noun | verb from it |
|---|---|
| el freno | fren**ar** — to brake |
| la planta | plant**ar** — to plant |
| el arreglo | arregl**ar** — to fix |

Note the third row runs backwards from the other two: **el arreglo** is the
noun built from the verb, not the verb built from the noun. Spanish makes
these in both directions and the ending alone will not tell you which came
first — a question for a dictionary, not for a rule.

What you can rely on is the shape: meeting a noun, try *-ar* on it; meeting
an *-ar* verb, try cutting it back to a noun. You will be right often enough
to make it worth the guess.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C449-TALLER-02] -->

Latin **frenum**, the bit — the metal bar in a horse's mouth that the reins
pull on. That is the oldest brake there is, and the word moved onto carts,
then bicycles, then cars, without ever changing its job.

English has the same word in the medical **frenulum**, the small fold of
tissue under your tongue, so called because it holds the tongue back. Spanish
keeps the sense alive too: *poner freno a algo* — to put a stop to something.
