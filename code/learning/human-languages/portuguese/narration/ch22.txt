Portuguese, chapter 22: Answering, Meeting, and Three Ways to Play.
4 lessons. All 4 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 4.
responder — answering, and a promise given back.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
A chapter of verbs for dealing with other people opens with the one this book has been missing. You have been able to ask since the last chapter. Now you can answer.

You'll want to know: A plain -er verb.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
eu respondo, nós respondemos.
tu respondes, vocês/eles respondem.
ele/ela/você responde, blank.
Responder is regular all the way through — an ordinary -er verb, the same shape as the writing and reading verbs you already conjugate. It pairs with perguntar exactly as English "answer" pairs with "ask":
Pergunto e tu respondes. — "I ask and you answer." Respondo que compro o pão. — "I answer that I am buying the bread."
The most everyday answer of all is one you have had since the third chapter. Asked Como se chama?, you respond: Chamo-me…, and then muito prazer.

The word, taken apart: pledging it back.
responder from Latin respondēre: re-, "back," plus spondēre, "to pledge, to promise solemnly." A Roman answer was not information handed over — it was a promise returned. Somebody pledged, and you pledged back.
That old weight is still on the English side of the family. Respond and response are the plain ones, but a responsible person is one who can be called on to answer, and to be despondent is to have given the pledge away. A sponsor is literally one who pledges for you — and a spouse is spōnsus, "the one who has been promised."
Set that against the verb it answers. Perguntar came from percontārī, from contus, a boatman's pole: to ask was to sound the depth of the water in front of you. So Portuguese probes with a pole and pledges back with a promise.

[pause 3 seconds]

("To pledge" — spouse, spōnsus.) Now answer Como se chama? aloud. (Chamo-me…, then muito prazer.) What was the pole in perguntar? (Contus — asking as sounding the depth.) And is comprar related to compare? (No — comprar comes from parāre, "to make ready.")


Lesson 2 of 4.
encontrar — to find.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Today encontrar has one job: “to find.”

You'll want to know: The present tense.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
eu encontro, nós encontramos.
tu encontras, vocês/eles encontram.
ele/ela/você encontra, blank.
Encontro o livro. — I find the book. Encontramos o café. — We find the café.
Keep the object directly after the verb, as in English.

The word, taken apart.
Encontrar grew from in plus Latin contrā, “facing” or “opposite.” To find something is to come face to face with it. English encounter belongs to the same family.

Lesson 3 of 4.
encontrar-se — to meet up.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
You know encontrar, “to find.” With people, it can also describe meeting up.

You'll want to know: Make one plan.
Encontramo-nos no sábado ao meio-dia. — We are meeting on Saturday at midday.
The little nos means “each other.” In much of Portugal it commonly attaches after the verb as shown. In Brazil, nós nos encontramos is more usual.
For a first introduction, keep using the conhecer family you already know: Conheci o Pedro, “I met Pedro for the first time.” Today’s encontrar-se is for meeting up.

Lesson 4 of 4.
jogar / brincar / tocar — the three ways to play.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
English plays chess, plays in the garden and plays the piano with one verb. Portuguese has three, and will not let you swap them.

You'll want to know: Which play is which.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
jogar — a game: jogo, jogas, joga. brincar — a child, a joke: brinco, brincas, brinca. tocar — an instrument: toco, tocas, toca.
jogar — a game: Jogo futebol. brincar — a child, a joke: O meu irmão brinca. tocar — an instrument: Toco piano.
All three are ordinary -ar verbs, endings exactly as in falar, morar and trabalhar. The work is choosing.
Jogar is a game with rules and a winner. Brincar is free play — a child with a toy, and from there joking, so brincar com someone is to tease them. Tocar is an instrument. Brincar is the one English cannot match: "play" is too wide, "mess about" too dismissive.

Grammar Lens: Two coasts, one preposition apart.
Portugal commonly puts an a after jogar: jogar à bola, jogar às cartas. Brazil, and northern Portugal, take the thing straight: jogar bola.
Tocar also keeps its plainer meaning, to touch. You touch with a mão — and a piano wants as duas mãos, duas not dois, because mão is feminine.

The word, taken apart: a joke, a blow, and an honest gap.
jogar from Latin iocārī, "to jest," from iocus, "a joke" becomes English joke, jocular, and juggler, a Latin ioculātor or jester. Portuguese plays its games with the Latin word for a gag.
tocar from Vulgar Latin toccāre, "to knock, to strike," most likely an imitation of the sound. Old French carried it into English as touch — a blow at its lightest is a touch. The striking sense stayed with music: a toccata, a tocsin or alarm bell. You tocar an instrument because you strike it.
brincar is the honest gap. The leading account runs through brinco, today "an earring," from Latin vinculum, "a bond" — a bound ornament, then a trinket, then playing with trinkets. A Germanic word for gleaming is the rival, and neither is settled. Vinculum survives plainly as vínculo, "a bond."
