Spanish, chapter 432: La Casa Por Dentro.
7 lessons. All 7 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 7.
la lavadora — the thing that washes.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say lavar. Say el proveedor. You met the doer-ending one chapter ago. Put it on lavar and guess before you are told.

You'll want to know: la lavadora.
la lavadora — the washing machine.
If you guessed it, that is the point of the last chapter arriving before this one.

The word, taken apart.
lavar plus -dora. The same ending as el proveedor, in its feminine form, and it does the same job — except that here the doer is a machine rather than a person.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
verb: lavar. the doer: la lavadora.
verb: secar. the doer: la secadora.
verb: calcular. the doer: la calculadora.
Spanish names most household machines this way, so -dora is worth more to you than any single appliance. A machine that does something is usually that something plus this ending, and you can often reach the word without having met it.

Lesson 2 of 7.
el contador — the thing that counts.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say contar. Say la lavadora. Do the same move again: the ending on the verb, and say what the machine must be.

You'll want to know: el contador.
el contador — the meter. The box that counts your water, your gas, your electricity.
contar carries two senses you already hold — to count, and to tell a story. The meter takes the counting one.

The word, taken apart.
Twice in two lessons the same ending has handed you a word. That is worth pausing on, because it is the difference between a vocabulary you memorise and one you can extend.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
you know: lavar. you can reach: la lavadora.
you know: contar. you can reach: el contador.
el contador del agua is the phrase you will actually meet, on notices about somebody coming to read it. Note the gender: this one is masculine where the washing machine was feminine, and nothing about the ending decides that — the machine takes whatever gender its own word already had.

Lesson 3 of 7.
la estantería — a place full of shelves.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say el contador. That ending has now served you twice. Here is a different one, and it does a different job.

You'll want to know: la estantería.
la estantería — the shelving, the bookcase, the shelf unit.
A single shelf is el estante. The -ería makes it the whole structure.

The word, taken apart.
-ería does not make a doer. It makes a place or collection of the thing:
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
the thing: el estante. the place or collection: la estantería.
the thing: el pan. the place or collection: la panadería.
the thing: el libro. the place or collection: la librería.
That is why so many shop names end this way: a panadería is where the bread is, a librería where the books are. Meeting the ending once tells you what a shop sells before you have read the sign properly.
Two endings, two different jobs: -dor gives you who or what does it, -ería gives you where it is kept.

Lesson 4 of 7.
la tubería — all the pipes together.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say la estantería. Say el agua. A tubo is a pipe. Say what a tubería must be before reading on.

You'll want to know: la tubería.
la tubería — the pipework, the plumbing, the run of pipes.
el tubo is one pipe. The ending collects them, exactly as it collected the shelves.

The word, taken apart.
Third time for an ending in two chapters, and the second time for this one:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
one: el estante. the collection: la estantería.
one: el tubo. the collection: la tubería.
This is the word in the notice that says the water is being cut off: reparar una tubería. The repair is to the run of pipes, not to one length of tube, and the word chooses itself accordingly.
If you are keeping count, you have now reached four words in this chapter and three of them you could have guessed from a piece you already had.

Lesson 5 of 7.
el sofá — the one you could not guess.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say la silla. You can name one seat. Name the long soft one that three people share.

You'll want to know: el sofá.
el sofá — the sofa.
Stressed on the last syllable, so-FÁ, and the written accent is there to say so.

The word, taken apart.
This one was not built from anything you hold. Four words in this chapter came apart into a piece plus an ending; this one did not, and pretending otherwise would waste your time.
It came into Spanish from Arabic, where the word named a raised, cushioned bench along a wall. Spanish took hundreds of words from Arabic over several centuries, and they tend to arrive whole like this rather than as pieces you can recombine.
So the honest lesson of the chapter is two-sided:
[a table of 1 row, read aloud]
when a word comes apart: look for the ending first. when it does not: learn it whole, and move on.
Knowing which kind you are looking at is the skill. Trying to decompose sofá would cost you minutes and teach you nothing.

Lesson 6 of 7.
(lavadora, contador, estantería, tubería, sofá) — from cold.

Warm-up.
[pause 3 seconds]
Close the lessons before this one. Say the washing machine, the meter and the sofa.

Grammar Lens: two endings and one exception.
[a table of 5 rows, read aloud]
word: la lavadora. built from: lavar + -dora.
word: el contador. built from: contar + -dor.
word: la estantería. built from: estante + -ería.
word: la tubería. built from: tubo + -ería.
word: el sofá. built from: nothing — borrowed whole.
Four of five came apart. Do not read a rule into it — the next five household words might all be whole. What this chapter is for is the question, asked before you reach for a dictionary: is there a piece in here I already have?
The two endings answer different questions. -dor and -dora name the one that does it. -ería names where the thing is kept or collected.

What you've built.
You could name a house and a chair, and almost nothing inside either. La casa, la silla and el agua have been yours for a long time. What was missing was everything a notice on a door actually talks about: the meter somebody comes to read, the pipework they are repairing, the machine that broke.
You now hold the vocabulary of the building, not only of the home.
