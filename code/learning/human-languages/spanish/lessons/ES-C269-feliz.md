# feliz — happy, and an adjective that refuses to pick a gender

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[] -->

[PAUSE 2s] You can say where things are and what has gone wrong. This chapter is
about saying how it *felt*. It starts with the most useful feeling word, which
also happens to be conveniently lazy.

## You'll want to know first
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[] -->

- [problema](./ES-C268-problema.md) — and the gender surprise it carried.

## Sounds you'll need
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[] -->

- Two syllables, stress on the last: *fe-LIZ*. No written accent is needed —
  words ending in *-z* are stressed on the final syllable by default. →
  [reference](../pronunciation-reference.md)

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-FELIZ]; assesses=[] -->

**feliz** — "happy." Latin *felix*, which first meant **fruitful** — of soil that
bore a crop, of a tree heavy with fruit. Happiness, to a Roman farmer, was the
field doing what it was supposed to do. The same *felix* is the name **Felix**,
and it is inside English **felicity**.

You already meet it once a year without translating it: *¡feliz cumpleaños!*

## What you've built
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-GRAMMAR-ADJECTIVE-NO-GENDER-Z]; assesses=[] -->

*Feliz* does something the adjectives you have met do not. *Bueno* becomes
*buena* for a feminine noun; *cansado* becomes *cansada*. *Feliz* does neither —
it is *feliz* for everyone. Adjectives ending in a consonant simply do not carry
gender, so *un niño feliz* and *una niña feliz* use the identical word.

That is a saving, not an exception: every adjective you meet ending in *-z*,
*-l* or *-e* is one fewer form to remember.
