Lesson 1 of 5.
stressado — stressed.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Portuguese for the rise, then the Portuguese for the qualification.

You'll want to know: stressado.
stressado — "stressed".
Ando muito stressado com os exames. I have been very stressed about the exams.
European spelling; in Brazil estressado. A woman says stressada.

The word, taken apart.
stressado is built on stress, borrowed from English stress, which EtymDB traces to Middle English destresse "distress" (EtymDB); that word is usually traced through Old French to Latin strictus, the participle of stringere "to draw tight" (Lewis & Short).

Lesson 2 of 5.
esgotado — worn out, exhausted; sold out.

Warm-up.

You'll want to know: esgotado.
esgotado — "worn out, exhausted; sold out".
Depois de doze horas de trabalho, estou esgotado. After twelve hours of work, I am worn out.
Also sold out: os bilhetes estão esgotados.

The word, taken apart.
esgotado is the participle of esgotar "to drain to the last drop", built from es- and gota "drop", inherited from Latin gutta "a drop of a fluid" (Lewis & Short).

Lesson 3 of 5.
as horas extraordinárias — the overtime.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Portuguese for stressed, then the Portuguese for worn out.

You'll want to know: as horas extraordinárias.
as horas extraordinárias — "the overtime".
Fiz muitas horas extraordinárias este mês. I did a lot of overtime this month.
In speech as horas extra; in Brazil as horas extras.

The word, taken apart.
hora is inherited from Latin hōra "hour", and extraordinário is a learned borrowing from Latin extraordinārius "out of the common order" (Lewis & Short), on ōrdō "order".

The word, taken apart.
eficiente is a learned borrowing from Latin efficiēns (EtymDB), the present participle of efficere "to bring about", built on facere "to make" (Lewis & Short).
