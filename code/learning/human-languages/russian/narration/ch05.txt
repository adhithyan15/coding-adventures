Russian, chapter 5: Taking, Asking, Helping, Loving.
4 lessons. All 4 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 4.
брать (brat') — "to take".

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
A short verb, an English cousin you carry every day, and a partner that shares not one letter with it.

You'll want to know first — брать.
брать — brat' — to take.
Five letters, all of them yours, and the familiar -ть.
ya berú — I take.
The stem grows a vowel: бр- in the infinitive, бер- once an ending arrives. Then ty beryósh, so this verb sits in the znat' family, and that -u is the plain form of the -ю you have been saying.
Three sentences, all ordinary Russian:
Ya chitáyu. Ya pishú. Ya berú.

Grammar Lens: a pair with nothing in common.
The partners so far were recognisable: chitát', prochitát' added a prefix, ponimát', понять reshaped a middle. Now the hard case.
Брать's finished partner is vzyat' — ya vaz'mú. Not a prefixed брать (brat'), not a squeezed one: a different word entirely, drafted in as half of the pair.
Grammarians call this suppletion, and you have met it once: идти takes shol for its past, from another root, exactly as English go takes went. Here the same trick builds an aspect pair instead of a past tense.
There is a quiet reward in it. Vzyat' is vz- on the very root that built ponimát', the ancient root meaning take. So брать (brat')'s finished partner is family with the verb for understand, and брать (brat') is the outsider.

The word, taken apart — taking is carrying.
брать (brat') is Indo-European bʰer-, "to carry, to bear", one of the richest roots English owns: bear, birth, burden, bier; Latin ferre behind transfer, refer, fertile; Greek phérein behind metaphor, a carrying-across. Russian keeps the load too — brémya is a burden, and berémennaya, pregnant, is literally carrying.
Now the trap, because this track flags both kinds. Брат means brother, sits one soft sign from брать (brat'), and is not related: брат is bʰréh₂ter, English brother. And byt' is bʰuH-, English be. Three roots that all begin bʰ — a family accent, not a shared word.

(Vzyat', a different root; go and went, as идти takes shol.) Which verb of yours is vzyat' related to? (Ponimát' — both on the old root for take.) And is брать (brat') related to брат? (No — different roots, and byt' is a third.)


Lesson 2 of 4.
спрашивать (spráshivat') — "to ask".

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
You have asked a question in Russian since Chapter 2 without owning the verb for asking. Here it is.

You'll want to know first — спрашивать.
спрашивать — spráshivat' — to ask (a question).
Long on the page, easy in the mouth: the stress lands on спра- and stays, SPRA-shi-vat'. The ш in the middle is the letter pishú introduced.
ya spráshivayu — I ask.
The same -ю, and ty spráshivayesh puts it in the znat' family. Set it beside your other verb for opening your mouth: говорить is to speak, спрашивать (spráshivat') is to speak with a question mark on the end. And one distinction English hides: Russian asks a question with спрашивать (spráshivat'), but for a thing with просить.

Grammar Lens: an ordinary pair, after a very strange one.
Брать took vzyat', a partner from a different root. That was the worst case, not the norm. Спрашивать's partner is спросить — ya sproshú, ty sprósish — one root worn into two shapes, the ongoing member long, the finished short.

Why it's said this way — asking is a social act.
The moment you ask, Russian makes you choose. Kak vas zovút? takes вас for a stranger and тебя for a friend — a choice that sits inside ty and vy, the split English lost when thou, ty's cousin, left ordinary use. Other languages make the same move by other grammar: French vous and Russian vy are the plural doing polite duty, German Sie borrows the third person, Spanish usted grew out of a title.
And recall what that question literally asks: how they call you, not what your name is — an action where English reaches for a possession, as French and Spanish also do.

The word, taken apart — ask, and pray.
Strip the prefix and спрашивать (spráshivat') rests on the root of просить, "to request": Indo-European preḱ-, "to ask." In Latin it became precārī, "to pray", and English took the lot: pray, prayer, precarious, deprecate. German fragen is the Germanic outcome, so спрашивать (spráshivat') and fragen are one inherited word.
English ask is not in this family; the cousin kept here is pray.

Lesson 3 of 4.
помогать (pomogát') — "to help".

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
This verb is built out of the word for being able, and that word is English may. Helping, in Russian, is lending someone your can.

You'll want to know first — помогать.
помогать — pomogát' — to help.
The г is the letter говорить introduced: Greek gamma, a hard g as in go. Both оs lean towards a away from the stress, as говорить's do — pa-ma-GAT'.
ya pamagáyu — I help.
Same -ю, and ty pamagáyesh files it with znat'.
One honest limit: the person you help takes a case this book has not reached, so keep the sentence bare. Ya pamagáyu alone is ordinary Russian.

Grammar Lens: a partner that does not end in -ть.
Every infinitive so far ended in -ть. The partner of помогать (pomogát') does not: it is pomóch', ending in -ch', and ya pamagú, ty pamózhesh.
A small handful of Russian verbs end that way, and they are built on old roots about power. The shape is a signpost, not an exception to memorise blind.
Three pairs cover the chapter so far: брать (brat'), vzyat', spráshivat', спросить, помогать (pomogát'), pomóch'.

The word, taken apart — help is power lent.
помогать (pomogát') is по- plus -могать, and under it sits moch', "to be able" — ya magú, "I can."
That root is Indo-European magʰ-, "to be able, to have power", and English holds several pieces of it:
may and might — the ordinary modal, and the noun for strength in with might and main.
dismay — to strip someone of their power to act.
German mögen and Macht; and Greek mēkhanḗ, "a contrivance", which is English machine and mechanic.
Note what did not happen here. Magú and may still mean the same thing after five thousand years. That is not the usual outcome: zhit''s root handed English quick, which once meant alive and now means fast. Some roots keep their sense, some drift out of recognition, and spráshivat''s went from ask all the way to pray. Each one has to be checked rather than guessed.

Lesson 4 of 4.
любить (lyubít') — "to love, and to like".

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
The last verb of the chapter is English love, and it lets you say every verb you own in a real sentence.

You'll want to know first — любить.
любить — lyubít' — to love, and to like.
One verb for both: with a person, love; with an activity, closer to like.
ya lyublyú — I love.
And ty lyúbish — an -ish, so любить (lyubít') joins говорить and видеть, not znat'. The stress moves too: lyublyú on the ending, lyúbish on the stem.

Grammar Lens: the letter that appears out of nowhere.
Lyubít' has no л after the б. Lyublyú does.
You have met this shape. Видеть swapped д for zh in vízhu and nowhere else. Here it happens by insertion: stems ending in б, п, в or the letters for m and f push an л in — but only in the I form.
So люблю, then lyúbish, любят, with no л at all. Learn the I form and the rest behaves.

Grammar Lens: now say everything at once.
Lyubít' takes a bare infinitive, as English like does — the dictionary form goes straight in:
Ya lyublyú chitát'. — I like reading. Ya lyublyú pisát'. — I like writing. Ya lyublyú dúmat'. — I like thinking. Ya lyublyú pomogát'. — I like helping. Ya lyublyú zhit'. — I love living.

The word, taken apart — love, belief, and by your leave.
любить (lyubít') is Indo-European leubʰ-, and English love is the same word — no borrowing, no detour. The family is wider than it looks: lief; believe (be- plus the root — to hold a thing dear enough to trust); leave in by your leave; and Latin libet, "it pleases", behind libido.
Its partner полюбить deserves an honest line: not to finish loving but to come to love, the moment the feeling starts. A pair does not always mean doing against done — which is why they get a later chapter of their own.
And the root thread: бр- carries (bear), -прос- asks (pray), mog- is able (might), люб- holds dear (love) — with пис- from last chapter still scratching its mark (picture).
