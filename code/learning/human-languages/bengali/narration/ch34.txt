Lesson 1 of 5.
সে (she) — he, she.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, because the lesson points at something written down. You can listen to all of it now and come back to the parts that need looking at once you have stopped.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say I. Say the two words for you, and say which one is for somebody you are careful with.
[your turn — recall: two chapters back, the word that points at something near — ei]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

You'll want to know: সে.
সে ভালো — she bhālo — he is well, or she is well.
Thirty-three chapters, and every sentence this book can build has been about আমি or তুমি. Somebody is either speaking or being spoken to. Nobody has ever been talked about.
সে is the word that changes that, and it does two things at once that English needs two words for. It is he and it is she, and Bengali will not tell you which. Sex is not marked on a Bengali pronoun at all — not here, not anywhere.

Grammar Lens: the third row of a table you already half know.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
person: I. plain: আমি. careful: —.
person: you. plain: তুমি. careful: আপনি.
person: he, she. plain: সে. careful: next lesson.
Chapter two taught you that Bengali grades you for respect. It grades the third person the same way, on the same ladder, and the careful form arrives in the next lesson. What the table shows is that সে (she) is the plain one: it is what you use of a child, a friend, a younger sibling — and what you must not use of somebody's grandmother.

Lesson 2 of 5.
তিনি (tini) — he, she, respectfully.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, because the lesson points at something written down. You can listen to all of it now and come back to the parts that need looking at once you have stopped.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say he is well with the plain pronoun. Then say the careful word for you.

You'll want to know: তিনি.
তিনি ভালো — tini bhālo — he is well, said of somebody you respect.
The table from the last lesson is now complete:
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
person: I. plain: আমি. careful: —.
person: you. plain: তুমি. careful: আপনি.
person: he, she. plain: সে. careful: তিনি (tini).
Two rungs in the second person, two rungs in the third, and the choice is made the same way both times: age, standing, and how well you know them.

Grammar Lens: the cost of getting it wrong.
This is not a nicety a learner can put off. Bengali offers no neutral pronoun to hide behind: every time you mention a third person you have to pick a rung, and picking the low one about somebody's elder is a real discourtesy — the kind you would then be apologising for with dukkhito.
The safe habit while you are learning: তিনি (tini) for anybody older than you or unknown to you, সে (she) for children and for friends your own age. Over-politeness is heard as slightly formal. Under-politeness is heard as rude.
Notice too that তিনি (tini) and আপনি share their ending. The careful rung is built the same way on both rows, which is one shape to learn rather than two.

Lesson 3 of 5.
ও (o) — him, her, the one standing there.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say that shirt. Then say what ও does when it stands between two things.

You'll want to know: ও as a person.
ও ভালো — o bhālo — he is well — said of somebody standing right there.
Chapter thirty-two promised this letter a third job, and here it is. ও on its own, in front of a verb, is a person: the one nearby, the one you could gesture at.
Three jobs, one syllable, and they are told apart by position:
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
where it sits: between two things. what it is: also, and.
where it sits: in front of a noun, as ওই. what it is: that.
where it sits: standing alone as a subject. what it is: him, her.

Grammar Lens: near, there, and away.
Bengali's third person is not one word but a small set sorted by how far away the person is, exactly the way its pointing words are:
এ — this one, right here.
ও — that one, over there.
সে (she) — him, her, away from us both, or not present at all.
They are all the plain rung. তিনি (tini) is the careful rung for all three distances at once, which is why it has no near-and-far partners.
So the pointing system you built two chapters ago was never only about shirts. It sorts people too, and that is why এই and ওই were worth a whole chapter.

Lesson 4 of 5.
তার (tār) — his, her.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say my. Then say he is well.

You'll want to know: তার.
তার ভাই — tār bhāi — his brother, or her brother.
তার জামাটা — tār jāmāṭā — his shirt (that one).
Chapter two taught আমার and তোমার and left it there, so possession has been available for exactly two people out of everybody in the world. তার (tār) is the third, and it is built the way the other two are:
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
person: I. pronoun: আমি. possessive: আমার.
person: you. pronoun: তুমি. possessive: তোমার.
person: he, she. pronoun: সে. possessive: তার (tār).
An -র on the end, every time. That is not a coincidence to be memorised: it is a case ending, the same kind of stuck-on piece as the classifier, and it is the second of several you will meet.
The careful form follows the same rule from তিনি (tini): তাঁর — tā̃r — with the moon-dot you learned in chapter five sitting over the vowel.

Grammar Lens: order does not change.
A possessive takes the front slot, in front of everything, and the noun keeps its own ending at the back exactly as before:
তার লাল জামাটা — tār lāl jāmāṭā — his red shirt.
Front to back: whose, then what kind, then the thing with its ending. Nothing you learned about the queue has changed; one more slot has been filled at its head.
