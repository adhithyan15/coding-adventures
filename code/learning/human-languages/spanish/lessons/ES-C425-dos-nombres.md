# be larga o be corta — the letters that needed a second name

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-ORTH-C425-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say the names of **b** and **v**. Now say the two letters out loud
as sounds, and notice the problem you have created.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-ORTH-C425-02]; assesses=[] -->

In Spanish, **b** and **v** are pronounced **identically**. There is no
*v* sound in the language at all.

That is fine while you are speaking and a catastrophe while you are spelling. So
Spanish grew extra names whose whole job is to tell the two apart:

| letter | plain name | the disambiguating names |
|---|---|---|
| b | be | **be larga**, be alta, be grande |
| v | uve | **ve corta**, ve baja, ve chica |

*Larga* and *alta* describe the tall stem of **b**; *corta* and *baja* describe
the short **v**. A Spanish speaker dictating an address will reach for these
without thinking, and a listening paper will play them.

## Grammar Lens: the other letters with two names
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-ORTH-C425-02, ES-ORTH-C425-01] -->

| letter | names |
|---|---|
| w | uve doble, doble ve, doble u |
| y | ye, i griega |
| z | zeta, ceta |

**Y** is the interesting one. *I griega* means "Greek i" — the letter arrived
from Greek *upsilon* and Spanish named it after where it came from, while
calling the ordinary **i** *i latina* when it needs the contrast. The Academy
now recommends **ye**, and both are in use.
