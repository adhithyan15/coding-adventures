# わ — the sign for *wa*, and the trap beside it

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[JA-SCRIPT-HA-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Write the three-stroke sign read *ha*: vertical, crossbar, loop.

## Script — the shape
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[JA-SCRIPT-WA-01]; assesses=[JA-SCRIPT-HA-01] -->

> わ

Two strokes:

1. the **vertical** on the left, with the small flick at the foot
2. the **body** — a short bar right from the top, then down, round to the left in
   a loop, and out to the lower right with a curl

Read **wa**: like English *wa* in *wander*, one beat.

## What you've built — and the trap
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[JA-SCRIPT-WA-01, JA-PARTICLE-WA-SPELLING] -->

Here is the thing this sign exists in this lesson to protect you from.

The daytime greeting **sounds** like it ends in *wa*. **It is not written with
this sign.** It ends with the sign read *ha* — the one you learned early and were
told was holding a second job.

That second job is grammatical: は is also Japanese's topic marker, and **as a
topic marker it is pronounced *wa***. The greeting is a frozen fragment of a
longer sentence, and it kept the marker's spelling along with the marker's sound.

So the rule you need is not about sound at all:

- **わ** is the sign for the mora *wa* inside ordinary words.
- **は** read as *wa* means you are looking at the topic marker, or at something
  that used to contain one.

Getting this backwards is the single most common beginner spelling error in
Japanese, and it is the reason this book taught you the sign for *wa* **after**
you already knew the greeting — so the shape could arrive with its warning
attached rather than be quietly assumed.
