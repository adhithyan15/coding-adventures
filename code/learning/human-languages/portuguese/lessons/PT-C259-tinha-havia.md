# tinha, havia — the words a scene is made of

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[PT-GRAMMAR-IMPERFEITO-01, PT-GRAMMAR-IDADE-04, PT-LEX-TER-HAVER-02] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Build "I was speaking" from *falar*. (*Falava.*) Say "I am twenty"
the way chapter fourteen taught it. (*Tenho vinte anos.*) Then "there is
bread". (*Há pão.*)

[PAUSE 1s] Hold on to all three. This lesson moves them into the past.

## You'll want to know: era and tinha
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[PT-LEX-TINHA-01]; assesses=[PT-ETYMON-SER-ROOTS-02, PT-LEX-TER-02, PT-LEX-C74-CIDADE2-01, PT-LEX-C64-ADJ1-02] -->

Only four verbs refuse last lesson's recipe, and two of them are the ones you
use most. The first you met in chapter sixteen: **ser** gives **era**.

> **era**, **eras**, **era**, **éramos**, **eram**
> *A estação era pequena.* — The station was small.

The second is **ter**, and it gives **tinha**:

> **tinha**, **tinhas**, **tinha**, **tínhamos**, **tinham**
> *Tinha vinte anos.* — I was twenty.
> *Tínhamos um cão.* — We had a dog.

Age is the clearest case. *Tenho vinte anos* is now; *tinha vinte anos* is the
age you were, all through some stretch of the past. The other two refusers are
**vir**, which gives *vinha*, and **pôr**, which gives *punha*; you will meet
them when you need them.

## You'll want to know: havia
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[PT-LEX-HAVIA-02]; assesses=[PT-LEX-TER-HAVER-02, PT-LEX-C102-COISAS102-03] -->

You have **há**, "there is" and "there are". Its past is **havia**, and it
follows the recipe after all, since it comes from *haver*:

> *Havia muitas pessoas.* — There were a lot of people.
> *Não havia pão.* — There was no bread.

Like *há*, it does not change for a plural: one *havia* for one thing or for a
hundred. That is how a European Portuguese scene opens. In everyday Brazilian
speech you will often hear *tinha* doing this job instead: *tinha muitas
pessoas*.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[] -->

**ter** is inherited from Latin *tenēre*, "to hold" (EtymDB 2.1; Lewis & Short,
under *teneo*), and **tinha** goes back to its past *tenēbat*, "he was holding".
**havia** goes back to *habēbat*, "he was having", the past of *habēre*, which
gave **haver** (EtymDB 2.1). Its first *b* softened to *v*, as in *falava*, and
the second wore away, as in *comia*.

**era** is Latin *erat*, "he was" (Lewis & Short, under *sum*). Latin's own past
of "to be" had no *b*, which is why *era* looks nothing like *falava*.
How the *-nh-* of *tinha* arose is not something either source traces, so it
is left open here.
