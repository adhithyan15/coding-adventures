Arabic, chapter 45: Writing --- Four Lines, And The Joiner That Is Not A Word.
2 lessons. The first lesson already needs your eyes or your hands, so save this one for when you have stopped.


Lesson 1 of 2.
Four lines, and the joiner that is not a separate word — four lines to one reader, and the joiner that is not a separate word at all.

Before we start: this one needs your hands, so it is not a driving lesson. You will want a pen and something to write on and your eyes, because the lesson points at something written down. You can listen to all of it now and come back to the parts that need looking at once you have stopped.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
The last lesson asked for writing finished on time. This one asks for writing that holds together.
[pause 1 second]
Pick one person who will read it.

You'll want to know.
[pause 2 seconds]
Connected writing is not longer writing. Four short lines can be connected; one long line can fail to be. What makes it connected is that a sentence stops standing on its own.
[pause 2 seconds]
You hold three joiners. و (wa) adds. لكن sets against. لأن gives the reason.
[pause 2 seconds]
And the first of those does something the others do not. لكن and لأن stand as words, with space on both sides. و (wa) does not. It is written onto the front of the next word, with no space at all.
[pause 2 seconds]
So in Arabic the join is not a word the eye can skip over on its way to the content. It is a letter the following word now begins with — the two things being joined are physically fastened, and a reader meets the joint before they meet the second thing.
[pause 2 seconds]
A hand that leaves a space after و (wa) has not made a small typographic slip. It has written a letter that now belongs to nothing.

Writing — connected composition.
[pause 2 seconds]
Write four lines to the person you picked, right to left.
At least one must use و (wa) — attached, no space. At least one must use لأن.
[pause 3 seconds]
Use only words you already write. This lesson adds no vocabulary; the whole exercise is the joining.

Wrap-up recall.
[pause 2 seconds]
Cover your first line and read the second alone. Does it still make sense without the first? If it does, you wrote a list — which is where you were one lesson ago.
[pause 2 seconds]
Then look at every و (wa) you wrote. Is there a space after it? There should not be.


Lesson 2 of 2.
The last page — the closing page — write the first word the book ever assembled, name the catch that is a consonant and not a vowel, and keep the one sentence that rescues every conversation.

Before we start: this one needs your hands, so it is not a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, for letter shapes on the page. You can listen to everything else now — leave the sections called Writing: the hook family, and the word it builds and Script: the mark that is not a vowel until you have stopped, and I will say so again when we reach it.

Warm-up.
[pause 4 seconds]
Write خير by hand, right to left, and say the one sentence that gets a lost conversation moving. Both were taught once, at opposite ends of this book.

Writing: the hook family, and the word it builds.
ح ح خ ج share one body: a rounded hook flowing into a tail that drops below the line. Only the dot tells them apart — ح has none, خ takes one above, ج one below.
[once you have stopped driving — write: خير — khāʾ with its dot above, reaching left into yāʾ, then rā, and lift]
[once you have stopped driving — write: بخير — glue بـ on the front, one dot below, joining into the khāʾ]
Nothing joins after ر, which is why the pen lifts. And خير is the word that was hiding inside the morning greeting before anyone told you it was there; adding بـ, in, turns good into the answer in goodness.

Script: the mark that is not a vowel.
Say English uh-oh slowly and feel the catch between the halves. That catch is a glottal stop, and Arabic writes it as a real consonant: ء, the hamza. It is not one of the short-vowel marks. Sitting on an alif seat it becomes أ, which is how the word for you begins.
The short-vowel marks do something else entirely, and the clearest proof is the word for you: أنت is one consonant skeleton, and a single mark that ordinary print omits decides whether you have addressed أنتَ, a man, or أنتِ, a woman.

Guided Practice: the sentence to keep.
كرّر من فضلك — karrir min faḍlik.
[your turn — say: you have not understood, then ask for the sentence back — lā afham. karrir min faḍlik]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: call somebody first, then ask — yā …, karrir min faḍlik]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
Of everything in this book, that is the line to carry out of it. The marks Arabic leaves off the page are the ones a beginner most needs, so a beginner will miss words — and the fix is not a better memory. It is four syllables asking for another pass.

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 4 seconds]
Say whether hamza is a vowel mark and what it writes. (No — a glottal stop.) Say what tells ح, خ and ج apart. (The dot — none, one above, one below.) Say what بـ adds to خير. (In — in goodness.) And give the sentence that rescues a conversation. (Karrir min faḍlik.)
