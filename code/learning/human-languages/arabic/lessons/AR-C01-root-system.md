# The Arabic root system — this whole book, but literal

## Warm-up

[PAUSE 2s] Every book in this series is obsessed with *roots*. Arabic is the
language where roots aren't a historical curiosity — they're the *living
machine* that builds almost every word. Understand this one idea and Arabic
stops looking like memorization and starts looking like arithmetic.

## Two things about the script first

Arabic is written **right to left**. And its letters **connect** into each
other like cursive, so a letter changes shape depending on whether it's at the
start, middle, or end of a word. Don't try to master the alphabet up front —
you already read Arabic (if rusty), and this book reintroduces letters *inside
real words* as they come. For now, just re-set two habits: eyes move
right-to-left, and letters join up.

## The big idea: three consonants = one meaning-family

Most Arabic words are built on a **root** of (usually) **three consonants**
that carries a core meaning. You then pour those consonants into fixed
**patterns** (vowels and prefixes/suffixes slotted around them) to make
related words. The classic example:

**k–t–b** (ك ت ب) — the idea of *writing*:

- **kataba** — he wrote
- **kitāb** (كتاب) — a book
- **kātib** (كاتب) — a writer
- **maktab** (مكتب) — an office (a place of writing)
- **maktaba** (مكتبة) — a library / bookshop
- **maktūb** — written (also "a letter"; and, fatalistically, "destiny")

Six words, one root. Notice the pattern **ma---a-** ("a place of ___") turns
*k-t-b* into *maktab* "office" — and the *same* pattern on other roots makes
other "place" words. You're not memorizing six words; you're learning one root
and a couple of patterns.

> This is the Latin/Greek root idea from the Spanish and German books, but
> **more regular and more visible**. Where English hides *scribe / describe /
> script* inside *scrib-*, Arabic wears its root on the surface of every
> derived word. Every greeting in this chapter is a root plus a pattern.
