Tamil, chapter 6: Chapter 6.
4 lessons. All 4 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 4.
-உக்கு (-ukku) — "to, for".

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Your first case ending. English says "to me" with a separate word in front. Tamil says it with a piece stuck on the back — and that small difference is the biggest structural fact about the language.

Grammar Lens: Adding it on.
Take a noun you know and add -உக்கு (-ukku):
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
word: பெயர் peyar — name. + the dative: பெயருக்கு peyarukku. meaning: to/for the name.
word: வேலை vēlai — work. + the dative: வேலைக்கு vēlaikku. meaning: for work.
The suffix has two shapes — -உக்கு -ukku and -க்கு -kku — chosen by the sound the noun ends in. One case, two forms; the seam still shows.
And with "I" — this one is irregular and worth memorising, because you'll use it constantly:
நான் nāṉ ("I") becomes எனக்கு enakku ("to me").
The pronoun changes its body (nāṉ becomes en-) before the suffix lands. Nouns don't — they just take the ending.

[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
What does -உக்கு mean? ("To" or "for.") Where does it go? (On the end of the noun — Tamil adds, it doesn't put a word in front.) What is "to me"? (எனக்கு enakku — the pronoun's body changes to en- first.) Why does the suffix show up as -ukku and -kku? (One case, two shapes, chosen by the noun's ending.) Lesson 2 of 4.
-உக்கு (-ukku) — one carriage in a suffix train.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
You can point to peyar and -ukku separately. That visible seam is the structural lesson.

Grammar Lens: Stacking versus fusion.
[a table of 4 rows, read aloud]
fixes the case? Tamil -ukku: dative, always. Latin -īs: dative or ablative.
carries number? Tamil -ukku: no; another suffix can. Latin -īs: plural is baked in.
carries declension? Tamil -ukku: no. Latin -īs: first/second class is baked in.
visible seam? Tamil -ukku: peyar + ukku. Latin -īs: one fused ending.
Tamil stacks. Each suffix contributes one meaning in a fixed order, like a train carriage. Latin fuses case, number, and class into an indivisible ending, and -īs does not even identify one case by itself.
This is agglutinative structure: long words remain buildable because their pieces stay legible. The comparison is not about simplicity; it is about where the grammatical information lives.

Guided Practice.

[your turn — segment: peyar + ukku]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: "one suffix, one meaning, visible seam"]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — contrast: Tamil stacks, Latin fuses]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
What does -ukku contribute? (Dative to/for, one meaning.) What does Latin -īs fuse? (Case, plural number, and declension class.) Which system leaves the seam visible? (Tamil's agglutinative stack.)


Lesson 3 of 4.
எனக்குத் தமிழ் தெரியும் (enakkut tamiḻ teriyum) — "I know Tamil".

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
In Chapter 5 you said நான் தமிழ் பேசுகிறேன் — "I speak Tamil," with nāṉ as the subject. Now say "I know Tamil." Tamil will not let you use nāṉ.

You'll want to know: The sentence.
எனக்குத் தமிழ் தெரியும் (enakkut tamiḻ teriyum). enakku tamiḻ teriyum. literally: "to-me Tamil is-known."
Word by word:
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
piece: எனக்கு(த்) enakku(t), "to me" — the dative from the last lesson.
piece: தமிழ் tamiḻ, Tamil (Ch. 5).
piece: தெரியும் teriyum, "is known / is apparent".
That extra த் on enakku is sandhi — a joining consonant Tamil inserts between certain words to smooth the seam. It belongs to neither word on its own; it exists only because they are next to each other. Nothing to learn yet, but worth noticing, because you will see it constantly.
You are not the subject. Nāṉ is gone — the person has been moved into the dative. What's left in the plain, unmarked form is tamiḻ: the sentence is now about the language, and you are merely the one it is known to.
(Grammarians call enakku the dative subject, because it behaves like the subject even though it isn't in the subject case. The odd feeling of the sentence is exactly that split.)

Grammar Lens: Why: things that happen to you.
Tamil sorts verbs by whether you do something or something happens to you. Speaking is something you do — so Ch. 5 used nāṉ. But knowing, liking, wanting, being hungry, being cold: these arrive at you. Tamil marks that by putting the person in the dative.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
English frames it as: I know Tamil. Tamil frames it as: Tamil is known to me.
English frames it as: I like it. Tamil frames it as: it is pleasing to me.
English frames it as: I am cold. Tamil frames it as: cold is to me.
English does this occasionally and archaically — "methinks" is exactly it (me is dative: "it seems to me"). Tamil made it a rule.

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
What does எனக்குத் தமிழ் தெரியும் (enakkut tamiḻ teriyum) literally say? ("To-me Tamil is-known.") Which word has English got that Tamil hasn't? (A nominative "I" — nāṉ is replaced by the dative enakku.) Why does Tamil use the dative here? (Because knowing happens to you; you don't do it.) Which English word preserves the same idea? (Methinks — "it seems to me".) Next: watch all four Dravidian sisters use the same experiencer shape.


Lesson 4 of 4.
-ukku, -ku, -ge, -ikku — the family shows its bones.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Tamil says knowing happens to you. Its three closest sisters make the same grammatical choice.

Grammar Lens: Four sentences, one construction.
[a table of 4 rows, read aloud]
language: Tamil. "I know [the language]": enakku tamiḻ teriyum. dative: -ukku.
language: Telugu. "I know [the language]": nāku telugu vaccu. dative: -ku.
language: Kannada. "I know [the language]": nanage kannaḍa gottu. dative: -ge.
language: Malayalam. "I know [the language]": enikku malayāḷam aṟiyām. dative: -ikku.
The verbs differ, but the experiencer sits in a dative whose forms visibly belong together: -ukku / -ku / -ge / -ikku. This is family evidence, just as blanc/bianco/branco exposes Romance kinship.
The comparison also makes cross-language practice useful: once you recognize "to me," you can search for the known or apparent thing rather than forcing an English subject onto the sentence.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: "-ukku, -ku, -ge, -ikku"]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[once you have stopped driving — point: the experiencer in each sentence]
[your turn — paraphrase: "the language is known to me"]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Do the four sisters agree on dative experiencers? (Yes.) Which four suffix shapes reveal the relationship? (-ukku, -ku, -ge, -ikku.) What stays plain in the sentence? (The thing known or experienced.)
