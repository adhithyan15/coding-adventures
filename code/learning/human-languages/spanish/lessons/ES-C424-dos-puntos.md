# los dos puntos — a plural name, and the mark that opens a letter

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-ORTH-C424-02] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say *la coma*. The next mark also introduces a list — but it stands
in front of the list rather than inside it.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-ORTH-C424-03]; assesses=[] -->

**los dos puntos** — "the colon." Spanish names it for what it looks like:
**two points**, and the name is plural, so it takes *los* and a plural verb.

Its main job is to **announce** what follows:

> Tengo tres colores favoritos: rojo, azul y verde.

The colon promises a list; the commas then divide it; and there is still no
comma before the final *y*.

## Grammar Lens: the letter opening, where English disagrees
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-ORTH-C424-03] -->

This is the exam-relevant half, because a written paper often asks for a note
or a message:

| language | opening |
|---|---|
| Spanish | Querido Juan**:** |
| English | Dear John**,** |

Spanish puts **dos puntos** after the greeting of a letter, an email or a note.
English puts a comma. A Spanish reader notices the comma immediately, the way
an English reader notices a colon.

After it, the text begins on the next line, and it begins with a **capital**.
