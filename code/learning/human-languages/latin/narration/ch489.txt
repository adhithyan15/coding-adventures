Latin, chapter 489: Joy and gladness.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
laetitia, laetitiae — joy, gladness.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a ditch, then the Latin for a rampart.

You'll want to know: laetitia, laetitiae.
laetitia, laetitiae — "joy, gladness".
Post victōriam magna laetitia in urbe erat. After the victory there was great joy in the city.
Feminine, first declension. Laetitia is lively joy that shows; gaudium is joy felt inside.

The word, taken apart.
laetitia is built on the adjective laetus "glad, happy" with the noun ending -itia (Lewis & Short; EtymDB derives it from laetus).

Lesson 2 of 5.
laetor, laetārī — to rejoice, be glad.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a rampart, then the Latin for joy.

You'll want to know: laetor, laetārī.
laetor, laetārī — "to rejoice, be glad".
Māter laetātur, quod fīlius sānus redit. The mother rejoices because her son comes home healthy.
Deponent: laetor, laetārī, laetātus sum. With the ablative or with dē: victōriā laetor — I rejoice at the victory.

The word, taken apart.
laetor is a deponent verb built on laetus "glad, happy" (Lewis & Short).

Lesson 3 of 5.
hilaritās, hilaritātis — cheerfulness, merriment.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for joy, then the Latin for to rejoice.

You'll want to know: hilaritās, hilaritātis.
hilaritās, hilaritātis — "cheerfulness, merriment".
Hilaritās avī omnēs hospitēs laetōs facit. Grandfather's cheerfulness makes all the guests happy.
Feminine, third declension. English hilarity comes from it.

The word, taken apart.
hilaritās is built on hilaris "cheerful", an early borrowing from Greek hilaros "cheerful" (EtymDB; Lewis & Short). English hilarity comes from it.

Lesson 4 of 5.
exsultō, exsultāre — to leap for joy, rejoice greatly.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to rejoice, then the Latin for cheerfulness.

You'll want to know: exsultō, exsultāre.
exsultō, exsultāre — "to leap for joy, rejoice greatly".
Puerī in hortō exsultant, quod hodiē fēriae sunt. The boys leap for joy in the garden, because today is a holiday.
exsultō, exsultāre, exsultāvī, exsultātum. First to leap up, then to be overjoyed. English exult.

The word, taken apart.
exsultō is built on ex- "out, up" and saltō "to jump, dance", the frequentative of saliō "to leap" (Lewis & Short). English exult comes from it.

Lesson 5 of 5.
iūcunditās, iūcunditātis — pleasantness, delight.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for cheerfulness, then the Latin for to leap for joy.

You'll want to know: iūcunditās, iūcunditātis.
iūcunditās, iūcunditātis — "pleasantness, delight".
Iūcunditātem hortī nostrī omnēs amīcī laudant. All our friends praise the pleasantness of our garden.
Feminine, third declension, built on iūcundus — pleasant.

The word, taken apart.
iūcunditās is built on iūcundus "pleasant, agreeable" with the noun ending -itās (Lewis & Short, under jucunditas); Lewis & Short links iūcundus with iocus "a joke", but its origin is not settled.
