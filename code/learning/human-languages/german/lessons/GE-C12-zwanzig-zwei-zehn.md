# -zig and -ty

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GE-LEX-ZWANZIG-01, GE-LEX-ZEHN-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] The ending changed at twenty. It did not change subject.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[GE-ETYMON-ZWANZIG-ZWEI-ZEHN-01]; assesses=[GE-LEX-ZWEI-01, GE-LEX-ZWANZIG-01] -->

Older Germanic *twaintig*, which is *twain* — "two" — plus *tig*, and ***tig* is
ten**.

| word | is | and means |
|---|---|---|
| *zwanzig* | *zwei* + *-zig* | two tens |
| twenty | twain + *-ty* | two tens |

So *-zig* and *-ty* are the **same ending**, both of them *ten* worn down by
being said a great many times. German's *zehn* stayed whole because it kept
being used on its own; the version welded onto twenty wore out instead.

## Why it's said this way
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GE-ETYMON-ZWANZIG-ZWEI-ZEHN-01, GE-GRAMMAR-ZIFFER-PLUS-ZEHN-01, GE-ETYMON-LIF-LEFTOVER-01] -->

Stand back and the whole span from eleven to twenty is three ideas, and you have
all three.

- **Eleven and twelve** count what is *left over* after ten fingers.
- **Thirteen to nineteen** are a digit with *ten* on the end.
- **Twenty** is *two tens*, with the same *ten* worn to *-zig*.

Every one of those is a compound. Not one number in this chapter is a word you
had to be given — they are all built, and the parts were yours before the
chapter started.
