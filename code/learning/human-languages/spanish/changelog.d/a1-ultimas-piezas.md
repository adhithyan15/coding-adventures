## Three of the five were stale notes rather than gaps

This is now the dominant shape of late-stage coverage work, and it is worth
stating plainly: **the notes are the least-maintained part of the inventory,
because nothing re-reads them when a chapter lands.**

`A1-F4-01` — *give an order* — said the corpus *"never introduces the
affirmative imperative."* It does, and has since chapter 136:
`ES-C50-habla` teaches *habla · come · vive* across all three conjugations and
`ES-C50-ocho-cortos` teaches the eight irregulars *di · haz · ve · pon · ten ·
sal · sé · ven*.

**The inventory was contradicting itself.** `A1-V-11` — *affirmative imperative,
second person, with its irregulars* — has been wired to those two atoms all
along. One point said the thing was taught and another said it did not exist.

The point's second exponent is a **softened order with an attenuator**, and
that is `por favor` (ES-C06) together with `ES-C50-sintesis-pedir-bien`, which
reads *Coma, por favor* and makes the better argument: in Spanish the
attenuation lives mostly in the choice of *tú* or *usted*, decided before the
command leaves your mouth. Closed with **zero authoring**.

`A1-NE06-01` and `A1-NE07-04` were **partly** stale, which is the more
dangerous kind, because the note reads as a survey:

| note said | corpus had |
|---|---|
| "none of the four listed words" | *universidad*, *clase*, *biblioteca* |
| "neither paro nor trabajo as a noun" | *el trabajo* (ES-C394) |

Both gaps were **one word wide**, not four and not two.

## el instituto — the missing middle rung

The one word `A1-NE06-01` needed is the one that mattered. The corpus could
name the primary school and the university and had **nothing for the years
between them**:

| age | building |
|---|---|
| about 6 to 12 | la escuela |
| about 12 to 18 | **el instituto** |
| after 18 | la universidad |

It is also a false friend — an English *institute* is a research body — and on a
sign it appears as **IES**, *Instituto de Educación Secundaria*, which a reading
paper prints without expansion.

## The marks half, which the note was right to insist on

`A1-NE06-05`'s note is the careful one in the set. It had **already corrected
one false claim** — *examen* is introduced by `ES-C410-examen` — and still held
the point uncovered, because the label is *"examinations and marks"* and the
marks half was wholly absent. That was the right call, and chapter 429 satisfies
it on its own terms rather than by loosening it.

The four words carry three Latin pictures worth keeping:

| word | the picture under it |
|---|---|
| nota | a scratch cut into a surface |
| aprobar | a thing tested and found good |
| suspender | a thing left hanging |

The third is **still literally true**. A failed subject in Spain hangs over the
student until a resit, and the phrase for it — *una asignatura pendiente* — is
used outside school for anything you have been putting off.

*Aprobar* carries a second observation. English *I passed the exam* puts the
student in charge; Latin *approbare* is *ad-* plus *probare*, "to test and find
good," so the judgement travels the other way.

Two words for one number, sorted by register:

| where you meet it | which word |
|---|---|
| a friend asking in a corridor | la nota |
| a column heading on a document | la calificación |

The synthesis lesson reads a three-row report card with **IES** at the top and
*calificación* over the numbers, which is what a reading paper looks like.

## Three garments, each with its own trap

`A1-NE12-02` was accurate at four of seven — *pantalón*, *falda*, *camisa* and
*zapato* were taught. Chapter 430 adds exactly the three that were missing, and
none of them is a bare vocabulary item:

| word | what to watch |
|---|---|
| los vaqueros | plural every time, with a plural article |
| el jersey | plural *jerséis*, and it gains a written accent |
| el bolso | *la bolsa* is a different object |

*Jerséis* is the best of the three, because it shows a **borrowed word being
made to obey**. *Jersey* is stressed on its last syllable and needs no accent
while it ends in **-y**; change the ending for the plural and the accent has to
appear to hold the stress where it was. Spanish did not leave the word foreign.

Three of the four new words in the chapter are named after something other than
themselves — *el paro* after the stopping, *los vaqueros* after the cowherd, *el
jersey* after a Channel island. That is the habit that lets a reader guess at a
word they have not met.

## estar en paro — and why it is not *ser*

It takes **estar**, because it names a situation. *El paro* names the
**stoppage** where English names the missing employment, and the verb behind it
travelled a long way to get there: Latin *parare* meant **to make ready**, which
became *to stand ready*, which became *to stand still*.

Spain says *paro*; much of Latin America says *desempleo*, which is also the
word a newspaper prints. The pair a form asks you to choose between is short —
*tengo trabajo* against *estoy en paro* — and the noun has been taught since the
professions chapter with nothing to stand against it until now.
