# el patio — the space in the middle

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C354-WHERE-30, ES-LEX-C302-AWAY-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say **el jardín** and **la calle**. A patio is neither, and it sits
between them.

## You'll want to know: el patio
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-C450-SUELO-01]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C354-WHERE-30] -->

**el patio** — the courtyard: an open space with the building around it.

*Un piso con patio* — a flat with a courtyard. *Los niños están en el patio* —
the children are out in the yard. *El patio de butacas* — the stalls of a
theatre, the flat part with the seats.

A *jardín* has plants and can be anywhere. A *patio* is defined by what
surrounds it, and it may hold nothing but a washing line.

## Grammar Lens: the school patio
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C450-SUELO-01, ES-LEX-C302-AWAY-01] -->

In a school, *el patio* is the playground, and with it comes a phrase you will
hear constantly:

| Spanish | what it covers |
|---|---|
| *salir al patio* | to go out at break |
| *la hora del patio* | break time |

The second one names the time by naming the place, and Spanish does this
often — *la hora de la calle* for knocking-off time works the same way. When
a Spanish phrase seems to be about a location, check whether it is really
about a moment in the day.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C450-SUELO-01] -->

Here the honest answer is that nobody knows.

The best-supported account brings it from Occitan *pati*, waste ground, and
from there back to Latin **pactum**, an agreement — common land held by
agreement. A rival account points at Latin *patere*, to lie open, which fits
the meaning better and the sound worse. Neither has settled the matter.

That is why this lesson lists no root. An empty list is not an oversight in
this material; it is a claim, and the claim is that the origin is genuinely
unresolved. Saying so is more useful than picking the prettier story, because
the next time you see a root listed you can trust that somebody checked.

What is certain is where the word went. Spanish carried *patio* into English,
French and German, and it is now the ordinary word for a paved space behind a
house in places that have never used it for anything else.
