## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-C370-WHERE-55]; assesses=[] -->

**la costa** — "the coast."

Latin **costa** is a **rib**. Not the sea, not the shore — a bone in your
side.

The step to a coastline is a picture rather than a definition. A rib is the
**side** of a body; a coast is the side of a country. English took the same
picture and uses it every day without opening it up:

- **coast**, from the Latin through French;
- **intercostal**, the muscles between the ribs, straight off the Latin;
- to **accost** — *ad* plus *costa*, to come up alongside. You accost somebody
  by arriving at their side;
- **cutlet**, which is French *côtelette*, a diminutive of a diminutive: a
  little little rib. English speakers pulled its first syllable towards **cut**
  because a cutlet is something cut, and the word has looked like that ever
  since.

There is a footnote about the Spanish word itself. *Costa* did not come down
the inherited line into Castilian; it was taken in from a neighbouring
language of the peninsula. The form that **did** come down the inherited line
is **cuesta**, a slope — with the broken vowel that marks an inherited word.
One Latin rib, two Spanish words, and the diphthong tells you which is which.

Two neighbours that are nothing to do with it: **cosa**, a thing, and
**cosecha**, a harvest. Both begin the same and neither is a relative.
