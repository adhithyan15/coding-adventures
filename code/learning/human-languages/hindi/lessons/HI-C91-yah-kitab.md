# यह किताब — the same word, one step to the left

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[HI-LEX-C40-DEIXIS-01, HI-LEX-C40-DEIXIS-02] -->

[PAUSE 2s] You have **यह** and **वह** as answers to *which one* — standing
alone, meaning *this one* and *that one*. Try putting one in front of a noun.

## You'll want to know: यह किताब
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[HI-LEX-C91-NOUN-07]; assesses=[] -->

**यह किताब** — this book. **वह आदमी** — that man.

Nothing is added and nothing bends. The word you already have is placed in
front of a noun, and that is the whole of it.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[HI-LEX-C91-NOUN-07, HI-LEX-C40-DEIXIS-01] -->

| how it stands | what it means |
|---|---|
| यह | this one |
| यह किताब | this book |

English does exactly the same thing with the same two words — *this* is a
pronoun in *this is mine* and a determiner in *this book* — so there is nothing
here to translate differently. The reason it needs saying is that the track
taught these words **only** as standalone answers, and a reading paper uses them
in front of nouns far more often.

There is a second reason it matters. Hindi has no article, so **यह** and **वह**
are what a writer reaches for when *the* would have been enough in English. A
sentence that begins **वह आदमी** is often best read as *the man* rather than
*that man*.

## Grammar Lens: what fills the space before a noun
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[HI-LEX-C91-NOUN-07, HI-LEX-C40-DEIXIS-02, HI-CONCEPT-C37-KITAAB-01] -->

| in front of the noun | what it does |
|---|---|
| nothing | a book, or the book |
| एक | one book, a book |
| यह / वह | this book, that book |

Three choices and the reader picks from context. That is the whole of Hindi
definiteness at this level, and it is why the track has spent no time on
articles: **there are none to spend it on.**
