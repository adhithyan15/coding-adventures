# chhah ākār — six shapes, and the two that were only ever about things

## Grammar Lens: which ones a person can be
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[HI-LEX-C82-BODY-01, HI-LEX-C82-BODY-02, HI-LEX-C82-BODY-03, HI-LEX-C82-BODY-04, HI-LEX-C41-ADJ-02, HI-GRAMMAR-C41-ADJ-SYSTEM] -->

You had two size words already, and they were taught about objects. The four
new ones are the ones a person gets described with:

| adjective | feminine | used of a person? |
|---|---|---|
| बड़ा | बड़ी | as *elder*, not as *large* |
| *choṭā* | *choṭī* | as *younger*, not as *small* |
| लंबा | लंबी | yes — tall |
| नाटा | नाटी | yes — short in height |
| पतला | पतली | yes — thin |
| मोटा | मोटी | yes — stout |

The first two rows are the ones worth pausing on. **बड़ा** and *choṭā* applied
to a person almost always mean **older** and **younger**, not big and small —
*baṛā bhāī* is an elder brother, whatever his height. So the track could size a
box long before it could describe a body, which is why these four were needed —
and why *nāṭā* had to be a separate word rather than a reuse of *choṭā*.

All six end in **-आ**. All six bend. That is not a coincidence: size and shape
words are old inherited vocabulary, and inherited Hindi adjectives are the ones
that kept the ending.
