\markboth{Pronunciation}{Pronunciation}

Not to be read before the lessons --- each lesson gives the sounds its word
needs. This page just gathers them. Italian is gloriously \textbf{phonetic}:
once you know these rules, you can read almost any word aloud correctly.

\section*{The traps for an English speaker}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Every letter is sounded}, and vowels are pure: \emph{a} (ah),
    \emph{e} (eh), \emph{i} (ee), \emph{o} (oh), \emph{u} (oo) --- never the
    English glide.
  \item \textbf{c} and \textbf{g} are soft (\emph{ch}, \emph{j}) before
    \emph{e/i} (\emph{ciao}, \emph{giorno}), hard (\emph{k}, \emph{g}) before
    \emph{a/o/u} (\emph{casa}, \emph{gatto}). \textbf{ch}/\textbf{gh} force the
    hard sound (\emph{chi} $=$ ``kee'').
  \item \textbf{gli} $=$ the \emph{lli} of \emph{million}; \textbf{gn} $=$ the
    \emph{ny} of \emph{canyon}; \textbf{sc} before \emph{e/i} $=$ \emph{sh}.
  \item \textbf{z} $=$ \emph{ts} or \emph{dz} (\emph{grazie} $=$ ``GRAH-tsyeh'').
  \item \textbf{Double consonants are held longer} and change meaning:
    \emph{no\underline{tt}e} (night) vs.\ \emph{note} (notes).
  \item \textbf{h} is always silent --- it exists only to harden \emph{c}/\emph{g}.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Stress}

Most words stress the \emph{second-to-last} syllable (\emph{giOR-no}). When the
stress falls on the last syllable it is written with an accent
(\emph{citt\`{a}}, \emph{cos\'{\i}}, \emph{caff\`{e}}).

\section*{Italian in the Romance family}

Italian, Spanish, and French are all worn-down Latin. Italian stayed closest to
the parent, so its \emph{-ct-} became \emph{-tt-} (\emph{notte}) where Spanish
went \emph{-ch-} (\emph{noche}) and French \emph{-it-} (\emph{nuit}); and it kept
final vowels (\emph{giorno}, \emph{notte}) that French dropped. The lessons draw
these contrasts as they arise.
