# el estado — the condition

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-ESTAR, ES-LEX-C473-CONSEJO-02] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say **estar** and **recomendar**. What somebody recommends often
depends on the thing's condition — and the word for that is hiding inside
*estar*.

## You'll want to know: el estado
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-C473-CONSEJO-03]; assesses=[ES-LEX-ESTAR] -->

**el estado** — the state, the condition.

> Aceptar solo lo que esté en buen **estado**.

**En buen estado** — in good condition. **En mal estado** — in bad condition,
and of food it means gone off. These two phrases are the word's everyday work.

You have read one of them already, on a donation notice: *juguetes en buen
estado*.

## Grammar Lens: a participle hardened into a noun, again
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C473-CONSEJO-03, ES-LEX-C431-PEDIDO-01] -->

*Estado* is also the past participle of *estar* — *he estado aquí* — and that
is worth being exact about, because the two are not quite the same story.

| word | where the noun came from |
|---|---|
| el pedido | the participle of *pedir*, hardened |
| **el estado** | Latin *status*, borrowed as a noun |

[El pedido](./ES-C431-pedido.md) really is its verb's participle put to work.
*El estado* arrived as a noun already, from Latin, and only **looks** like the
participle because both descend from the same verb. The shape coincides; the
route does not.

File it with them anyway — the family is the easiest place to remember it —
but do not tell yourself you derived it.

Which is why it is *estar* and never *ser* underneath. A condition is
temporary by definition, and the word carries that in its bones.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C473-CONSEJO-03, ES-LEX-ESTAR] -->

Latin **status**, from **stare**, to stand — the root *estar* itself came from.

English took *status* whole, and took the rest of the family too: **statue**,
something made to stand; **station**, a standing-place; **stable**, able to
stand; **estate** and **state**, which is the same word arriving twice by
different roads.

So *el estado* is a standing — and the political **State** is that word again,
meaning the way a country stands. Spanish uses one noun for both, and a capital
letter does the separating.
