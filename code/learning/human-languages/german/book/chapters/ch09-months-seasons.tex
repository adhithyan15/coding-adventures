\chapter{Months and Seasons}
\label{ch:months-seasons}

The German year is built from two different quarries. Its twelve
\emph{months} were carried north from Rome, name and all; its four
\emph{seasons} are homegrown Germanic, one of them the twin of English
\textbf{harvest}. This chapter walks both halves and shows where the seam runs.
\emph{Practice lessons: \texttt{lessons/GE-C09-*.md}.}

\section{\emph{die Monate} --- Rome's calendar in German}
\label{lesson:monate}

German's numbers are homegrown (\emph{eins}, \emph{zwei}), and its weekday-gods
are Germanic (\emph{Donnerstag}, Thor's day). But its \textbf{months} --- like
its clock-word \textbf{die Uhr} --- are \textbf{borrowed straight from Latin}.
Time-reckoning, again, came to the German world from Rome.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
German & $\leftarrow$ Latin & who / what \\
\midrule
\emph{Januar} & \emph{Januarius} & \textbf{Janus}, god of doors and beginnings \\
\emph{Februar} & \emph{Februarius} & the \emph{Februa} purification festival \\
\emph{M\"{a}rz} & \emph{Martius} & \textbf{Mars}, the war-god \\
\emph{April} & \emph{Aprilis} & perhaps \emph{aperire}, ``to open'' \\
\emph{Mai} & \emph{Maius} & \textbf{Maia}, goddess of growth \\
\emph{Juni} & \emph{Junius} & \textbf{Juno}, queen of the gods \\
\emph{Juli} & \emph{Julius} & \textbf{Julius Caesar} \\
\emph{August} & \emph{Augustus} & the emperor \textbf{Augustus} \\
\emph{September} & \emph{september} & ``\textbf{7th}'' (\emph{septem}) \\
\emph{Oktober} & \emph{october} & ``\textbf{8th}'' (\emph{octo}) \\
\emph{November} & \emph{november} & ``\textbf{9th}'' (\emph{novem}) \\
\emph{Dezember} & \emph{december} & ``\textbf{10th}'' (\emph{decem}) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{etymology}
\textbf{M\"{a}rz} $=$ \textbf{Mars.} German borrowed the Roman war-god's month
whole --- the same god behind English \textbf{March} and behind French
\emph{mardi}. And that same god stands behind German's own \emph{Dienstag}
(``Tuesday''), where the Romans' Mars was \emph{translated} into the Germanic
god Tiw. The god came in twice: once by name (\emph{M\"{a}rz}), once by
translation (\emph{Dienstag}).
\end{etymology}

\begin{etymology}
\textbf{September--Dezember} $=$ \textbf{7--10.} Even in German these keep the
Latin numbers \emph{septem}, \emph{octo}, \emph{novem}, \emph{decem} --- because
the Roman year began in March, so the ``seventh'' month was September. German's
\emph{own} numbers are \emph{sieben}, \emph{acht}, \emph{neun}, \emph{zehn} ---
but its \emph{month} names count in Latin.
\end{etymology}

\begin{culture}
Three layers of the German calendar, three origins: the \textbf{numbers} are
native, the \textbf{weekdays} are Germanic gods, and the \textbf{months} (along
with the clock-word \emph{Uhr}) are Latin.
\end{culture}

\section{\emph{die Jahreszeiten} --- the seasons go native}
\label{lesson:jahreszeiten}

The months were Latin. The \textbf{seasons} swing back to \textbf{native
German} --- and one of them, \emph{Herbst}, is the same word as English
\textbf{harvest}. The word for ``season'' itself is transparent:
\textbf{Jahres-zeit} $=$ ``year-time.''

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
German & built from & English twin \\
\midrule
\emph{der Fr\"{u}hling} (spring) & \emph{fr\"{u}h} ``early'' $+$ \emph{-ling} & the ``early''-season \\
\emph{der Sommer} (summer) & native Germanic & \textbf{summer} \\
\emph{der Herbst} (autumn) & native Germanic & \textbf{harvest} \\
\emph{der Winter} (winter) & native Germanic & \textbf{winter} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

All four are homegrown Germanic, and three are the plain twins of English
\emph{summer}, \emph{winter}, and --- the surprise --- \textbf{harvest}.

\begin{morphologybox}
\textbf{Fr\"{u}hling} is built from \textbf{fr\"{u}h} (``early''), a cousin of
English \textbf{early}, plus the ending \emph{-ling}: literally the
``early-season,'' the year's first. (German also has \textbf{der Lenz}, an old
poetic word for spring.)
\end{morphologybox}

\begin{cousinweb}
\textbf{Herbst} $\leftrightarrow$ \textbf{harvest}. They are the same Germanic
word: the \emph{reaping} season. English kept \emph{harvest} but narrowed it to
the \emph{act} of gathering, and reached for the Latin-derived \textbf{autumn}
to name the season; German kept the old word for the season itself. Two
languages, one word, split in meaning.
\end{cousinweb}

\begin{culture}
So the German year splits cleanly: \textbf{Latin} for the months (like
\emph{die Uhr}, the clock), \textbf{native Germanic} for the seasons (like the
numbers, and like \emph{Mittag} ``midday'' and \emph{Mitternacht}
``midnight'').
\end{culture}
