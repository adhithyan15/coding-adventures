# मेरे लिए — the same bend, in front of a different postposition

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[HI-LEX-C84-POSS-05] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say *mere pās*. Say why it is *mere* and not *merā*. The word in
this lesson works the same way, which is the whole point of putting them
together.

## You'll want to know: मेरे लिए
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[HI-LEX-C84-POSS-06]; assesses=[] -->

**मेरे लिए** (*mere liye*) — "for me."
*mere sāth* — "with me."

Three phrases, one shape. The possessive goes oblique and the postposition
follows:

| phrase | meaning |
|---|---|
| mere pās | near me — *I have* |
| mere liye | for me |
| mere *sāth* | with me |

## Grammar Lens: the pattern generalises to every person
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[HI-LEX-C84-POSS-06, HI-LEX-C84-POSS-05, HI-LEX-C84-POSS-02] -->

Once the bend is automatic, the whole grid follows without memorising a single
new form:

| owner | oblique | with *liye* |
|---|---|---|
| I | mere | mere liye |
| he, she | uske | uske liye |
| we | hamāre | hamāre liye |
| the subject's own | apne | apne liye |

The last row is the one both practice papers reach for. Phrases like *apne
dost ke sāth* — "with one's own friend" — put three of this chapter's ideas in
four words: the reflexive possessive, the oblique bend, and a two-word
postposition.
