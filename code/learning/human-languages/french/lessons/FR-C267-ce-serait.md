# ce serait — it would be

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[FR-GRAM-CONDITIONNEL-01, FR-GRAM-CEST-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Build "I would speak". (*Je parlerais.*) Then "it's good", now.
(*C'est bien.*)

## You'll want to know: ce serait
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[FR-LEX-CE-SERAIT-01]; assesses=[FR-ETYMON-ETRE-ESSE-07, FR-LEX-C99-VILLE51-04, FR-LEX-C100-VILLE52-01, FR-GRAM-PLUS-QUE-01] -->

**ce serait** — "it would be", "that would be".

**être** refused the imparfait's recipe and gave you *c'était*. Its
conditionnel has a stem of its own too, **ser-**, with the same endings:
*je serais*, *tu serais*, *il serait*. *C'est* is now, *c'était* was, and
***ce serait*** is the judgement on something that has not happened:

> ***Ce serait** bien.* — That would be good.
> ***Ce serait** plus rapide en train.* — It would be quicker by train.

*En train*, "by train", and *en voiture*, "by car", are how French says the
way you travel.

## Grammar Lens: si, the imparfait, and the would
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[FR-GRAM-SI-IMPARFAIT-02]; assesses=[FR-GRAM-SI-PRESENT-01, FR-LEX-CETAIT-01, FR-LEX-C242-VERBES242-03, FR-GRAM-IMPARFAIT-01] -->

Now the condition that is **not** true. Put the *si* half in the
**imparfait**, and the result in the **conditionnel**:

> ***Si j'avais** le temps, **je partirais** en vacances.* — If I had the
> time, I'd go on holiday.
> ***Si c'était** moins cher, **je prendrais** le train.* — If it were
> cheaper, I'd take the train.

Set it beside last chapter's real condition. *S'il pleut, je reste*: it
might rain, and here is the plan. *S'il pleuvait, je resterais*: it is not
raining, and I am imagining. The conditionnel goes in the result, and the
*si* half takes the imparfait.

In relaxed speech, in Québec and in France alike, you may hear *si j'aurais*
for "if I had"; standard French keeps the imparfait after *si*, and so should a
letter or an exam.

## The phrase, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[FR-ETYMON-CONDITIONNEL-02] -->

**être** is inherited, through Old French *estre*, from Latin *esse*, "to be"
(EtymDB 2.1). The stem **ser-** is usually traced to a spoken Latin
infinitive *essere*, reshaped from *esse*, with the worn *habēbat* of the
last lesson on its end: something like "had to be". Neither source records
forms this late, so that step is the usual explanation rather than a cited
one.
