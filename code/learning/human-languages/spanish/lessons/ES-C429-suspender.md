# suspender — to fail, and the picture the word is holding

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C429-EDU-04, ES-LEX-C429-EDU-02] -->

[PAUSE 2s] You can say *aprobar*. Guess its opposite, and then read on to see
what the Spanish word is actually picturing.

## You'll want to know: suspender
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-C429-EDU-05]; assesses=[] -->

**suspender** — to fail, as in to fail an exam or a subject.

*He suspendido el examen.* — "I have failed the exam." A mark below **five** does
it.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C429-EDU-05, ES-LEX-C429-EDU-04] -->

Latin **suspendere** — *sub-* ("beneath") plus **pendere**, "to hang." To
suspend a thing is to leave it **hanging**.

That is not a dead metaphor here. A failed subject in Spain is **not finished**:
it hangs over the student until a resit in a later term. There is even a word
for one — *una asignatura pendiente*, "a hanging subject" — which people also
use for anything in life they have been putting off.

**Pendere** built a shelf of words that all hang:

| word | what hangs |
|---|---|
| **pend**iente | a slope, an earring, a thing outstanding |
| de**pend**er | to hang from something else |
| **pens**ar | to weigh a thing in the mind |

English *pendant*, *pending*, *suspense* and *depend* hang from the same nail.

## Grammar Lens: the English false friend, and the Spanish one
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C429-EDU-05, ES-LEX-C429-EDU-04] -->

English *suspend* keeps only the harsher senses — a suspended player, a
suspended sentence. The school sense does not survive the crossing, so an
English speaker reading *he suspendido* will hear something far more dramatic
than what happened.

The pair, side by side:

| mark | verb |
|---|---|
| five or above | aprobar |
| below five | suspender |

Both verbs take the exam as their object, so the exam is what is passed or
failed, rather than the student.
