# la natación — the same root, arriving twice

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-NADAR, ES-LEX-C438-CUERPO-01] -->

door does not list *nadar*; it lists a noun.

## You'll want to know: la natación
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-C438-CUERPO-02]; assesses=[ES-LEX-NADAR] -->

**la natación** — swimming, the activity as a named thing.

*Hago natación los martes* — I swim on Tuesdays. *Un curso de natación* — a
swimming course. **nadar** is what your arms do; **la natación** is what the
timetable, the club and the medal call it.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C438-CUERPO-02] -->

**nat-** from Latin *natāre*, to swim, plus **-ción**, the ending you met on
*documentación* for turning an action into a thing.

*documentar* gave *documentación*; *natar* gives *natación*. Same machine.

## Why it's said this way
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C438-CUERPO-02, ES-LEX-NADAR] -->

Here is the part worth slowing down for. **nadar** and **la natación** come
from the *same* Latin word, and they do not look alike. That is because they
took different roads out of Latin.

| the word | the road it took |
|---|---|
| nadar | carried in every mouth, worn smooth |
| natación | lifted off the page, centuries later |

*natāre* was spoken without a break for a thousand years, and a thousand years
of speaking wore the **t** soft into a **d** and dropped a syllable: *natāre*
became *nadar*. Then scholars who could read Latin needed a formal noun, went
back to the written form, and took *natātiōnem* out intact.

A pair like this is called a **doublet**: one word, two arrivals, both alive.
Once you can see them, Spanish is full of them, and the learned twin is nearly
always the one on the official sign.
