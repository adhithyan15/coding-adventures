Malayalam, chapter 18: Telling Time.
3 lessons. You can do the first 2 of them in the car; after that you will want to have stopped.


Lesson 1 of 3.
ഒരു (oru) — one, when something follows it.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Count one to five out loud.
[pause 2 seconds]
Now say the first of them on its own: ഒന്ന്.

You'll want to know.
ഒരു (oru) — one, with a noun coming after it.
ഒന്ന് and ഒരു (oru) are one number wearing two shapes, and which shape you reach for depends on nothing more than whether a noun follows it:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
ഒന്ന്, standing alone — how many? one.
ഒരു (oru), leaning on a noun — ഒരു (oru) പേര്, one name.
You counted with the first. The second is the one you will meet on nearly every page from here, because a number usually has something to count.
Tamil keeps the same pair, oru against oṉṟu, split the same way — which is a sign the split is old rather than a tidy-up Malayalam did on its own.

The word, taken apart.
ഒരു (oru) is native Dravidian, the shape "one" takes in front of a noun. DEDR 990 lists Tamil oru before a consonant and ōr before a vowel, with Kannada ondu, "one thing".

Grammar Lens: the same word does the work of "a".
Malayalam has no word for a. A bare noun covers it: പേര് on its own is name, a name, or the name, and which one it is comes from what is being talked about.
But when a speaker wants to set one unspecified thing in front of you — the a that means some one or other — ഒരു (oru) is what they say. So the same small word carries two weights:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
counting, ഒരു (oru) പേര് — one name.
introducing, ഒരു (oru) പേര് — a name.
Nothing in the word changes between those two rows. The difference lives in why it was said, and a listener takes it from the situation — the same bargain Malayalam makes everywhere else it declines to mark something.
The next lesson puts a noun after it straight away, and when it does, this is the shape you will be reading.

[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: ഒരു (oru) പേര്, and both things it could mean]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: what a bare പേര് could mean with nothing in front of it]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Which shape of one stands alone? (ഒന്ന്.) Which leans on a noun? (ഒരു (oru).) Does Malayalam have a word for a? (No — but ഒരു (oru) does that work when a speaker wants it done.)


Lesson 2 of 3.
മണി (maṇi) — the bell that tells the hour.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
An hour you can hear: before clocks were everywhere, a bell struck it. Malayalam still names the hour after the bell.

You'll want to know.
മണി (maṇi) = "hour, o'clock", and also "bell, gong". The bell came first: the hour is the stroke of the bell that announces it.

Why it's said this way.
The Dravidian Etymological Dictionary lists this word as Dravidian (DEDR 4672): Tamil maṇi, "bell, gong, hour", Malayalam maṇi, "little bells, gong, hour by the bell", with Kota, Toda and Tulu bell words beside them. DEDR then adds a query: perhaps from Sanskrit maṇi, "jewel". It does not decide. Malayalam's own row hints at how the two could meet, since its little bells were worn as jewels, but that link stays an open question, not a finding.

Grammar Lens: telling the time.
ഒരു മണി. — "One o'clock." (oru maṇi, "one hour/bell") രണ്ട് മണി. — "Two o'clock." (raṇṭŭ maṇi, "two hour/bell").

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: "maṇi" — hour, and the bell that strikes it]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: "oru maṇi" — one o'clock]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: the honest point — DEDR lists maṇi as a Dravidian bell word shared with Tamil, and only queries a Sanskrit "jewel" source]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
What did maṇi name before it named the hour? (A bell or gong.) Which language family does DEDR put it in, and with which Tamil word? (Dravidian, with Tamil maṇi, "bell, gong, hour".) Is a link to Sanskrit maṇi, "jewel", settled? (No — DEDR marks it with a query.)

Script you'll notice: ഷ.
ഷ — ṣa.
It is a consonant, and in this script a consonant is never bare: it comes with an a already in it. So it is not ṣ, it is ṣa.
You already say these, and every one of them has ഷ (ṣa) somewhere inside it:
സന്തോഷം santōṣaṁ — joy / pleased to meet you.
ക്ഷമിക്കണം kṣamikkaṇaṁ — (you) should forgive / sorry.

Writing: ഷ — follow the numbered strip.
Follow the numbered strip: start where movement 1 begins, and let each panel show you the next movement before your pen makes it. Then write ഷ (ṣa) yourself — slowly, and larger than it is printed.
The strip shows where to start the character and which way to travel, in one attested order. That is taught with real variation from school to school, so the strip names its source beneath it: treat it as a sound way in, not the only one.
