French, chapter 10: Family.
2 lessons. All 2 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 2.
le père, la mère — the oldest words in the family.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
The words for "father" and "mother" are among the oldest words we can reconstruct — older than Latin, older than Rome. French kept them almost bare: père and mère. And they are secretly the same words as English father and mother.

Taken apart.
le père ("father") from Latin pater from PIE ph₂tḗr.
la mère ("mother") from Latin māter from PIE méh₂tēr.
Note the articles: le père (masculine), la mère (feminine) — the gender you have been tracking since Chapter 1.

The English connection (one sound-law).
French took pater/māter straight from Latin. English took the same PIE words down a different road, through Grimm's law, which turned Latin's p becomes f and t becomes th:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
PIE: ph₂tḗr. Latin / French: pater becomes père. English: father.
PIE: méh₂tēr. Latin / French: māter becomes mère. English: mother.
So père and father are not lookalikes borrowed late — they are the same inherited word, one keeping the p, the other shifting it to f. The Latin root also gives English its "of a parent" adjectives: paternal, paternity, patron and maternal, maternity, matron, matter (māter becomes materia, "stuff a tree-mother makes").

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Give "father" and "mother" with their articles. (le père, la mère.) What one sound-change makes père into English father? (Grimm's law: p becomes f — and t becomes th.) Name an English cousin of each. (père becomes paternal/patron; mère becomes maternal/matron.) Next: le frère, la sœur — brother and sister.


Lesson 2 of 2.
le frère, la sœur — brother and sister.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
With parents in hand, the next pair: frère ("brother") and sœur ("sister"). Both are straight from Latin, and both hand English a whole family of words.

Taken apart.
le frère ("brother") from Latin frāter from PIE bʰréh₂tēr (the same PIE root that, through Grimm's law bʰ becomes b, gave English brother).
la sœur ("sister") from Latin soror. The odd-looking œ (an o and e fused into one letter) spells the vowel that soror wore down to — soror becomes seror becomes sœur.

The English cousins.
frère becomes fraternal, fraternity, fraternize, and — softened in old French — friar (a "brother" in a religious order).
sœur becomes sorority, sororal; and a sister in a convent is still called a sœur. (English sister comes from the same PIE root swésōr, by the Germanic road — Latin soror and English sister are cousins.)
So the four family words now form a set: père, mère, frère, sœur — each one a key to a Latin-through-English word (paternal, maternal, fraternal, sorority).

Wrap-up Recall.
