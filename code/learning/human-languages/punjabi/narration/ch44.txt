The word, taken apart.
ਦੂਜਾ — dūjā — second.
Say ਦੋ (do), then ਦੂਜਾ (dūjā). The number has not gone anywhere: the ਦ is still at the front and the vowel has only lengthened.

Script — one tiny step.
Punjabi keeps two sets of number words, and they answer two different questions:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
the question: ਕਿੰਨੇ? — how many? the set: ਇੱਕ ਦੋ ਤਿੰਨ ਚਾਰ ਪੰਜ. example: ਦੋ ਰੋਟੀ — two rotis.
the question: which one in the line? the set: ਦੂਜਾ (dūjā) and its fellows. example: ਦੂਜੀ ਰੋਟੀ — the second roti.
ਕਿੰਨੇ, taught one lesson ago, is exactly the question the first set answers. This chapter teaches the other set.
An ordinal is an ordinary -ਾ adjective, so it agrees with what it counts: ਦੂਜਾ (dūjā) ਦੋਸਤ (the second friend), ਦੂਜੀ ਰੋਟੀ (the second roti).

The word, taken apart.
ਤੀਜਾ — tījā — third.
Put the two you now have beside the numbers under them:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
how many: ਦੋ (do). which one in the line: ਦੂਜਾ (dūjā).
how many: ਤਿੰਨ (tinn). which one in the line: ਤੀਜਾ (tījā).
The first letter survives in both, and the same -ਜਾ sits on the end of each.

What you've built.
That shared -ਜਾ is not a Punjabi invention and it is not a rule you can use. Wiktionary traces ਦੂਜਾ (dūjā) to Sanskrit dvitīya and ਤੀਜਾ (tījā) to Sanskrit tṛtīya — one Sanskrit ending, -tīya, worn down to -ਜਾ in two words that travelled together.
Two words is a resemblance, not a pattern, and this book will not call it one. Watch what the next number does with it.

The word, taken apart.
ਚੌਥਾ — chauthā — fourth.
ਚਾਰ (chār) gives ਚੌ- (chau-), and then the word ends in -ਥਾ.
The ਥ in it is the letter you wrote five lessons ago for ਕਿੱਥੇ. It is doing the same shape here in a word that has nothing to do with where.

What you've built.
Three ordinals now, and two endings:
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
how many: ਦੋ. which one in the line: ਦੂਜਾ (dūjā). ending: -ਜਾ.
how many: ਤਿੰਨ. which one in the line: ਤੀਜਾ (tījā). ending: -ਜਾ.
how many: ਚਾਰ. which one in the line: ਚੌਥਾ (chauthā). ending: -ਥਾ.
So -ਜਾ was a resemblance and not a rule, exactly as the last lesson refused to promise. Wiktionary says why: ਦੂਜਾ (dūjā) and ਤੀਜਾ (tījā) come from Sanskrit dvitīya and tṛtīya, and ਚੌਥਾ (chauthā) comes from Sanskrit caturtha — a different Sanskrit ending, worn down its own way.
These are not built words. They are inherited ones, each worn separately, which is why no rule joins them.

The word, taken apart.
ਪਹਿਲਾ — pahilā — first.
Now go looking for the number. ਦੂਜਾ (dūjā) kept its ਦ. ਤੀਜਾ (tījā) kept its ਤ. ਚੌਥਾ (chauthā) kept its ਚ. This word keeps nothing of ਇੱਕ — not a letter.

What you've built.
Four ordinals, three endings, and one of the four with no number in it at all:
[a table of 4 rows, read aloud]
how many: ਇੱਕ. which one in the line: ਪਹਿਲਾ (pahilā). the number, still visible?: no.
how many: ਦੋ. which one in the line: ਦੂਜਾ (dūjā). the number, still visible?: yes.
how many: ਤਿੰਨ. which one in the line: ਤੀਜਾ (tījā). the number, still visible?: yes.
how many: ਚਾਰ. which one in the line: ਚੌਥਾ (chauthā). the number, still visible?: yes.
That is why first arrives fourth in this chapter rather than opening it. A word can only be seen to BREAK a shape after the shape has been seen holding, and it had to hold three times before this one could be read as a surprise.
Wiktionary explains the surprise: ਪਹਿਲਾ (pahilā) goes back through Prakrit to Sanskrit prathama, which is pra- "forward" plus a superlative ending. It never meant one-th. It meant foremost — and English first is the superlative of fore, which is the identical idea reached on a different road.

The word, taken apart.
ਪੰਜਵਾਂ — panjvāṁ — fifth.
Say the four you have, then this one, and listen to the end of each:
pahilā, dūjā, tījā, chauthā … panjvāṁ.
The first four end in a plain -ਾ. This one ends in -ਵਾਂ — the ਾਂ carries a nasal, and you can hear it. The seam in this set is audible before it is explained.

What you've built: an inherited word that turned into a rule.
Wiktionary says two things about this word, and it is worth reading both, because they look like they contradict each other and do not.
ਪੰਜਵਾਂ (panjvāṁ) is inherited — from Prakrit paṃcama, from Sanskrit pañcama. Like the other four, it came down whole.
"By surface analysis", it is ਪੰਜ + -ਵਾਂ.
Both are true, and the second is what happened AFTER the first. The word arrived in one piece; then speakers looked at it, saw their own word for five sitting at the front, took the rest to be an ending, and started using that ending on other numbers.
So the seam in this chapter is not where Punjabi stopped inheriting. Every one of these five words was inherited. The seam is where the inheriting stopped being visible — where a whole word came to look like a sum, and the language believed it.
That is why -ਵਾਂ goes on numbers this book has not taught you yet, and -ਜਾ and -ਥਾ go on nothing at all.

What you've built.
Four endings across five words:
[a table of 5 rows, read aloud]
ordinal: ਪਹਿਲਾ (pahilā). ending: -ਲਾ. comes from: Sanskrit prathama, "foremost".
ordinal: ਦੂਜਾ (dūjā). ending: -ਜਾ. comes from: Sanskrit dvitīya.
ordinal: ਤੀਜਾ (tījā). ending: -ਜਾ. comes from: Sanskrit tṛtīya.
ordinal: ਚੌਥਾ (chauthā). ending: -ਥਾ. comes from: Sanskrit caturtha.
ordinal: ਪੰਜਵਾਂ (panjvāṁ). ending: -ਵਾਂ. comes from: Sanskrit pañcama.
Every row of that last column is a whole word handed down, not a sum. So the useful question about this set is not which ones were built — none of them were. It is which ending got a second life, and the answer is the one at the bottom: -ਵਾਂ is the only one a Punjabi speaker can put on a number they have never made an ordinal of before.
