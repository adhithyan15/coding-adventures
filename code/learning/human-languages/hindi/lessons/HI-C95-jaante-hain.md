# क्या आप जानते हैं? — asking it

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[HI-LEX-C95-KNOW-01, HI-CONCEPT-C02-KYA-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say *I know*. Then say the little word that turns a Hindi statement
into a yes-or-no question.

## You'll want to know: जानते हैं
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[HI-LEX-C95-KNOW-02]; assesses=[] -->

**क्या आप जानते हैं?** — Do you know?

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[HI-LEX-C95-KNOW-02, HI-LEX-C95-KNOW-01] -->

Every piece is old and the assembly is regular:

| piece | job |
|---|---|
| क्या | marks the whole thing as a question |
| आप | the respectful *you* |
| जानते | the verb, agreeing with **आप** |
| हैं | *to be*, also agreeing with **आप** |

**आप is grammatically plural**, which is why both the verb and *to be* take
their plural endings even when you are speaking to one person. That is not a
new rule — it is the same respect-as-plural the track has used since the
second chapter, turning up on a new verb.

## Grammar Lens: where क्या sits
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[HI-LEX-C95-KNOW-02, HI-CONCEPT-C02-KYA-01] -->

**क्या** goes at the **front**, and the rest of the sentence keeps its ordinary
order behind it. English reshuffles a question — *you know* becomes *do you
know* — and Hindi does not move anything at all.

So a yes-or-no question costs **one word and no rearrangement**, which makes it
one of the cheapest things in the language to build once you can say the
statement.
