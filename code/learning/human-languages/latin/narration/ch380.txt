Latin, chapter 380: Hiring and Hard Labour.
6 lessons. All 6 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 6.
condūcō, condūcere — to hire, to rent; to bring together.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for toilsome, then the Latin for care.

You'll want to know: condūcō, condūcere.
condūcō, condūcere — "to hire, to rent; to bring together".
Agricola operāriōs condūxit. The farmer hired labourers.
condūcō, condūcere, condūxī, conductum. The man who hires or rents is the conductor. English conduct.

The word, taken apart.
condūcō is built from con- "together" and dūcō "to lead" (EtymDB; Lewis & Short): to lead workers together. English conduct comes from it.

Lesson 2 of 6.
locō, locāre — to place; to let out, to hire out.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for care, then the Latin for to hire.

You'll want to know: locō, locāre.
locō, locāre — "to place; to let out, to hire out".
Dominus domum suam mercēde magnā locāvit. The owner let his house for a high rent.
locō, locāre, locāvī, locātum, built on locus. In Roman law locāre is to let out and condūcere to take on hire. English locate.

The word, taken apart.
locō is built on locus "a place" (EtymDB; Lewis & Short): to put in place, then to let out for hire. English locate comes from it.

Lesson 3 of 6.
famulus, famulī — a household servant, an attendant.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to hire, then the Latin for to place.

You'll want to know: famulus, famulī.
famulus, famulī — "a household servant, an attendant".
Famulus cēnam in mēnsā posuit. The servant laid dinner on a table.
famulus, famulī m. Familia first meant the household's servants, the famulī. English family and familiar come from that word.

The word, taken apart.
famulus is inherited from Proto-Italic famelos (EtymDB), and familia "a household" is built on it (Lewis & Short). English family and familiar come from familia.

Lesson 4 of 6.
nāvō, nāvāre — to do with energy (operam nāvāre: to work hard).

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to place, then the Latin for a household servant.

You'll want to know: nāvō, nāvāre.
nāvō, nāvāre — "to do with energy (operam nāvāre: to work hard)".
Amīcō operam nāvāvit. He worked hard for his friend.
nāvō, nāvāre, nāvāvī, nāvātum, built on nāvus (old gnāvus), busy; ignāvus, lazy, is the opposite. It mostly appears in operam nāvāre + dative.

The word, taken apart.
nāvō is built on nāvus, old gnāvus "busy, active" (EtymDB; Lewis & Short), the adjective that ignāvus "lazy" denies.

Lesson 5 of 6.
fatīgō, fatīgāre — to tire out, to weary.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a household servant, then the Latin for to do with energy.

You'll want to know: fatīgō, fatīgāre.
fatīgō, fatīgāre — "to tire out, to weary".
Longum iter equōs fatīgāvit. The long journey tired out the horses.
fatīgō, fatīgāre, fatīgāvī, fatīgātum. English fatigue.

The word, taken apart.
fatīgō is linked by Lewis & Short with affatim "enough" and fatīscō "to grow weak", but its full origin is not settled. English fatigue comes from it through French.
