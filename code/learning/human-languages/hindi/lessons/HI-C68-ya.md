# … या … (*yā*) — "or"

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[HI-JOIN-AUR-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] One lesson ago you got *aur*, which adds. Say *chāy aur dūdh*. Now
the word that does the opposite: it puts two things side by side and asks the
listener to take exactly one.

## You'll want to know: या
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[HI-JOIN-YA-01]; assesses=[HI-CONCEPT-C37-CHAI-01, HI-CONCEPT-C37-DUDH-01] -->

> **या** — *yā* — **or**

> **चाय या दूध?** — *chāy yā dūdh?* — **Tea or milk?**

It sits exactly where *aur* sat. The two are a matched pair: same slot, opposite
job. *aur* hands you both; **या** makes you choose.

Two syllables of good news about the writing: **या** is **य** plus the long
**ा**, and you have had both for a long time. Unlike *aur*, you can write this
one today.

Offering a choice is a whole conversational move, not just a word. **चाय या
दूध?** is a complete, polite offer with no verb in it at all, and it is how a
host asks and how an examiner's role-play begins.

**या** is Sanskrit **वा** (*vā*), the ancient particle of alternatives, with the
*v-* softened to *y-*. Latin kept the same particle as the **-ve** in *sīve*,
"or if," and Marathi still carries it compounded inside **किंवा** — *kim* +
*vā*, "what-or." One old word, three descendants.
