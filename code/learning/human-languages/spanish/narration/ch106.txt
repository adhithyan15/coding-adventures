hecho, dicho, visto, puesto — four participles that refused the ending — and they are the four verbs you would have guessed.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
By the rule, the participle of hacer should be hacido. Say it. Now be told that nobody has ever said it.

Grammar Lens: four, and you could have guessed which.
[a table of 4 rows, read aloud]
verb: hacer. participle: hecho. in use: he hecho — I have done.
verb: decir. participle: dicho. in use: he dicho — I have said.
verb: ver. participle: visto. in use: he visto — I have seen.
verb: poner. participle: puesto. in use: he puesto — I have put.
Look at that list of verbs before you look at the forms. Do, say, see, put — four of the commonest verbs in any language, and the same four that have been irregular in every tense you have met. The verbs that break rules are not a random selection: they are always the ones said most often, because frequent words are the only ones used enough to survive being irregular.
You already know this in English. Go/went, see/saw, do/did, put/put — and nobody struggles with them, because you meet them a hundred times a day. The same will happen here.

The word, taken apart.
These four are not broken. They are older than the rule.
hecho from Latin factum — the thing done. English fact is that same word: a fact is literally a thing that has been done.
dicho from Latin dictum — the thing said. English keeps dictum whole, and dictation, verdict ("a true saying"), contradict.
visto from Latin vīsum — the thing seen. English vision, visible.
puesto from Latin positum — the thing placed. English position, deposit, compose.
Latin already had participles for these verbs, and Spanish inherited them intact instead of rebuilding them from the stem. The regular -ado / -ido forms are the newer machinery; these four are what was there before it.
So an irregular participle is not a form that went wrong. It is a form that was never replaced.
