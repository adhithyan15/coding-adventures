# हमें / तुम्हें — what mujhe was all along

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[HI-CONCEPT-C35-PASAND-01, HI-LEX-C86-PRON-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say *mujhe … pasand hai*. You learned it as a frame for saying what
you like. Say the first word on its own and ask what it actually is.

## You'll want to know: हमें and तुम्हें
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[HI-LEX-C86-PRON-03]; assesses=[] -->

**हमें** (*hamẽ*) — "to us."
**तुम्हें** (*tumhẽ*) — "to you," at the *tum* level.

*Mujhe* is the same kind of word: **मैं** in its oblique form, meaning *to me*.
The liking frame was never a lump — it was a pronoun in a case, and now the
rest of the set arrives with it.

## Grammar Lens: the same bend, a third time
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[HI-LEX-C86-PRON-03, HI-LEX-C86-PRON-01] -->

| subject | oblique | meaning |
|---|---|---|
| मैं | *mujhe* | to me |
| हम | **हमें** | to us |
| तुम | **तुम्हें** | to you |

The possessive chapter bent **-आ** to **-ए** in front of a postposition. The
imperative chapter bent the verb to match the listener. Here the pronoun itself
bends, and the ending it takes is the nasal **-ें**.

That is three separate systems doing the same thing, which is the reason Hindi
rewards a learner who looks for patterns rather than lists: the language keeps
asking the same question — *what stands after this word?* — and answering it the
same way.
