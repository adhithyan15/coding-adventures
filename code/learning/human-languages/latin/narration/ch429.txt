Latin, chapter 429: Wagon, Light gig, War chariot, Covered carriage, Travelling coach.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
carrus, carrī — a wagon, a baggage cart.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to travel abroad, then the Latin for to go forward.

You'll want to know: carrus, carrī.
carrus, carrī — "a wagon, a baggage cart".
Carrus frūmentum ex agrīs ad oppidum vehit. The wagon carries grain from the fields to the town.
Second declension, masculine: carrus, carrī m. (also carrum, carrī n.). A heavy cart for goods. English car, carry and career come from it.

The word, taken apart.
carrus was borrowed from Gaulish karros 'wagon', from PIE ḱers- 'to run' (EtymDB); Lewis & Short give no origin. English car, carry and career go back to it.

Lesson 2 of 5.
cisium, cisiī — a light gig, a fast two-wheeled cart.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to go forward, then the Latin for a wagon.

You'll want to know: cisium, cisiī.
cisium, cisiī — "a light gig, a fast two-wheeled cart".
Cisium celere est; itaque mercātor cisiō ad urbem it. A gig is fast; so the merchant goes to the city by gig.
Second declension, neuter: cisium, cisiī n. A light two-wheeled carriage for one or two people, for speed rather than baggage.

The word, taken apart.
The origin of cisium is not settled. Lewis & Short give the sense, a light two-wheeled vehicle, but no source, and EtymDB does not list it.

Lesson 3 of 5.
essedum, essedī — a two-wheeled chariot; a light travelling carriage.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a wagon, then the Latin for a light gig.

You'll want to know: essedum, essedī.
essedum, essedī — "a two-wheeled chariot; a light travelling carriage".
Dīves essedō celerī ad vīllam vehitur. The rich man rides to his country house in a fast two-wheeled carriage.
Second declension, neuter: essedum, essedī n. First a war chariot of northern peoples, later a smart carriage in the city.

The word, taken apart.
essedum names the war chariot of the Gauls and Britons (Lewis & Short, under essedārius), later a travelling carriage. It is usually thought to be a Gaulish word, but neither Lewis & Short nor EtymDB states an origin, so the line is hedged.

Lesson 4 of 5.
carpentum, carpentī — a covered carriage; a two-wheeled coach.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a light gig, then the Latin for a two-wheeled chariot.

You'll want to know: carpentum, carpentī.
carpentum, carpentī — "a covered carriage; a two-wheeled coach".
Māter et fīliae carpentō ad vīllam vehuntur. The mother and her daughters ride to the country house in a covered carriage.
Second declension, neuter: carpentum, carpentī n. Married women of rank rode in it. English carpenter first meant a maker of carriages.

The word, taken apart.
carpentum was borrowed from Gaulish carbantos 'two-wheeled carriage' (EtymDB); Lewis & Short give no origin. English carpenter comes, through French, from Latin carpentārius 'carriage-maker', built on it.

Lesson 5 of 5.
carrūca, carrūcae — a travelling coach, a four-wheeled carriage.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a two-wheeled chariot, then the Latin for a covered carriage.

You'll want to know: carrūca, carrūcae.
carrūca, carrūcae — "a travelling coach, a four-wheeled carriage".
Dīves in carrūcā sedet et servī post eum ambulant. The rich man sits in his coach and the slaves walk behind him.
First declension, feminine: carrūca, carrūcae f. A grand four-wheeled coach. In French it later became charrue, a plough.

The word, taken apart.
carrūca is built on carrus 'wagon' (EtymDB), which was borrowed from Gaulish karros. Lewis & Short gloss it as a four-wheeled travelling and state coach.
