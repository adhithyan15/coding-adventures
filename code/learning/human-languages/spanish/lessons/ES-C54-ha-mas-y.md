# ha + y — the fossil in a three-letter word

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-HAY, ES-LEX-HABER, ES-GRAMMAR-PERFECT-COMPLETE] -->

[PAUSE 2s] *Hay* looks like nothing. Say the *haber* form for he/she. (*Ha*.)
Now look at *hay* again with that in mind.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-ETYMON-HAY-IBI]; assesses=[ES-ETYMON-HABERE, ES-LEX-HABER, ES-GRAMMAR-HAY-NO-AGREEMENT] -->

*Hay* is two words fused:

> **ha** + **y**

The *ha* you already hold: it is *haber*, third person. The **y** is the
interesting half. It is Latin **ibi**, meaning **"there"** — and Spanish
otherwise **lost that word completely.**

There is no *y* meaning "there" anywhere else in modern Spanish. It survives in
exactly one place, welded to the front of a verb, because words that get glued
to something else stop being available to be replaced. *Hay* is a museum case
with one object in it.

So the literal reading is: **"it has there."** Which is a strange way to say
something exists, until you notice that the neighbours do the same:

- **French: *il y a*** — "it there has." Same *haber* verb, same *ibi*, same
  order, three words instead of one. French kept its *y* separate, so its
  fossil is still visible on the surface.
- **English: *there is*** — which also starts with a place-word doing a job
  that has nothing to do with place. Nobody is pointing anywhere when they say
  *there is a problem*.

Three languages, three different verbs, and all of them reached for a word
meaning **"there"** to announce that something exists. That is not borrowing;
it is the same instinct arriving three times.
