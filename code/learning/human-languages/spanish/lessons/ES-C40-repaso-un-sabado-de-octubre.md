# Review — one Saturday, from morning to midnight

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-WEEKEND-01, ES-LEX-MONTHS-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] The first day of the weekend. (*El sábado*.) The month before
*noviembre*. (*Octubre*.)

## What you've built
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-SEASONS-01, ES-LEX-MONTHS-01, ES-GRAMMAR-TELLING-TIME-03, ES-LEX-VEINTE-06, ES-GRAMMAR-WEATHER-05, ES-LEX-AMARILLO-03, ES-LEX-NOON-MIDNIGHT-01] -->

Every piece below has been a separate chapter. Here they are as one day.

> *Es **sábado**. Es **octubre**, y es **otoño**.*
> *Son las nueve y **veinte**. **Llueve**.*
> *Al **mediodía** el cielo está **amarillo**.*
> *A **medianoche**, todavía **llueve**.*

[PAUSE 3s] Read it again and count what it took: a day name, a month, a season,
an hour, the minutes, a weather verb with nothing in front of it, two halves of
the day, and a colour. Nine chapters, four lines.

## Why it's said this way
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-CULTURE-ROMAN-MONTH-NAMES, ES-LEX-MONTHS-01] -->

One thing in that story is quietly wrong, and it has been wrong for two
thousand years.

[PAUSE 2s] ***Octubre*** carries *ocho* inside it. Eight. And it is the
**tenth** month.

The Roman year opened in March, so the counting started there: *septiembre*
seventh, *octubre* eighth, *noviembre* ninth, *diciembre* tenth. Then the year
was moved to January, everything slid by two, and nobody renamed anything.

So four of your months are still counting from a new year that stopped
happening — which is why you can read them without being told what they mean.
