# rechazar — to turn it down

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C456-CUENTA-03, ES-LEX-C458-PLAZO-03] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say **aceptar** and **admitir**. The word coming next undoes the
first, and it is made of the same Latin.

## You'll want to know: rechazar
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-C461-DONAR-03]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C456-CUENTA-03] -->

**rechazar** — to turn down, to reject, to refuse.

*Rechazar una oferta* — to turn down an offer. *Han rechazado la solicitud* —
the application has been rejected. *El banco rechazó la tarjeta* — the card was
declined.

**El rechazo** is the noun: a refusal, and in medicine the rejection of a
transplant.

## Grammar Lens: the three verbs of the counter, completed
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C461-DONAR-03, ES-LEX-C458-PLAZO-03] -->

| the move | the verb |
|---|---|
| you agree to what is offered | **aceptar** |
| the institution lets it through | **admitir** |
| it is turned back | **rechazar** |

*Rechazar* is the mirror of **both** of the others, and which one it mirrors
depends on who is doing it. A shop *rechaza* your card where it might have
*aceptado* it; an office *rechaza* your form where it might have *admitido* it.

Notices usually phrase a refusal by negating the other two — *no se aceptan*,
*no se admiten* — but *rechazar* takes the same *se* form when the refusing
itself is the point: *se rechazan los rotos*. In the plain active it is a
person who does it: *rechacé la oferta*.

Note the spelling: **rechacé**, with a **c**. Spanish spelling prefers **c**
before *e*, so a stem *z* turns into a *c* right through the conjugation, which
is why *empezar* gives *empecé*. A handful of borrowings keep the *z* — *zen*,
*zeta* — but no verb ending does.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C461-DONAR-03, ES-LEX-C461-DONAR-02] -->

**re-** plus *chazar*, to drive back — a verb Spanish took from Old French
*chacier*, and behind that Vulgar Latin **captiare**, to chase, built on
**capere**, **to take**. That French leg is why this word has **ch**: Spanish's
own inherited form of *captiare* is **cazar**, to hunt.

So *rechazar* and **aceptar** are cousins, and the pair is worth holding: *ad-*
plus *capere* is taking a thing **towards** you, and *re-* plus *captiare* is
driving it **back**. One Latin verb of taking, pointed two ways by two
prefixes.

The same *captiare* gave Spanish **cazar**, to hunt, and French *chasser*,
which English borrowed as **chase** — and, through a second borrowing, as
**catch**. To reject something is to chase it back the way it came.
