# s'il pleut, je reste — the condition that really happens

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[FR-GRAM-SI-01, FR-LEX-IL-FAISAIT-01, FR-LEX-IL-PLEUT-04] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say "if". (*Si.*) Say "it was raining". (*Il pleuvait.*) Now the
present: "it's raining". (*Il pleut.*)

## Grammar Lens: both halves in the present
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[FR-GRAM-SI-PRESENT-01]; assesses=[FR-LEX-C100-VILLE52-01, FR-LEX-PRENDRE-01, FR-LEX-JE-DEVRAIS-01, FR-LEX-C54-SANTE6-05] -->

> **S'il pleut, je reste** à la maison. — If it rains, I stay at home.
> **Si le train est cher, je prends** la voiture. — If the train is
> expensive, I take the car.

This is a **real** condition, true now or whenever it happens. Both halves
take the plain present, as in English: nothing new to build.

The result half can also be advice, with the *tu devrais* of chapter two
hundred and sixty-one, or a command:

> *Si tu es malade, **tu devrais** dormir.* — If you're ill, you should
> sleep.
> *Si tu es fatigué, **dors** !* — If you're tired, sleep!

## Why it's said this way
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[FR-LEX-RATE-LE-TRAIN-01, FR-GRAM-DEUX-FLECHES-01, FR-LEX-C106-TRAJET58-02] -->

A real condition is a plan with a branch in it. Someone who takes the train
to work every day lives by two of them:

> *Si je rate le train, je prends le métro. Si je rate le métro, je reste à
> la maison.*

Compare *donc*. *Il pleut, donc je reste* says it **is** raining. *S'il
pleut, je reste* says it **might**, and here is what happens if so. Same
consequence; the second has not decided whether the cause is true.

## Grammar Lens: tomorrow, still in the present
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[FR-GRAM-SI-DEMAIN-02]; assesses=[FR-LEX-ADEMAIN-05, FR-GRAM-FUTUR-PROCHE-01] -->

For one particular occasion still to come, the result can move into the
future, with the *aller* + infinitive you know. The *si* half does not move:

> *S'il pleut **demain**, je **vais rester** à la maison.* — If it rains
> tomorrow, I'm going to stay at home.

*Il pleut*, the present, even about tomorrow. English does the same, "if it
rains tomorrow", so here the ear is on your side.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[FR-ETYMON-ETRE-STARE-09] -->

**rester** goes back to Latin *restāre* (EtymDB 2.1), "to stay behind, to
hold still" (Lewis & Short, under *resto*): *re-*, "back", plus *stāre*, "to
stand", the standing verb behind *j'étais*. To stay is to stand still while
the others go on.

## Wrap-up Recall
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[FR-GRAM-SI-PRESENT-01, FR-GRAM-SI-DEMAIN-02, FR-GRAM-SI-01] -->

[PAUSE 3s] Which tense goes in the *si* half of a real condition? (The
**present**, even for tomorrow.) What is the difference between *il pleut,
donc je reste* and *s'il pleut, je reste*? (The first says it **is**
raining; the second has **not decided**.) What is *rester*, taken apart?
(***Re-*** and ***stāre***: to stand back.)
