# तक — where it stops

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[HI-LEX-C90-ROUT-01, HI-LEX-C88-TIME-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say the word that marks where something comes **from**. Then say *at
five o'clock*. One of those two words has been doing its job alone.

## You'll want to know: तक
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[HI-LEX-C94-SPAN-01]; assesses=[] -->

**पाँच बजे तक** — until five o'clock.

**से** marks a **start**. **तक** marks a **stop**.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[HI-LEX-C94-SPAN-01, HI-LEX-C90-ROUT-01] -->

| word | what it marks |
|---|---|
| से | the point something begins at |
| तक | the point something ends at |

Both are **postpositions**, so both come **after** the word they attach to.
English puts *until* in front and Hindi puts **तक** behind, and that is not a
quirk of this word — it is the same fact about the language that put **के** in
the middle of every phrase in the last chapter.

**तक** also stretches across space, not only time: **घर तक** is *as far as the
house*. One word covers both, because a stopping point is a stopping point
whether you measure it in hours or in streets.

## Grammar Lens: it does not have to be a pair
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[HI-LEX-C94-SPAN-01, HI-LEX-C88-TIME-01] -->

**पाँच बजे तक** on its own is a complete and ordinary thing to say. It names
only the end, and leaves the beginning to be understood — usually *now*.

That matters because the next lesson pairs the two words, and a reader who has
only ever seen the pair will not recognise **तक** standing by itself on a sign
or in a notice, which is where it most often appears.
