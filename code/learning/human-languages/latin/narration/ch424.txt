Latin, chapter 424: Crossroads, Fork in the road, Bend, Winding, To bend.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
compitum, compitī — a crossroads, a place where roads meet.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a slope, then the Latin for to step.

You'll want to know: compitum, compitī.
compitum, compitī — "a crossroads, a place where roads meet".
Ad compitum viātor stat et viam quaerit. At the crossroads the traveller stops and looks for the road.
Second declension, neuter: compitum, compitī n. Small altars to the gods of the crossroads stood there.

The word, taken apart.
compitum is usually explained as built from com- 'together' and the root of petō 'to make for', a place where ways come together. Lewis & Short derive it from competō 'to come together'; EtymDB lists it without a parent.

Lesson 2 of 5.
bivium, biviī — a fork in the road, a place where two roads meet.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to step, then the Latin for a crossroads.

You'll want to know: bivium, biviī.
bivium, biviī — "a fork in the road, a place where two roads meet".
In biviō duae viae sunt: ūna ad mare, altera ad montēs. At the fork there are two roads: one to the sea, the other to the mountains.
Second declension, neuter: bivium, biviī n. Compare trivium, where three roads meet.

The word, taken apart.
bivium is built on bi- 'two' and via 'road' (Lewis & Short, under the adjective bivius 'having two ways'). It is a native Latin compound.

Lesson 3 of 5.
flexus, flexūs — a bend, a turn (in a road or river).

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a crossroads, then the Latin for a fork in the road.

You'll want to know: flexus, flexūs.
flexus, flexūs — "a bend, a turn (in a road or river)".
Post flexum viae oppidum appāret. After the bend in the road the town comes into view.
Fourth declension, masculine: flexus, flexūs m. English flex and reflex belong to the same family.

The word, taken apart.
flexus is built on flectō 'to bend' (EtymDB; Lewis & Short), a fourth-declension noun of action made from the verb's stem flex-. English flex comes from the same verb.

Lesson 4 of 5.
anfrāctus, anfrāctūs — a winding, a twisting stretch of road.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a fork in the road, then the Latin for a bend.

You'll want to know: anfrāctus, anfrāctūs.
anfrāctus, anfrāctūs — "a winding, a twisting stretch of road".
Via per montēs multīs anfrāctibus it. The road goes through the mountains with many windings.
Fourth declension, masculine: anfrāctus, anfrāctūs m. Also a long, roundabout way of speaking.

The word, taken apart.
anfrāctus is built on am(b)- 'around' and frangō 'to break' (Lewis & Short; EtymDB lists frangō, from PIE bʰreg- 'to break'): a road that keeps breaking round in turns.

Lesson 5 of 5.
flectō, flectere — to bend, to turn.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a bend, then the Latin for a winding.

You'll want to know: flectō, flectere.
flectō, flectere — "to bend, to turn".
Agricola bovēs dextrā flectit, et plaustrum in viam angustam intrat. The farmer turns the oxen to the right, and the wagon goes into the narrow road.
flectō, flectere, flexī, flexum. Iter flectere, to change the direction of a journey. English flexible and reflect come from it.

The word, taken apart.
flectō is inherited from Proto-Italic flektō (EtymDB). English flexible, reflect and inflection all come from it (Lewis & Short).
