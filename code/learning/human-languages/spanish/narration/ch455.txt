Spanish, chapter 455: La habitación.
7 lessons. You can do the first 4 of them in the car; after that you will want to have stopped.


Lesson 1 of 7.
pintar — to paint.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say verde and arreglar. One way of fixing a room is to change its colour.

You'll want to know: pintar.
pintar — to paint, both walls and pictures.
Voy a pintar la cocina de verde — I'm going to paint the kitchen green. Recién pintado — wet paint, the wording on the sign. ¿Quién pintó esto? — who painted this?
Note the de in pintar de verde. Spanish marks the resulting colour with de, where English puts the adjective straight after the object.
La pintura is both the paint in the tin and the painting on the wall.

Grammar Lens: recién plus a participle.
Recién means "newly" or "a moment ago", and it attaches only to participles:
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
recién pintado means wet paint.
recién llegado means newly arrived.
recién casados means newlyweds.
In Spain it never stands alone and never modifies a normal adjective — recién limpio is not said. Take it as half of a two-word unit whose other half ends in -ado or -ido.
Much of Latin America is freer with it. Recién llegué — I arrived a moment ago — and recién ayer are ordinary in the Southern Cone, where recién behaves like a plain adverb of time.
The participle agrees: recién pintada for la pared, recién pintados for los bancos.

The word, taken apart.
Latin pingere, to paint — a verb that also meant to embroider, and behind both of those an older word for pricking or cutting a mark into a surface.
So painting and needlework were one verb in Latin, because both put a design onto cloth. The relatives come off the painting side: English picture, pigment, paint and pictorial, and Spanish pintoresco — worth-painting — which English borrowed through French as picturesque.
The Latin participle pictus is where the harder consonants survived: Spanish kept the soft, spoken pintar and borrowed the bookish pictórico alongside it. Another pair arriving twice, by the two roads.

Lesson 2 of 7.
el dormitorio — the bedroom.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say dormir and el piso. Name the room in the second where you do the first.

You'll want to know: el dormitorio.
el dormitorio — the bedroom.
Un piso de dos dormitorios — a two-bedroom flat, the wording in every advert. El dormitorio da al patio — the bedroom looks onto the courtyard.
La habitación also covers it in speech — mi habitación is what a child says — but a property listing counts dormitorios, because habitación means any room at all.

Grammar Lens: the vowel that has been breaking all along.
You met duermo early on, as a verb that changes its stem. It is the same single rule you have now seen in a noun, an adjective and a conjugation:
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
plain o: dormir, dormitorio. stressed, so ue: duermo.
plain o: rodar. stressed, so ue: la rueda.
plain o: mostrar. stressed, so ue: muestro.
Dormitorio keeps the plain o because the stress lands on the -to-, three syllables along. Say dormitorio and duermo one after the other and you can hear the vowel change as the stress moves.
Nothing here is irregular. One vowel, one condition, and it has been running under the language since the first weeks of it.

The word, taken apart.
Latin dormitorium, a sleeping place — from dormire, to sleep.
-orio names the place where something is done, and once you have it you can read a lot of signs:
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
word: el dormitorio. the place for: sleeping.
word: el laboratorio. the place for: working.
word: el velatorio. the place for: keeping watch over the dead.
English took dormitorium whole as dormitory, and shortened it to dorm. It also has dormant, lying asleep, and the dormouse, named for how much of the year it spends doing exactly that.

Lesson 3 of 7.
claro — light, and clear.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say sí, claro and verde. You have been saying the first for a very long time. This lesson is about what its second word means.

You'll want to know: claro.
claro — light, pale, bright, clear.
Un dormitorio muy claro — a very light bedroom, one that gets the sun. Verde claro — light green. Azul claro — pale blue. The opposite is oscuro, dark.
Beyond colour: el agua está clara — the water is clear. No me queda claro — I'm not clear about it.

Grammar Lens: the word you have been using without meeting.
Sí, claro has been in your mouth since early on, as a way of agreeing — "yes, of course". You were told then that it is Latin clarus, bright — but never that you could point it at a wall, because the phrase does not need the colour sense to work.
It is the same word. Claro on its own means it's clear, and agreeing with somebody by saying "clear!" is how Spanish arrives at "of course".
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
what you say: sí, claro. what it is doing: of course — the fixed phrase.
what you say: un cuarto claro. what it is doing: a light room — the adjective.
what you say: claro que sí. what it is doing: of course — the phrase, emphasised.
This is the fourth time a fixed phrase has turned out to have an ordinary word inside it. Mira gave you mirar, el punto gave apuntar, interesante gave interesar, and now sí, claro gives claro.
Check every new word against the phrases you already say. By this stage of a language, a surprising share of new vocabulary is not new.

Grammar Lens: colour words with claro and oscuro.
A colour modified by claro or oscuro stops agreeing:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
one thing: una pared verde. more than one: unas paredes verdes.
one thing: una pared verde claro. more than one: unas paredes verde claro.
Add the shade and the pair freezes. The reason is that verde claro is standing in for "of a light green colour" — a description of the shade rather than of the walls — and Spanish leaves such compounds unchanged.

Lesson 4 of 7.
tender — hang it out.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say la tienda and limpio. A shop is about to turn out to be a stretched thing.

You'll want to know: tender.
tender — to hang out, to spread, to stretch out.
Voy a tender la ropa — I'm going to hang the washing out. El tendedero — the airer or the washing line. Tender la mano — to hold out your hand.
In a flat with no garden this is daily vocabulary, and the sign in a stairwell saying no tender ropa en la fachada is asking you not to hang washing on the front of the building.

Grammar Lens: tender is an e-to-ie verb.
The same break you have watched all chapter, one vowel over:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
stress on the stem: tiendo, tiendes. stress on the ending: tendemos.
stress on the stem: tiende, tienden. stress on the ending: tendéis.
And now say tiendo and la tienda together. They are the same shape, because la tienda is this verb: a shop was a stretched awning, a cloth tent pulled open over a market stall.
The shop chapter named that cloth when it taught la tienda; here is the verb it was named from, so the noun and its verb finally sit together. Tienda de campaña — a camping tent — kept the original object entirely.

The word, taken apart.
Latin tendere, to stretch.
The family in English is long: tend, tent, tension, extend, intend, pretend — to stretch something out in front of you — attention, a stretching towards.
Spanish has the whole set too: tender, la tienda, extender, pretender, la tensión, atender. Every one of them is something being pulled taut, either a cloth or a mind.

Lesson 5 of 7.
meter — put it in.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, because the lesson points at something written down. You can listen to all of it now and come back to the parts that need looking at once you have stopped.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say el permiso and la caja. There is a Latin verb inside the first, and you are about to use it on the second.

You'll want to know: meter.
meter — to put in, to put inside.
Mete la ropa en la caja — put the clothes in the box. Meter la llave — put the key in. Se ha metido en la cama — he's got into bed.
Meterse also does the social senses: no te metas — don't interfere. Meterse con alguien — to pick on somebody.

Grammar Lens: meter is not poner.
Both come out as "put" and they are not interchangeable:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
what you are doing: putting something inside something. the verb: meter.
what you are doing: putting something somewhere. the verb: poner.
Pon el libro en la mesa — on the table, a surface. Mete el libro en la caja — into the box, an inside. Swap them and a Spanish speaker will picture you burying a book in a table.
Meter nearly always brings en with it, because "in" is the whole idea of the verb. If there is no inside, you want poner.

The word, taken apart.
Latin mittere, to send — and its participle missus.
Spanish wore the plain verb down to meter and shifted it from sending to inserting, which is a short move: to send a thing into a box is to put it there. The bookish arrivals kept the shape: admitir, permitir, emitir, transmitir.
And there it is in a word you already have. El permiso is per- plus missus: something sent through. English took the same set as permit, admit, mission, missile and message — every one of them a sending.
So meter la ropa en la caja and pedir permiso run on one Latin verb, one worn smooth and one carried in from the page.

Grammar Lens: the fourth phrase to give up a word.
[a table of 4 rows, read aloud]
what you already said: interesante. the word inside it: interesar.
what you already said: mira. the word inside it: mirar.
what you already said: el punto. the word inside it: apuntar.
what you already said: sí, claro. the word inside it: claro.
And la tienda joins them from the other direction: a shop turned out to be this chapter's verb, a stretched awning.
Claro is the strongest case yet, because sí, claro is among the first things anybody learns to say in Spanish and among the commonest things anybody says. You were told back then that it means bright — and still never once used it to describe a room, because agreeing with somebody asks nothing of the colour sense.
The habit to keep: when a new word resembles a phrase you already use, look for the connection before learning it twice.

Grammar Lens: two verbs that both mean put.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
the picture: into an inside. the verb: meter — meter en la caja.
the picture: onto or at a place. the verb: poner — poner en la mesa.
Meter comes with en because "in" is the verb's whole meaning. If nothing encloses the thing, poner is what you want.
The same test sorts pintar de verde, where de marks a resulting colour. Spanish tells you a great deal through its prepositions, and each of these verbs carries one it will not give up.

Why it's said this way.
De azul claro carries both of this chapter's small grammar points at once. De marks the resulting colour, and azul claro does not agree with anything — no azules claros even if there were several walls, because the pair has frozen into a name for the shade.
Meter las cajas en el trastero is meter doing exactly its job: a storeroom is an inside, and poner would be wrong. Then tiende is the tú command of a stem-changing verb, so the ie is there — tiende, never tende.
Notice the register throughout. Oye, ¿puedes…?, deja — every one is tú, because this is a flatmate and not a counter. Set it beside the notices in the chapters before this one and the difference is visible in the verb endings alone.

[pause 3 seconds]

Then check yourself: did you use de before the colour, meter for the inside, and the tú forms throughout?
