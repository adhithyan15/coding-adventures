# quiero que… — making the subjunctive appear

## Warm-up

[PAUSE 2s] You can now **build** the subjunctive. This lesson is about what makes
it **show up** — and the core case is beautifully simple: **one person wanting a
different person to do something.**

## Two subjects, joined by que

Compare:

| | |
|---|---|
| **Quiero hablar** español. | I want **to speak** Spanish. |
| **Quiero que hables** español. | I want **you to speak** Spanish. |

The first has **one** subject — I want, I speak — so Spanish uses a plain
infinitive, exactly like English.

The second has **two**. *I* am the wanter; *you* are the speaker. The moment the
subjects differ, Spanish must:

1. join the halves with **que**, and
2. put the second verb in the **subjunctive**.

> **Quiero** que **hables**. — Two subjects, so *que*, so subjunctive.

That is the *subiūnctīvus* of last lesson doing exactly what its name says:
living in a clause **joined underneath** the main one.

## Why it isn't a fact

*Hables* is not a report that you speak. It's what I **want** — which may never
happen. The main clause reaches out and bends the second one into something
unreal, and the mood marks the bend.

Watch the difference this makes:

| | |
|---|---|
| Sé que **hablas** español. | I know that you speak Spanish. *(a fact — indicative)* |
| Quiero que **hables** español. | I want you to speak Spanish. *(not yet real — subjunctive)* |

Same *que*, same person, different mood — because knowing reports the world and
wanting tries to change it.

## Wrap-up Recall

[PAUSE 3s] What makes the subjunctive appear after *querer*? (**Two different
subjects** — joined by *que*.) Say "I want to speak" and "I want you to speak."
(*Quiero hablar* / *Quiero **que hables***.) Why does *Sé que hablas* stay
indicative? (Knowing reports a **fact**; wanting doesn't.) What joins the two
subjects? (**Que**, followed by the subjunctive.)
