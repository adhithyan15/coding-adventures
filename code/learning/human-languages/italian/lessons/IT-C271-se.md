# se — if

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[IT-LEX-SI-02, IT-PRAGMATIC-NON-SO-02] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say "yes". (*Sì.*) Say "I don't know". (*Non so.*)

[PAUSE 1s] Every sentence so far has said what is, or what was. This chapter
says what follows **if** something else is true, and it begins with two
letters.

## Grammar Lens: se opens a condition
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[IT-GRAMMAR-SE-01]; assesses=[IT-LEX-C42-STARE-03, IT-LEX-C48-VERBI2-05] -->

**se** — "if".

> **Se** sei stanco, dormi. — If you're tired, sleep.
> **Se** il treno è in ritardo, leggo. — If the train is late, I read.

Whatever follows *se* is the **condition**; the rest of the sentence is what
follows from it. The condition can come first, with a comma, or second,
without one: *Leggo **se** il treno è in ritardo*.

**se** also means "whether", after a verb of knowing or asking, and here your
*non so* comes in useful:

> **Non so se** è vero. — I don't know if it's true.

## The word, taken apart: three small words
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[IT-ETYMON-SE-SI-02]; assesses=[IT-ETYMON-SI-03] -->

Listen to how close these are:

> **se** — "if" · **sì** — "yes", with its accent · **si** — "oneself", as in
> *come si chiama?*

**se** is inherited from Latin *sī*, "if" (EtymDB 2.1; Lewis & Short, under
*si*); the vowel opened from *i* to *e*. **sì**, "yes", is a different Latin
word: chapter twenty-nine traced it to *sīc*, "thus". *se* has its own vowel, so the ear
can tell it from the other two. *sì* and *si* sound exactly alike, and the
accent on *sì* is there to keep those two apart on the page, where the ear
cannot help.
