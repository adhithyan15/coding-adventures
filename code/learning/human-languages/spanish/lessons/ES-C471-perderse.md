# perderse — to get lost

## You'll want to know: perderse
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-C471-CURSO-06]; assesses=[ES-LEX-PERDER] -->

**perderse** — to get lost.

> Todos habían hecho cursos antes y yo **me perdía**.

Note what is lost there. Not the way — the **thread**. In a class, in a film,
in a conversation, *me pierdo* is *I lose track*, and it is the most useful
sense the verb has for you.

*Me he perdido* — I've got lost, or I've lost the thread. *No te pierdas* —
don't get lost, and also don't miss it.

## Grammar Lens: the family you were told to notice
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C471-CURSO-06, ES-LEX-C437-PLAN-03] -->

[Equivocarse](./ES-C437-equivocarse.md) named this verb explicitly, in a group
worth recalling whole:

| plain | reflexive |
|---|---|
| perder — to lose a thing | **perderse** — to get lost |
| olvidar — to forget a thing | olvidarse — it slips your mind |

The reflexive marks something that **befalls you** rather than something you
do to an object. *Perder el paraguas* is an act with a victim. *Perderse* has
no object at all: the losing happens to you.

That is why *me perdía* is not an admission of carelessness. It reports a
state she was in, which is exactly why it is the honest thing to say about a
class that had moved on without her.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C471-CURSO-06] -->

Latin **perdere**: **per-** plus **dare**, to give — to give **away**
thoroughly, to hand something over for good.

That *per-* is the thorough one, the same intensifier inside **perfect**
(thoroughly made) and **perturb**. So losing, in Latin, is a kind of extreme
giving: the thing goes and does not come back.

English **perdition** is the same word, kept for the most complete loss there
is.
