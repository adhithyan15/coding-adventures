# il parla · habló · falou — the same tense, four fates

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[] -->

[PAUSE 2s] The tense French retired to its libraries is a tense other people use
to order coffee.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[FR-ETYMON-PASSE-SIMPLE-ROMANCE-09]; assesses=[] -->

**il parla** comes from the plain Latin perfect, handed straight down with no
rebuilding at all. Which means it is the **same tense** as these:

| language | "he spoke" | status |
|---|---|---|
| French | *il parla* | books only |
| Spanish | *habló* | everyday speech |
| Portuguese | *falou* | everyday speech |
| Italian | *parlò* | regional |

One Latin ancestor, four different fates. A Spaniard saying *habló* and a French
novelist writing *il parla* are using the **same inherited form**, and only one
of them sounds archaic doing it.

That is worth sitting with, because it undoes an assumption. A tense is not
formal or informal, literary or everyday, by its nature. **Register is not a
property of a form; it is a property of what a particular community of speakers
happens to have kept.** French did not decide *il parla* was fancy. It just
stopped saying it, and the books did not.

[PAUSE 3s] What Latin tense is *il parla*? (The plain **perfect**, inherited
directly.) Which languages still say it every day? (**Spanish** — *habló* — and
**Portuguese** — *falou*.) So what makes a tense "literary"? (**Not the form** —
what its speakers kept using.) Next: what pushed it out, and where else that
happened.
