# lui et elle — the third pair, and the one that did not split

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[FR-LEX-MOI-01, FR-LEX-TOI-02, FR-VERB-ETRE-EST-03, FR-GRAM-CEST-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] *Je* became *moi*, *tu* became *toi*. Predict what *il* becomes, then
predict what *elle* becomes.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[FR-LEX-LUI-03]; assesses=[FR-LEX-MOI-01, FR-LEX-TOI-02, FR-VERB-ETRE-EST-03, FR-GRAM-CEST-01, FR-GRAM-LE-LA-GENDER-05] -->

> **lui** — him, standing alone. · **elle** — her, and also *she*.

> **C'est lui.** — It's him. · **C'est elle.** — It's her.

Only half the prediction comes true. **il** does split, into **lui** — and
**elle** does not split at all. The same four letters do both jobs: *elle parle*
and *c'est elle*.

Nothing decided that except which syllables happened to carry stress in Latin.
*Illi* gave *lui*, *ille* gave *il*, and *illa* gave one *elle* that was heavy
enough already. The masculine split and the feminine did not, and the language
has lived with the asymmetry ever since.

That same *ille* family is where **le**, **la** and **les** came from, so the
article and the pronoun in front of you are cousins several times over.
