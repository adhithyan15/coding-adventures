Spanish, chapter 448: La mudanza.
7 lessons. You can do the first 6 of them in the car; after that you will want to have stopped.


Lesson 1 of 7.
mudarse — changing where you live.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say la casa and el piso. You are about to leave one of them.

You'll want to know: mudarse.
mudarse — to move house.
Me mudo el sábado — I move on Saturday. Nos mudamos a un piso más grande — we are moving to a bigger flat. ¿Cuándo te mudas? — when do you move?
Spanish does not reach for a verb of carrying here. It reaches for a verb of changing, and that is the whole idea: you have not transported yourself, you have changed which address is yours.

Grammar Lens: what the -se is doing.
There is a plain verb mudar underneath, and it means to change something else:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
without -se: la serpiente muda la piel — sheds its skin. with -se: me mudo a Sevilla — I move.
without -se: mudar de opinión — change your mind. with -se: se muda de ropa — changes clothes.
On the left the verb reaches out to something: a skin, an opinion. On the right it turns back and lands on the person. Once it lands on you, the thing being changed is understood to be where you live — or, in a bedroom, what you are wearing. Context decides between those two, and it decides easily, because nobody packs a lorry to change their shirt.
Note that mudarse takes a for the destination — me mudo a Madrid — the same a you already use for going anywhere.

The word, taken apart.
Latin mutare, to change. It is a wide family and you can hear it in English: a mutation is a change, to mute a sound is to alter it, and a bird in moult is changing its feathers — the same picture as the snake.
Spanish keeps the plain sense in mudar de opinión and the specialised one in mudarse. A word narrowing from "change anything" to "change address" is ordinary: the commonest thing people announce they are changing is where they sleep.

Lesson 2 of 7.
la mudanza — the day itself.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say mudarse and la caja. Forty of the second, one of the first.

You'll want to know: la mudanza.
la mudanza — the move: not the decision, the day.
Hacer una mudanza — to do a move. La empresa de mudanzas — the removal firm. El camión de mudanzas — the removal van. La mudanza es el día quince — the move is on the fifteenth.
Spanish says hacer a move where English says do or make one, and it never says tener. A mudanza is something you carry out, not something you possess.

Grammar Lens: -anza names the doing.
Take a verb, cut the ending, add -anza, and you have a noun for the act or the state:
[a table of 4 rows, read aloud]
verb: mudar. noun: la mudanza — the move.
verb: esperar. noun: la esperanza — hope.
verb: enseñar. noun: la enseñanza — teaching.
verb: tardar. noun: la tardanza — the delay.
All four are feminine, and so is every other -anza noun you will meet: la confianza, la semejanza, la balanza. Spot the ending and the article comes free.
La esperanza is worth a second look. From esperar, to wait — hope, in Spanish, is named after the waiting rather than after the wanting. The ending took an ordinary verb and produced an abstract noun, and that is exactly what it does to mudar, except that a mudanza is not abstract at all: it is boxes.

The word, taken apart.
Latin mutare, to change, plus the ending. Old Spanish used mudanza for any change at all, including a change of heart, and you still meet that sense in older writing and in song lyrics.
The narrowing went the same way as the verb's. When people say la mudanza now, boxes are in the room.

Lesson 3 of 7.
duro — hard, and hard going.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say durante and fuerte. One of them is about how long, the other about how much force.

You'll want to know: duro.
duro — hard.
El pan está duro — the bread has gone hard. Un colchón duro — a firm mattress. And then, away from things you can press: un día muy duro — a hard day. El trabajo es duro — the work is heavy going. No seas tan duro con él — don't be so hard on him.
A mudanza is the standard example of un día duro, and no Spanish speaker would call it un día fuerte.

Grammar Lens: duro is not fuerte.
The two get confused because English "strong" and "hard" overlap. Spanish keeps them apart by asking a different question about each:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
word: fuerte. the question it answers: how much force does it have?
word: duro. the question it answers: how much does it resist?
Una persona fuerte can lift the boxes. Una persona dura will not soften when you ask for a favour. A rock is duro; it has no force at all. Wind is fuerte; it is not hard in the least.
So when a day is duro, the picture is not that the day was powerful. It is that the day would not give.

The word, taken apart.
Latin durus, hard — and from it the verb durare, to last.
That derivation repays a moment. What lasts is what resists wearing away, so "hard" and "long-lasting" are one idea seen twice. Spanish kept both halves: duro is the resisting, and durar — with durante inside it — is the lasting. La mudanza duró todo el día: the move held out all day.
English took both halves too, from the same place: durable and duration on one side, endure on the other.

Lesson 4 of 7.
estropear / estropeado — it no longer works.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say torpe and romper. Being the first often leads to the second.

You'll want to know: estropear.
estropear — to ruin, to wreck, to put out of action.
He estropeado el móvil — I've wrecked my phone. La lluvia estropeó la fiesta — the rain ruined the party. With -se it happens by itself: se ha estropeado la lavadora — the washing machine has broken down.
And the form you meet most is the adjective:
estropeado — out of order, damaged, spoiled.
Ascensor estropeado. Usen las escaleras.
That is a real sign, on a real lift, and it is the reason this word earns its place before the polite ones.

Grammar Lens: estropeado is not roto.
Both translate as "broken" and they are not interchangeable:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
what you mean: it is in pieces. the word: roto.
what you mean: it will not work. the word: estropeado.
A washing machine full of water that does nothing is estropeada and not rota — nothing came apart. A plate in two halves is rota and calling it estropeada would sound like an understatement.
Food takes estropeado too: la fruta se ha estropeado — the fruit has gone off. Again the test holds. The fruit is not in pieces; it no longer does its job.

The word, taken apart.
Spanish took it from Italian stroppiare, to cripple or maim, which goes back to Latin turpis — shameful, ugly.
So estropear and torpe are cousins, and the pair is worth holding side by side. The same Latin word for shameful came out of Italian as "wreck a thing" and out of Spanish as "clumsy" — an apology you make over a spilled glass. One branch kept the weight and one branch lost nearly all of it.
The e- at the front is Spanish's own doing. Spanish will not open a word with st-, so it fits a vowel in front: stroppiare became estropear, the way stare became estar and studiare became estudiar.

Lesson 5 of 7.
roto — in pieces.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say romper and la caja. Now say what happened to the box.

You'll want to know: roto.
roto — broken, torn, in pieces.
Un vaso roto — a broken glass. Los pantalones están rotos — the trousers are torn. He roto el plato — I've broken the plate.
It agrees like any adjective: roto, rota, rotos, rotas.

Grammar Lens: the participle that lost its middle.
A regular -er verb would give romp-ido. Spanish says roto, and it is one of a small group that behave this way:
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
verb: romper. the participle you expect: rompido. the one Spanish uses: roto.
verb: escribir. the participle you expect: escribido. the one Spanish uses: escrito.
verb: abrir. the participle you expect: abrido. the one Spanish uses: abierto.
These short forms are the older ones. They came straight down from Latin participles that were already short, and because the verbs are used so constantly, the regular pattern never got a grip on them. The same thing happens in English, where wrote survives beside worked.
One form covers both jobs: he roto el plato after haber, and el plato está roto as a plain description.

The word, taken apart.
Latin rumpere, to burst, with participle ruptus — and ruptus is what became roto.
English borrowed the same participle whole: a rupture, an eruption, something abrupt that breaks off. Spanish let it wear down into a two-syllable everyday word, and kept ruptura alongside for the borrowed, formal sense.
Keep the pair from the lesson before this one in view. Roto is ruptus, bursting. Estropeado is turpis, shameful. Spanish named the two kinds of failure after two different pictures, and the pictures still sort them.

Lesson 6 of 7.
(mudarse, la mudanza, duro, estropeado, roto) — from cold.

Warm-up.
[pause 3 seconds]
Close the lessons before this one. Say all five: to move house, the move, hard, out of order, in pieces.

Grammar Lens: two words for broken, one question.
This is the one distinction in the chapter that will cost you marks if it goes unsorted. Ask: did anything come apart?
[a table of 4 rows, read aloud]
the situation: lift stuck between floors. the word: estropeado.
the situation: window with a hole in it. the word: roto.
the situation: milk that has gone off. the word: estropeado.
the situation: shoelace snapped. the word: roto.
Notice the milk. Nothing could come apart in a carton, so roto is not available — and that is the test doing its work on a case where English gives you no help at all, since English would say neither "broken milk" nor "out-of-order milk".

Grammar Lens: where the five came from.
Four of the five were built out of things you already had.
[a table of 4 rows, read aloud]
new word: mudarse. what it was built on: mudar, to change.
new word: la mudanza. what it was built on: mudar plus the -anza of esperanza.
new word: duro. what it was built on: the dur- inside durante.
new word: estropeado. what it was built on: turpis, the Latin behind torpe.
Only roto arrived on its own, and even it came with romper attached. Five words, one genuinely new root among them. That is what happens once a vocabulary is large enough: new words start arriving as relatives.

Lesson 7 of 7.
(el día de la mudanza) — read it, then do it yourself.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, because the lesson points at something written down. You can listen to all of it now and come back to the parts that need looking at once you have stopped.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say la mudanza and estropeado. Both are in the note below, and one of them is doing the work of a warning.

Guided Practice.
A note left on the kitchen counter for whoever moves in next. Read it, then answer.
Hola. Nos mudamos hoy y te dejo unas cosas por escrito. El timbre está estropeado desde enero. El portero dice que lo arregla, pero lleva así medio año, así que llama por teléfono. En el armario hay un cristal roto. Lo he tapado con cartón, pero ten cuidado al abrirlo. La mudanza ha sido dura y quedan tres cajas en el trastero. Las recojo el domingo.
[pause 3 seconds]

[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: which of the two came apart, and how you know]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: what is still in the storage room, and when it goes]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Why it's said this way.
The note uses both words for broken within four lines, and each one is the only correct choice.
The doorbell is estropeado. Nothing about it is in pieces; it has stopped doing its job, and the fault is invisible. The pane of glass is roto, which is why it needed covering with cardboard — there is now a hole where glass used to be. Swap the two words and a Spanish reader would picture a smashed doorbell and a window that mysteriously fails to be a window.
Then look at nos mudamos hoy. Present tense, for something happening today, exactly as in English. And dura, agreeing with la mudanza: the day resisted, all day long.

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Now your turn, out loud and then in writing. Leave a note of four lines for the next tenant: say you are moving today, name one thing that is out of order, name one thing that is broken, and say the move has been hard.
Then check yourself: did each of roto and estropeado go to the right object, and did duro agree with what it describes?
