# il faisait froid — the weather, made in the past

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[FR-LEX-IL-Y-AVAIT-02, FR-GRAMMAR-IL-FAIT-03] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say "there were a lot of people", the way last lesson built it.
(*Il y avait beaucoup de monde.*) Then "it's cold", today. (*Il fait froid.*)

## You'll want to know: il faisait froid, il faisait chaud
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[FR-LEX-IL-FAISAIT-01]; assesses=[FR-LEX-TEMPS-02, FR-LEX-IL-PLEUT-04, FR-GRAM-IMPARFAIT-01] -->

English says the weather **was** cold. French says it **made** cold, and
you have that already: *il fait froid*. Move it into the past with the
recipe. *Nous faisons* gives the stem *fais-*:

> **Il faisait froid.** — It was cold.
> **Il faisait chaud.** — It was hot.

Listen to the first syllable. The *ai* of *faisait* is said like the short
*e* of *le*: "fe-zè". It is the sound of *nous faisons* too, and the
recipe carries it over.

Rain has a verb of its own. You have **il pleut**, "it's raining", and its
past is **il pleuvait**, "it was raining".

Keep **c'était** and **était** for things. *Le café était froid* — the
coffee was cold — is about the coffee. *Il faisait froid* is about the whole
day. Put three of these together and you have a morning before anything has
happened in it:

> *Il faisait froid et il pleuvait. Il n'y avait pas de soleil.*

In Québec, relaxed speech has its own word for a biting cold: *il faisait
frette*. In a letter, *froid* is the word to write.

## The phrase, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[] -->

**faire** is inherited from Latin *facere*, "to make, to do" (EtymDB 2.1),
and **faisait** goes back to its past *faciēbat*, "he was making" (Lewis &
Short, under *facio*). English **fact** and **factory** share it.

**froid** is inherited, through Old French *froit*, from Latin *frīgidus*,
"cold" (EtymDB 2.1), which English borrowed whole as **frigid**. **chaud**
goes back to Old French *chaut* (EtymDB 2.1), usually traced to Latin
*calidus*, "warm, hot" (Lewis & Short).
