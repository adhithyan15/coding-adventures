Persian, chapter 18: The Teens Are a Rule, Not Nine Words.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
یازده (yâzdah) — eleven, and one rule for nine numbers.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Count one to ten, then say dah on its own and what it means.

You'll want to know first — one word, and then a rule.
یازده — yâzdah — eleven.
Say it slowly and the ten is audible at the end: yâz + dah. The first piece is yek worn down; the second is the word for ten you learned in the last lesson.
That is the whole of the teens. Persian puts the unit in front of ten and stops:
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
12, davâz-dah — from do.
14, chahâr-dah — chahâr unchanged.
19, nuz-dah — from noh.
Nine numbers, one rule, and the unit is a number you already have. This is why ten had to come first.

Guided Practice.
[your turn — say: yâzdah — eleven]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — split: yâz + dah]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — build: chahâr + dah becomes chahârdah, fourteen]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
Sources: Wiktionary: یازده (yâzdah); Omniglot, Numbers in Persian.
[question — say your answer, then pause 12 seconds]
Build fourteen from chahâr and dah.


Lesson 2 of 5.
سیزده (sizdah) — thirteen, and how far a unit can bend.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say yâzdah, split it in two, and build fourteen.

You'll want to know first — one word.
سیزده — sizdah — thirteen.
si + dah. The unit is se, with its vowel lengthened and darkened — and English does the same thing without anyone noticing: three becomes thir- in thirteen, and five becomes fif- in fifteen.
Listen past the bending, then, and hear what does not bend: the ten stays the same, on the end, every time.

Guided Practice.
[your turn — say: sizdah — thirteen]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — split: si + dah]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — compare: se becomes si, as English three becomes thir-]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
Sources: Wiktionary: سیزده (sizdah); Omniglot, Numbers in Persian.
[question — say your answer, then pause 12 seconds]
Which part of sizdah never changes across the teens?


Lesson 3 of 5.
پانزده (pânzdah) — fifteen, and the one irregularity worth naming.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say sizdah and split it, then say panj.

You'll want to know first — one word.
پانزده — pânzdah — fifteen.
pân + z + dah. There is an n in there that panj does not have in the place you would expect it, and there is a z that came from the j.
Sixteen does the same thing: shânzdah, from shesh. Fifteen and sixteen are the only two of the nine teens that bend this far, and they bend the same way — so it is one irregularity, met once, not two.

Guided Practice.
[your turn — say: pânzdah — fifteen]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: shânzdah — sixteen, the same bend]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — run: yâzdah, sizdah, pânzdah and the ten inside each]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
Sources: Wiktionary: پانزده (pânzdah); Omniglot, Numbers in Persian.
[question — say your answer, then pause 12 seconds]
Which other teen bends the same way as pânzdah?


Lesson 4 of 5.
بیست and صد — twenty and a hundred.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say pânzdah and sizdah, and split each in two.

You'll want to know first — two words.
بیست — bist — twenty صد — sad — a hundred.
Two words, taken together because a price names one or the other far more often than it names any of the eighteen numbers between them.
صد is two letters and it is the largest number in this book. Its first letter, ص, is one no lesson has taught the hand to make — it enters this book by eye only, the way most of the track's words have, and reading is what the number needs.

The words, taken apart — viginti and centum.
بیست goes back to Indo-European wikmtih, "twenty" — Latin vīgintī, Sanskrit vimshati.
صد goes back to kmtom, "hundred" — Latin centum, Greek hekatón, English hundred. The Latin form is the one English borrowed twice over, in century and per cent, so a reader who has ever seen a percentage has already met sad's cousin.
The spelling is the joke: صد is written with a borrowed Arabic letter for a word that is not Arabic at all.

Guided Practice.
[your turn — say: bist — twenty; sad — a hundred]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — connect: sad from kmtom becomes Latin centum, English hundred]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — run: dah, bist, sad — the three round amounts]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
Sources: Wiktionary: صد; Omniglot, Numbers in Persian.
[question — say your answer, then pause 12 seconds]
Say ten, twenty and a hundred in Persian.


Lesson 5 of 5.
Payoff — one to twenty, and the hundred above it.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Count one to ten, then say a hundred.

Guided Practice.
Three of the nine teens were taught. Build the other six rather than recalling them.
From do, chahâr, haft, hasht and noh, build twelve, fourteen, seventeen, eighteen and nineteen by putting the unit in front of dah. Then check each against a printed list and repair only what missed.
Say sixteen, and say which taught number it bends like.
Count one to twenty straight through.
Hear twelve amounts named singly, from yek to sad, and say each.
The point of the chapter is that six of these nine numbers were never taught and you can say them anyway. That is what a rule buys and a word list does not.

Wrap-up Recall.
Source: Omniglot, Numbers in Persian.
[question — say your answer, then pause 18 seconds]
Build seventeen and nineteen from haft, noh and dah.
