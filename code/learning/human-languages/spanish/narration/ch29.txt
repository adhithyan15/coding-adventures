Spanish, chapter 29: Cl-, Pl-, Fl- to Ll-.
1 lesson. All 1 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 1.
cl-, pl-, fl- becomes ll- — three Latin clusters that all collapsed into one Spanish sound — and you have been saying one of them since you learned your name.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say "my name is." (Me llamo.) That ll- is the second sound law of this book, and you have been using it since your third chapter without being told.

Grammar Lens: three clusters, one destination.
Latin cl-, pl- and fl- all became Spanish ll-.
Llamar is Latin clāmāre, "to cry out." English took the same root untouched: claim, clamour, proclaim, exclaim.
llamar from clāmāre becomes claim, clamour.
And it happened to all three clusters, which is what makes it a law rather than a one-off:
[a table of 6 rows, read aloud]
Spanish: llamar. from: clāmāre. English cousin: claim, clamour.
Spanish: llave. from: clāvem. English cousin: clavicle, clef, conclave.
Spanish: llover. from: plovere. English cousin: pluvial.
Spanish: lleno. from: plēnum. English cousin: plenty, plenary.
Spanish: llano. from: plānum. English cousin: plain, plane.
Spanish: llama. from: flamma. English cousin: flame, inflammable.
Three different starting points arriving at one sound is unusual, and it is useful precisely because it is untidy: an unfamiliar Spanish word in ll- has three English shapes to try, and one of them is very often right.

Why it's said this way.
You now hold three sound laws, and they are not three facts. They are three instances of the same thing.
-ct- becomes -ch- — noctem becomes noche, and English kept nocturnal.
f- becomes h- — fabulārī becomes hablar, and English kept fable.
cl-, pl-, fl- becomes ll- — clāmāre becomes llamar, and English kept clamour.
Every one has the same shape: Spanish inherited the word and wore it down; English borrowed it later and left it closer to the Latin. That is why the English cousin so often looks more Latin than the Spanish does, which feels backwards until you see why.
So the laws are not trivia about the past. They are a conversion table between the Spanish in front of you and the English already in your head, and they will keep working on words no course will ever teach you.
