# ليس (laysa) — is not

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[AR-CONCEPT-C02-ISMII-01, AR-CONCEPT-C05-LA-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] **اسمي حسن** — *my name is Hasan* — is a complete Arabic sentence with
no verb in it, and it is the sentence shape this book teaches best. Try to deny
it with **لا** and there is no verb for **لا** to stand in front of.

- [YOU RECALL: the joiner two lessons back that has **لا** hiding inside it — *lākin*]

## You'll want to know: ليس
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[AR-GRAMMAR-C39-LAYSA-01]; assesses=[AR-CONCEPT-C02-ISMII-01, AR-CONCEPT-C33-HALIB-01] -->

> **اسمي ليس حسن** — *ismī laysa Ḥasan* — **my name is not Hasan.**

> **الحليب ليس ماءً** — *al-ḥalīb laysa māʾan* — **milk is not water.**

Arabic leaves the present-tense *is* out. To deny a sentence that never had one,
the language supplies a word that exists only for that job — and **ليس** is it.

Notice the **ـً** on **ماءً**: **ليس** puts what follows it in the accusative,
which is the one grammatical demand it makes and the reason its predicate wears
a tanwīn where the affirmative would not.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[AR-GRAMMAR-C39-LAYSA-01, AR-CONCEPT-C05-LA-01] -->

Arab grammarians read **ليس** as **لا** — the negator of chapter five — welded to
**أيس**, an old word meaning *there is*. So the word began as *there-is-not*, and
it still behaves like a verb: it takes person endings, even though nothing it
denies is a verb. It is a fossil doing a job nothing else in the language does.

## Wrap-up Recall
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[AR-GRAMMAR-C39-LAYSA-01, AR-CONCEPT-C05-LA-01, AR-LEX-C38-LAKIN-01] -->

[PAUSE 3s] Why can a nominal sentence not be denied with **لا**? (**There is no
verb for لا to stand in front of** — Arabic leaves *is* out.) What are the two
pieces grammarians find inside **ليس**? (**لا** and **أيس**, *there is*.) And
what does **ليس** do to the word after it? (**Puts it in the accusative**.)
