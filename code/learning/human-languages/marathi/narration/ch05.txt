Marathi, chapter 5: Marks Above, Below, and Beside the Letter.
7 lessons. The first lesson already needs your eyes or your hands, so save this one for when you have stopped.


Lesson 1 of 7.
ि — short i, written before, read after.

Before we start: this one needs your hands, so it is not a driving lesson. You will want a pen and something to write on and your eyes, for letter shapes on the page. You can listen to everything else now — leave the section called Script until you have stopped, and I will say so again when we reach it.

Warm-up.
Say dhanyavād once from memory. Every mark in this chapter hangs on a consonant your hand already knows.

Script.
ि (i).
The short-i mark is the one that breaks the rule you would guess. It is written to the LEFT of its consonant and read to the RIGHT of it: कि is ki, never ik. The eye meets the mark first; the ear meets it second.
Draw the upright on the left of the letter, then carry the curve up and over the headline so it hooks back to the right. The hook is what tells this mark apart from its long partner, which you will meet later.

Writing — observe and trace.
Keep कि in front of you. Trace the mark alone twice, then trace it beside क twice more. Say ki each time your pen finishes the hook, so the hand and the ear learn the same order the page will use.

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 2 seconds]
Which side of क does the mark sit on? (The left.) Which side do you hear it on? (The right.)
Source: Unicode Devanagari chart, U+093F.
[question — say your answer, then pause 20 seconds]
Trace the short-i mark and say where it is written relative to its letter.


Lesson 2 of 7.
ु — short u, a small hook below.

Before we start: this one needs your hands, so it is not a driving lesson. You will want a pen and something to write on and your eyes, for letter shapes on the page. You can listen to everything else now — leave the section called Script until you have stopped, and I will say so again when we reach it.

Warm-up.
One mark went to the left. This one goes straight down, and it is the mark you will meet most often of all.

Script.
ु (u).
The short-u mark is a small hook tucked under the letter, opening to the right. It hangs from the foot of the stem, not from the headline.
तु is tu — the opening syllable of the polite tumhī you already say. Start at the base of त, dip below the line, and curl right. Keep it small: its long partner is the same hook drawn deeper.

Writing — guided copy.
Copy तु twice with the model visible, saying tu as the hook closes. Then write the short-i mark once beside it from memory and check it still leans left.

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 2 seconds]
Above the line, below it, or beside it? (Below.)
Source: Unicode Devanagari chart, U+0941.
[question — say your answer, then pause 22 seconds]
Copy the short-u hook under ta, then name the mark that is written to the left of its letter.


Lesson 3 of 7.
ू — long ū, the deeper hook below.

Before we start: this one needs your hands, so it is not a driving lesson. You will want a pen and something to write on, your eyes, for letter shapes on the page and your eyes, because the lesson points at something written down. You can listen to everything else now — leave the section called Script until you have stopped, and I will say so again when we reach it.

Warm-up.
You have the short hook. The long one is the same gesture, carried further.

Script.
ू (ū).
Long ū is the short hook drawn deeper and turned back on itself, so it finishes pointing down-left rather than right. Same place, same start, more travel.
दू is dū — the first syllable of the word for milk. Say tu and dū one after the other and you can hear the length the extra travel is paying for.

Writing — delayed copy.
Look at दू for five seconds, cover it, and write it once. Uncover and compare the depth of the hook against the short one you wrote a moment ago. Repair only if the two look the same.

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 2 seconds]
tu or dū — which hook travels further? (dū.)
Source: Unicode Devanagari chart, U+0942.
[question — say your answer, then pause 24 seconds]
Write long uu after a delay, then say what separates it from short u.


Lesson 4 of 7.
ृ (ṛ) — the vocalic r mark, said ru.

Before we start: this one needs your hands, so it is not a driving lesson. You will want a pen and something to write on and your eyes, for letter shapes on the page. You can listen to everything else now — leave the section called Script until you have stopped, and I will say so again when we reach it.

Warm-up.
A third mark below the line. This one is not a vowel you have a letter for in English.

Script.
ृ (ṛ).
This mark writes a sound Sanskrit treated as a vowel made of r. Marathi speakers say it ru, so कृ is heard as kṛ, close to kru.
The shape is a small curl below the letter that opens to the left — the mirror of the short-u hook, and that mirroring is exactly how to remember it. It appears in tatsama words the language borrowed whole, like kṛtī, "activity".

Writing — guided copy.
Copy कृ twice with the model visible. Then put the short-u hook beside it and check that the two curls open in opposite directions.

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 2 seconds]
How does a Marathi speaker say this vowel? (ru.)
Source: Unicode Devanagari chart, U+0943.
[question — say your answer, then pause 22 seconds]
Copy the vocalic-r curl on ka, then say which direction it opens compared with short u.


Lesson 5 of 7.
ँ (ã) — the moon holding a dot.

Before we start: this one needs your hands, so it is not a driving lesson. You will want a pen and something to write on and your eyes, for letter shapes on the page. You can listen to everything else now — leave the section called Script until you have stopped, and I will say so again when we reach it.

Warm-up.
You already write one nasal mark above the headline. Here is its second, softer cousin.

Script.
ँ (ã).
The candrabindu is one dot cradled in a small upward crescent — a moon holding a dot, which is exactly what its name says.
Set it beside the anusvāra you already write:
ं closes the vowel onto a nasal consonant. ँ (ã) leaves the vowel open and sends the air through the nose while you are still saying it. Draw the crescent first, sitting on the headline, then place the single dot inside it.

Writing — observe and trace.
Trace ँ (ã) twice, then trace ं once beside it. Point at which of the two carries a crescent. Repair only the mark that came out wrong.

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 2 seconds]
Which of the two nasal marks has a moon under it? (The candrabindu.)
Source: Unicode Devanagari chart, U+0901.
[question — say your answer, then pause 20 seconds]
Trace the moon-and-dot, then name the mark that is a dot with no crescent.


Lesson 6 of 7.
अ — the vowel your consonants have been hiding.

Before we start: this one needs your hands, so it is not a driving lesson. You will want a pen and something to write on, your eyes, for letter shapes on the page and your eyes, because the lesson points at something written down. You can listen to everything else now — leave the section called Script until you have stopped, and I will say so again when we reach it.

Warm-up.
Say क on its own. You did not say a bare k. Something came after it.

Script.
अ (a).
Every Devanagari consonant carries a short a inside it unless a mark or a halant takes it away: क is ka, not k. अ is that same hidden vowel written out on its own, for when a word begins with it and there is no consonant to hide inside.
You already write its long partner आ, and the relationship is visible: आ is अ with one upright added on the right. Draw the left loop, the vertical stem, then the headline that ties them together.

Writing — delayed copy.
Look at अ for five seconds, cover it, and write it once. Then add the upright and check that you have made आ. If the two came out identical, the upright is missing.

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 2 seconds]
What does क say on its own? (ka — the vowel is already inside.)
Source: Saurmandal, Devanagari a stroke order.
[question — say your answer, then pause 26 seconds]
Write independent short a after a delay, then turn it into long aa.
