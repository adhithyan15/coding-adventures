## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-TREN]; assesses=[] -->

**el tren** — "the train."

Spanish borrowed the word from French **train**. The French noun belongs to
**traîner**, "to draw, drag, or pull along." Before rails, the central picture
was already a line of connected things following one another because the front
one pulled the rest. A railway made that old picture literal in iron.

English borrowed the same French noun. That is why a railway *train*, a bridal
*train*, and a *train of thought* all keep the same shape: one thing following
another in a connected line.

Now make the contrast the lesson, because the mock requires this word and the
book already owns its near-neighbour:

- **el tren** — the train;
- **tres trenes** — three trains.

The last sound carries the distinction: *-n* against *-s*. Do not guess from
context when your ear can learn the difference directly.
