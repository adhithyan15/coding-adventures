\chapter{The Compound Past}
\label{ch:passe-compose}

French's everyday past tense is built out of \emph{avoir} --- and it began life
not as a tense at all, but as a sentence about \emph{possessing} something. Then
we meet the older past it pushed out of the language. \emph{Practice lessons:
\texttt{lessons/FR-C15-*.md}.}

\section{\emph{j'ai parl\'{e}} --- the past built from ``have''}
\label{lesson:passe-compose}

French's everyday past tense. You already have both halves of it: the verb
\emph{avoir}, and the \emph{-er} verbs.

\begin{center}\itshape
avoir (conjugated) $+$ past participle
\end{center}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
Infinitive & Ends in & Participle \\
\midrule
\emph{parl\textbf{er}} & \emph{-er} & \emph{parl\textbf{\'{e}}} \\
\emph{habit\textbf{er}} & \emph{-er} & \emph{habit\textbf{\'{e}}} \\
\emph{fin\textbf{ir}} & \emph{-ir} & \emph{fin\textbf{i}} \\
\emph{vend\textbf{re}} & \emph{-re} & \emph{vend\textbf{u}} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

So:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}
\toprule
French & English \\
\midrule
\emph{j'\textbf{ai} parl\'{e}} & I spoke / I have spoken \\
\emph{tu \textbf{as} parl\'{e}} & you spoke \\
\emph{il \textbf{a} parl\'{e}} & he spoke \\
\emph{nous \textbf{avons} parl\'{e}} & we spoke \\
\emph{ils \textbf{ont} parl\'{e}} & they spoke \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Only \emph{avoir} changes. The participle sits there unchanged.

\begin{sounds}
\emph{parler}, \emph{parl\'{e}} and \emph{parlez} are all pronounced \emph{the
same}. Three spellings, one sound --- the context tells you which.
\end{sounds}

\begin{cousinweb}
Where the endings come from: \emph{parl\textbf{\'{e}}} $\leftarrow$ Latin
\emph{-\=atum}; \emph{fin\textbf{i}} $\leftarrow$ \emph{-\={\i}tum};
\emph{vend\textbf{u}} $\leftarrow$ \emph{-\=utum}. The same \emph{-t-} that
English kept in \textbf{finite}, \textbf{acute}, \textbf{statute}.\\[3pt]
A footnote on the third one: \emph{-\=utus} was a \emph{rare} ending in Latin
(\emph{min\=utus}, \emph{trib\=utus}). French and Italian took that small class
and spread it over a whole conjugation. So \emph{-\'{e}} and \emph{-i} are
straight inheritance; \emph{-u} is mostly the language tidying up by analogy.
\end{cousinweb}

\begin{grammarlens}
\textbf{The buried possessive.} Here is why this tense exists. Latin could say
\emph{habe\=o litter\=as script\=as} --- ``I \emph{have} letters
\emph{written}.''\\[3pt]
That is not a past tense. It is \emph{avoir} in its ordinary sense --- I
\emph{possess} some letters --- with \emph{script\=as} as an \textbf{adjective}
describing them, agreeing with \emph{litter\=as} in gender and number.\\[3pt]
Over centuries the phrase stopped meaning ``I possess a written thing'' and
started meaning simply ``\textbf{I wrote}.'' A possessive construction hardened
into a tense.\\[3pt]
And the fossil is still visible: when the object comes \emph{first}, the
participle still agrees with it, exactly as an adjective would ---
\emph{Les lettres que j'ai \'{e}crit\textbf{es}}, ``The letters that I wrote.''
That \emph{-es} is a two-thousand-year-old adjective ending doing its old job.
\end{grammarlens}

\begin{sounds}
\textbf{Practice.} Say the set: \emph{j'ai parl\'{e}, tu as parl\'{e}, il a
parl\'{e}, nous avons parl\'{e}, ils ont parl\'{e}}. Say the three homophones
--- \emph{parler} $\cdot$ \emph{parl\'{e}} $\cdot$ \emph{parlez}. Say
\emph{J'ai travaill\'{e}} (``I worked''). Then say the old meaning out loud:
``I \emph{have} a thing \emph{spoken}.''
\end{sounds}

\section{\emph{il parla} --- the past you read but never say}
\label{lesson:passe-simple}

French has a \emph{second} past tense. You will meet it in every novel and never
hear it spoken. Knowing why is worth more than knowing its endings.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
 & \emph{Pass\'{e} simple} & \emph{Pass\'{e} compos\'{e}} \\
\midrule
he spoke & \emph{il parla} & \emph{il a parl\'{e}} \\
they spoke & \emph{ils parl\`{e}rent} & \emph{ils ont parl\'{e}} \\
he had & \emph{il eut} & \emph{il a eu} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

You do \emph{not} need to produce these. You need to recognise them, because
they fill the past tense of essentially all French literature.

\begin{cousinweb}
\textbf{il parla} $\leftarrow$ Vulgar Latin \emph{*parabol\=avit} --- the plain
Latin perfect, handed straight down. Which means the \emph{pass\'{e} simple} is
the \emph{same tense} as Spanish \emph{habl\'{o}} and Portuguese \emph{falou}.
\end{cousinweb}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
Language & ``he spoke'' & Status \\
\midrule
French & \emph{il parla} & books only \\
Spanish & \emph{habl\'{o}} & everyday speech \\
Portuguese & \emph{falou} & everyday speech \\
Italian & \emph{parl\`{o}} & regional --- see below \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Same Latin ancestor, four different fates.

\begin{grammarlens}
\textbf{The retreat, three times over.} French is not alone.
\end{grammarlens}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
Language & Simple past & What replaced it in speech \\
\midrule
French & \emph{pass\'{e} simple} & \emph{pass\'{e} compos\'{e}} (\emph{j'ai parl\'{e}}) \\
German & \emph{Pr\"{a}teritum} & \emph{Perfekt} (\emph{ich habe gesagt}) \\
Italian & \emph{passato remoto} & \emph{passato prossimo} (\emph{ho parlato}) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Three languages --- two Romance, one Germanic --- each built a compound past out
of ``have,'' and each let that compound push the inherited simple past out of
everyday conversation and into writing.

\begin{culture}
Not three separate inventions, though. They are \textbf{neighbours}: France,
southern Germany and northern Italy form a connected block, and the change
spread through it by \emph{contact} over the same few centuries. Linguists call
this an \emph{areal} change --- one that travels across language boundaries
because the speakers do.\\[3pt]
And Spanish and Portuguese, out at the western edge of that block, largely
\emph{didn't} get it. They kept their simple past where it was. (Though Spain's
Spanish has since started drifting the same way, with \emph{he hablado} taking
ground from \emph{habl\'{o}} --- the change reaching the edge late.)
\end{culture}

\begin{sounds}
\textbf{Practice.} Say the pair --- \emph{il parla} \ldots{} \emph{il a
parl\'{e}}. Say the sisters --- \emph{il parla} $\cdot$ \emph{habl\'{o}}
$\cdot$ \emph{falou} $\cdot$ \emph{parl\`{o}}. Remind yourself where you'd meet
it: in a \emph{novel}, not a conversation. And say the three retreats ---
\emph{pass\'{e} simple} $\cdot$ \emph{Pr\"{a}teritum} $\cdot$ \emph{passato
remoto}. Next chapter: the other auxiliary, and the verbs that refuse
\emph{avoir}.
\end{sounds}
