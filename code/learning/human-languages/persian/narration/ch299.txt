Persian, chapter 299: Would, And Six Lines About A Trip.
4 lessons. You can do the first 3 of them in the car; after that you will want to have stopped.


Lesson 1 of 4.
اگر وقت داشتم، میرفتم (agar vaqt dâshtam, miraftam) — if I had time, I would go.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say "if the weather is good, we'll go". (Agar havâ khub bâshad, miravim.) Then "it depends". (Bastegi dârad.)
[pause 1 second]
That condition may come true. The last turn in this run is a condition that is not true at all, and what would follow if it were.

Grammar Lens: the plain past after agar, the mi- past after it.
اگر وقت داشتم، به سفر میرفتم. — agar vaqt dâshtam, be safar miraftam — If I had time, I would go on a trip.
I have no time, and I am not going. The agar half says so by moving into the plain past, dâshtam, the way English moves to "had". The result goes into the mi- past, miraftam.
Set it beside the real one: agar havâ khub bâshad, miravim, it may be fine, and then we'll go; agar havâ khub bud, miraftim, it is not fine, so we are not going.

Grammar Lens: the mi- past as "would".
The shape from chapter two hundred and ninety, miraftam, "I was going", does a third job here: "I would go".
اگر پول داشتم، یک ماشین میخریدم. — agar pul dâshtam, yek mâshin mikharidam — If I had the money, I'd buy a car.
اگر جای تو بودم، زودتر میخوابیدم. — agar jâ-ye to budam, zudtar mikhâbidam — If I were you, I'd go to bed earlier.
Jâ-ye to is "your place", so the advice is "if I were in your place". Budam and dâshtam keep their plain past here too, with no mi-.
The same pair can also look back, "if I had had time, I would have gone": agar vaqt dâshtam, miraftam covers both. The situation tells you which.

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Which past goes after agar when the condition is not true? (The plain past: dâshtam.) Which says "would"? (The mi- past: miraftam.) "If I were you"? (Agar jâ-ye to budam.)


Lesson 2 of 4.
دوست داشتم (dust dâshtam) — I would like, and if only.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say "I'd like to go". (Dust dâram beravam.) Then "if I had time, I would go". (Agar vaqt dâshtam, miraftam.)
[pause 1 second]
Put those two together and you have the politest way to turn something down.

Grammar Lens: dust dâshtam, for a wish held back.
Dust dâram beravam is open: I'd like to go, and I may. Move dâshtan into the past and the wish steps back from what is happening:
دوست داشتم بیایم، ولی وقت ندارم. — dust dâshtam biyâyam, vali vaqt nadâram — I'd love to come, but I don't have time.
The wished-for verb stays in the subjunctive, biyâyam, as it did after dust dâram. Only dâshtan moves. Said about the past, it is "I would have liked": dust dâshtam ân film râ bebinam, "I'd have liked to see that film". Bebinam is didan in the subjunctive, on its stem bin-.

Grammar Lens: kâsh, "if only".
کاش, kâsh, is a wish with no result attached. With the plain past it wishes against what is true now:
کاش وقت داشتم. — kâsh vaqt dâshtam — If only I had time.
With the subjunctive it wishes for something still open:
کاش فردا هوا خوب باشد. — kâsh fardâ havâ khub bâshad — I do hope the weather is good tomorrow.
That second one sits close to omidvâram ke, with more longing in it.

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Which is open, dust dâram or dust dâshtam? (Dust dâram.) Which verb stays in the subjunctive after either? (The wished-for one: biyâyam.) "If only I had time"? (Kâsh vaqt dâshtam.)


Lesson 3 of 4.
Review — what would be, and what may be.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say "if I had the money, I'd buy a car". (Agar pul dâshtam, yek mâshin mikharidam.)

What you've built.
Two chapters, and two kinds of "if". A condition that may come true takes the subjunctive: agar bârân biyâyad, dar khâne mimânim. One that is not true takes the plain past, and its result the mi- past: agar havâ khub bud, be pârk miraftim, "if the weather were good, we'd go to the park".
[pause 2 seconds]
The mi- past has now done three jobs. In chapter two hundred and ninety it painted a scene, mikhândam, and told a habit, har ruz miraftam. Here it says what would happen, miraftim.
[pause 2 seconds]
Dust dâshtam holds a wish back, and kâsh sighs for one:
dust dâshtam be safar beravam, vali vaqt nadâram. kâsh vaqt dâshtam!

[your turn — say: dust dâshtam be safar beravam, vali vaqt nadâram. kâsh vaqt dâshtam!]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — answer: fardâ be pârk miravi? — with bastegi dârad and a real condition]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: advice with agar jâ-ye to budam and a cardigan —... zhâkat mipushidam]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
"If it rains" and "if the weather were good": which verb follows agar in each? (Biyâyad, the subjunctive; bud, the plain past.) "If only"? (Kâsh.) Which letter opens ژاکت? (ژ, zhe.)


Lesson 4 of 4.
Six lines about a trip — six connected Persian lines to one reader about a trip — a scene, events in order, a reason, a feeling, and a last line with a condition or a wish.

Before we start: this one needs your hands, so it is not a driving lesson. You will want a pen and something to write on and your eyes or your hands for the steps that ask you to read, point, write or gesture. You can listen to everything else now. I will say so again when we reach it, but leave this section until you have stopped: Wrap-up recall.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Chapter two hundred and eighty-nine asked for four lines that held together. This one asks for six, and they tell a story.
[pause 1 second]
Pick one reader: a friend, by name.

You'll want to know.
[pause 2 seconds]
A told story is not a list of events. It has a scene, in bud and the mi- past. It has events in the plain past, put in order with avval, ba'd, vaqti and dar âkhar. It has at least one reason, with chon or barâ-ye hamin, and a feeling, with budam or shodam.
[pause 2 seconds]
A good one ends by looking forward or wishing: omidvâram ke..., agar... bâshad,..., or agar vaqt dâshtam,... miraftam.
[pause 2 seconds]
The B1 writing paper asks for a connected text of 130 to 170 words. Six lines is the first step towards it, not the whole of it.

Writing — connected composition.
[pause 2 seconds]
On paper, right to left, write six lines to your reader about a trip, real or invented, one line for each of these:
the scene: when it was, with gozashte or pish, and the weather;
where you went, who was with you, with bâ, and for how long;
the first thing that happened, with avval;
the next thing, with ba'd or vaqti, and a reason, with chon, be khâter-e or barâ-ye hamin;
how it ended and how you felt, with dar âkhar and budam or shodam;
one line forward: omidvâram ke, a real agar, or agar vaqt dâshtam.
[pause 3 seconds]
Use only words you already write. The whole exercise is choosing the right past for each line, and joining them.

[once you have stopped driving — this part needs your eyes or your hands; if you are driving, skip ahead to the next part]
Wrap-up recall.
[pause 2 seconds]
Now read your lines as you read the winter trip in chapter two hundred and ninety-one. Mark every verb S for scene or E for event. Line one should be mostly S; lines three and four mostly E. A line about the weather marked E is the commonest slip: change sard shod back to sard bud.
[pause 2 seconds]
Then three spelling checks. Every mi- is joined to its verb, میرفتم. Every و is followed by a break smaller than the space between words. And each word keeps its own letter: غذا with غ, قطار with ق, though the two sound alike.
[pause 2 seconds]
Last, read line six alone. If it has agar and something that may happen, is the verb after agar in the subjunctive? If it is a wish against the facts, is it in the plain past, with the mi- past after it?
