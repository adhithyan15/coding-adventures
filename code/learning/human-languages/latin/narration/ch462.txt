Latin, chapter 462: To trade at market, To keep shouting, To offer for sale, Goods, Hawker.
6 lessons. All 6 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 6.
nūndinor, nūndinārī — to trade at market, to buy and sell.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for smoky, then the Latin for filthy.

You'll want to know: nūndinor, nūndinārī.
nūndinor, nūndinārī — "to trade at market, to buy and sell".
Agricolae nōnō quōque diē in oppidō nūndinantur. Every ninth day the farmers trade at the market in the town.
Deponent: nūndinor, nūndinārī, nūndinātus sum. The market day, nūndinae, came every ninth day, counting both ends.

The word, taken apart.
nūndinor is built on nūndinae 'market day' (Lewis & Short), which comes from novem 'nine' and diēs 'day': the market held every ninth day.

Mercātōrēs in forō clāmitant. The merchants in the forum keep shouting.
clāmitō, clāmitāre, clāmitāvī, clāmitātum, a frequentative of clāmō: the -itō means 'again and again'.

Lesson 4 of 6.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to keep shouting, then the Latin for to offer for sale.

You'll want to know: mercimōnium, mercimōniī.
mercimōnium, mercimōniī — "goods, wares, merchandise".
Mercātor mercimōnia in nāve ad urbem vehit. The merchant carries his goods by ship to the city.
Second declension, neuter: mercimōnium, mercimōniī n., mostly plural. A word of comedy, from merx 'goods'.

The word, taken apart.
mercimōnium is built on merx, mercis 'merchandise' with the suffix -mōnium (Lewis & Short). English merchant and commerce come from the same family.

Lesson 5 of 6.
īnstitor, īnstitōris — a hawker, a pedlar; a shop agent.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to offer for sale, then the Latin for goods.

You'll want to know: īnstitor, īnstitōris.
īnstitor, īnstitōris — "a hawker, a pedlar; a shop agent".
Īnstitor per viās it et mercēs suās clāmat. The hawker goes through the streets and calls out his goods.
Third declension, masculine: īnstitor, īnstitōris m. A man who sells for someone else, often on the street.

The word, taken apart.
īnstitor is built on īnsistō 'to stand on, to keep at a thing' (Lewis & Short), from in- and sistō 'to stand'. EtymDB lists it without a parent.
