# el abogado — somebody called to your side

## Warm-up

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-C427-AGENT-05]; assesses=[] -->

**el abogado** — "the lawyer."

Latin **advocatus**: **ad-** to + **vocatus**, "called." Literally **one called
to your side** — in a Roman court, the friend you summoned to speak for you.

English borrowed the same word twice and kept both:

| word | the calling in it |
|---|---|
| **advocate** | one called to your side |
| **vocation** | a calling, in the older sense |
| **convoke** | to call together |

*Vocare*, "to call," is the root under all of them, and it is the same root as
*voz*, the voice.

Spanish wore *advocatus* down to *abogado* while English kept the Latin shape
intact, which is the ordinary difference between a word that was **inherited**
and a word that was **borrowed** — the same distinction *actor* and *trabajador*
demonstrated.

## Grammar Lens: the profession without a visible ending
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C427-AGENT-05, ES-LEX-C427-AGENT-01] -->

| profession | how the word was made |
|---|---|
| vendedor | Spanish added **-dor** to a Spanish verb |
| bombero | Spanish added **-ero** to a Spanish noun |
| abogado | Latin made it, and Spanish inherited it whole |

*Abogado* has no ending you can remove to find a Spanish verb, because it was
never built in Spanish. It arrived finished.

The feminine is the ordinary swap: *la abogada*.
