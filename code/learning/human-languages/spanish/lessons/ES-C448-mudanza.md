# la mudanza — the day itself

## You'll want to know: la mudanza
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-C448-MUDA-02]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C448-MUDA-01] -->

**la mudanza** — the move: not the decision, the day.

*Hacer una mudanza* — to do a move. *La empresa de mudanzas* — the removal
firm. *El camión de mudanzas* — the removal van. *La mudanza es el día
quince* — the move is on the fifteenth.

Spanish says **hacer** a move where English says *do* or *make* one, and it
never says *tener*. A mudanza is something you carry out, not something you
possess.

## Grammar Lens: -anza names the doing
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C448-MUDA-02, ES-LEX-ESPERAR-01] -->

Take a verb, cut the ending, add **-anza**, and you have a noun for the act or
the state:

| verb | noun |
|---|---|
| mudar | la mud**anza** — the move |
| esperar | la esper**anza** — hope |
| enseñar | la enseñ**anza** — teaching |
| tardar | la tard**anza** — the delay |

All four are feminine, and so is every other **-anza** noun you will meet:
*la confianza*, *la semejanza*, *la balanza*. Spot the ending and the article
comes free.

*La esperanza* is worth a second look. From *esperar*, to wait — hope, in
Spanish, is named after the waiting rather than after the wanting. The ending
took an ordinary verb and produced an abstract noun, and that is exactly what
it does to *mudar*, except that a mudanza is not abstract at all: it is boxes.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C448-MUDA-02] -->

Latin **mutare**, to change, plus the ending. Old Spanish used *mudanza* for
any change at all, including a change of heart, and you still meet that sense
in older writing and in song lyrics.

The narrowing went the same way as the verb's. When people say *la mudanza*
now, boxes are in the room.
