# क्योंकि (*kyoṁki*) — because

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[HI-JOIN-KYON-01, HI-JOIN-KI-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Ask **क्यों?** out loud. Then notice that you have no way at all to
answer it. Now say **कि** — the word from the last chapter that hangs a clause
on a verb. The answer is those two words stuck together.

## Grammar Lens: क्यों + कि
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[HI-JOIN-KYONKI-01]; assesses=[HI-LEX-SAKNA-01, HI-GRAMMAR-SAKNA-STEM-01, HI-CONCEPT-C07-NAHIN-01, HI-CONCEPT-C05-KARNA-01] -->

> **मैं कल नहीं आ सकता क्योंकि मैं काम करता हूँ.**
> — *maiṁ kal nahīṁ ā saktā kyoṁki maiṁ kām kartā hūṁ.* —
> **I can't come tomorrow because I work.**

Read the word: **क्योंकि** is **क्यों** + **कि**, *why-that*. Nothing is hidden
in it. Hindi answers *why* by saying *why-that* and then a whole ordinary
sentence, verb last, exactly as after plain **कि**.

The order is fixed and it is the useful way round: **the claim first, the reason
second**. English can lead with *because*; the ordinary Hindi sentence does not.

That example is worth memorising whole, because it is the shape of an entire
exam task: a written message that says you cannot meet and why. It needs three
things this book has now given you — the negation, **सकना**, and **क्योंकि** —
and it needed all three.

Look at what the word is made of one more time. **क्यों** comes down from
Sanskrit; **कि** was borrowed from Persian. Hindi welded them into a single
conjunction and nobody notices the seam. That is this language in one word.
