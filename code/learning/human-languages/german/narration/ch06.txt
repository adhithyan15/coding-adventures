German, chapter 6: One, Two, Three.
4 lessons. All 4 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 4.
eins — "one," and the German ei.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Here is where German is closer to English than any Romance language gets. Its numbers are not borrowed from Latin — they are German's own, and they are the near-twins of the English ones. Start with the first.

You'll want to know: eins.
eins — "one," the number you say when you are counting.

Sounds you'll need.
diphthong-ei — German ei is the English word eye. Always, in every word, with no exceptions to remember.
So eins is eyns. You already own this one: heißen is HY-ssen, and Wiedersehen has an ie, which is the other way round and says ee. ei says eye; ie says ee. The second letter tells you which.

The word, taken apart.
eins is English one. Not borrowed from it, not similar to it — the same inherited word, which is why it needs no story to make it stick.
English hides the relationship in its spelling. One is written as though it rhymed with bone, and it did once; the w-sound at the front (wun) crept in later. Strip that off and one and ein are the same shape.
The same word is also sitting inside English a and an — an apple is one apple, worn down. German does exactly the same thing with eins, and once the first five are learned there is a lesson on it.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: "eins" — eyns, with the English word eye inside it]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: "eins" then "one" — the same old word]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: "ei" as eye, "ie" as ee — the second letter decides]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
What is "one" in German? (Eins.) How is ei said? (Like the English word eye.) And ie? (Ee.) Which English word is eins? (One — and a/an underneath it.)


Lesson 2 of 4.
zwei — "two," and the z that is a ts.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
One down. The second number has the same ei you met a moment ago, and one letter that does not say what an English reader expects.

You'll want to know: zwei.
zwei — "two."
Said tsvy. The z is the ts of English cats — never the buzz of English zoo. That is the whole of the German z: it has one job and it never does anything else.
The other two letters you already own. The w is a v, as in wohnen, and the ei is eye, as in eins. So zwei is three rules you have and one that is new.

The word, taken apart.
zwei is English two, with the tw- opening turned into zw-.
English still says the tw- out loud in the words two grew from — twin (two born together), between (in the space of two), twice, twenty. It only lost the w in the number itself, which is why two is spelt with a letter nobody pronounces. German kept the pair and shifted the t to ts:
tw- becomes zw-: two / zwei, twin / Zwilling, twelve / zwölf.
So the silent w in English two is not a spelling accident. It is the w that German still says.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: "zwei" — tsvy, the z like the end of cats]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: "eins, zwei"]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: "two", "twin", "between" — then "zwei", and hear the w come back]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
What is "two"? (Zwei.) How is German z said? (Ts, as in cats.) Which English words still say the w that two dropped? (Twin, between, twice.)


Lesson 3 of 4.
drei — "three," and German's missing th.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Third number, third ei. And it shows you something that will keep paying: German has no th sound at all, and you can predict what it uses instead.

You'll want to know: drei.
drei — "three."
Said dry: the scraped r you met in lernen, and the ei saying eye.

The word, taken apart.
drei is English three. German has no th at all, and where English says one, German says d. That is not a fact about numbers — it is a fact about the whole language, and you have been using it since your first danke:
[a table of 4 rows, read aloud]
English: three. German: drei.
English: the. German: der, die, das.
English: thank. German: danke.
English: that. German: das.
Five words, one swap. Whenever an English word starts with th, try a d and you will often land on the German.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: "drei" — dry, scrape the r]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: "eins, zwei, drei"]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: the swap — "three/drei", "the/der", "thank/danke"]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
What is "three"? (Drei.) What does German use where English has th? (D.) Give three pairs. (Three/drei, the/der, thank/danke.)


Lesson 4 of 4.
Siblings, not borrowings — why German and English numbers match and the Latin ones only rhyme.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Drei, three, and Latin tres all mean three and all start with a different sound. Before the next seven numbers, it is worth knowing why — because the answer makes each of them cheaper to learn.

Grammar Lens: siblings and cousins.
German and English are siblings. Latin is a cousin.
That is not a figure of speech. All three grew out of one older language, and Germanic — the parent of English and German — split off from the rest first. So drei did not come from tres, and English three did not either. All three are what one prehistoric word became along three different roads.
Which is why the German numbers are the easy ones and the Romance numbers are the hard ones, for an English speaker: three and drei are the same word two doors apart, while tres is the same word across the street.

Why it's said this way.
The differences are not random. A sound law is a change that happened to every word in a language at once, so once you know one you can predict the rest.
You have already met one of these. Gut is good with the d turned to a t, and machen is make with the k turned to a ch — one shift, two words, and it caught every word in German that had those sounds.
The shift that separates Germanic from Latin is older and works the same way. The two turns worth carrying into the next lessons are:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
Older sound: t. Germanic sound: th, and German d. Seen in: Latin tres, English three, German drei.
Older sound: p. Germanic sound: f. Seen in: Latin pes "foot", English foot.
So when a Latin word and a German one sound nothing alike, try the first consonant before deciding they are unrelated. Often they are the same word, one law apart.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: "tres", "three", "drei" — one word, three roads]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: the two turns — "t becomes th", "p becomes f"]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: "good/gut", "make/machen" — the shift you already knew]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Is drei borrowed from Latin tres? (No — they are cousins, both from one older word.) What is a sound law? (A change that hit every word in the language at once, so it is predictable.) Which two turns should you carry forward? (Old t to th/d, old p to f.)
