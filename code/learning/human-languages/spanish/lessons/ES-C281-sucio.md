## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-SUCIO]; assesses=[] -->

**sucio** — "dirty."

Latin **sucidus**, from **sucus**, juice or sap. *Sucidus* meant juicy, sappy,
greasy — and its most ordinary use was for **wool that had not been washed**,
still heavy with the natural grease it came off the animal with.

From there the road is short. Ungreased wool is unwashed wool; unwashed becomes
unclean; unclean becomes dirty in general, including dirty in the moral sense
that *sucio* also carries. The word never described mud. It described a fleece.

English keeps *sucus* in **succulent**, a plant full of juice, and the connection
to *suck* is real but ancient — both go back to a root about drawing liquid.

## What you've built
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-ETYMON-SUCIO]; assesses=[] -->

That is sixteen plain words, and every one of them turned out to have somewhere
it came from.

Look back at what the pair you just finished has in common. *Limpio* began at
clear water and moved to clean; *sucio* began at greasy wool and moved to dirty.
Neither started life as an opposite of the other. They were pulled into a pair
later, by speakers who needed two words and had these two lying about — which is
how most of the neat oppositions in any language got built.
