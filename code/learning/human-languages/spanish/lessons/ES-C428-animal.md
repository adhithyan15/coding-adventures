# el animal — a thing with breath in it

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-ORTH-C428-04] -->

three creatures you can say in Spanish, and try to say the word that covers all
three at once.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-ORTH-C428-05]; assesses=[] -->

**el animal** — "the animal."

Latin **animal**, from **anima**, "breath, soul." An animal is literally **a
thing that has breath in it** — which is how Latin drew the line between the
living and the not-living, at the lungs.

*Anima* is generous, and every word on it is about that breath:

| word | the breath in it |
|---|---|
| **anim**al | a thing that breathes |
| **ánim**o | spirit, courage |
| des**anim**ado | discouraged — the breath gone out |
| re**anim**ar | to revive — to put the breath back |

English took the same root for **animate**, **unanimous** (of one *anima*,
one mind) and **magnanimous** (of a great one).

## Grammar Lens: the word that covers the others
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-ORTH-C428-05] -->

This is a **superordinate** — a word one level up from the ones it covers. The
corpus has taught particular creatures for hundreds of chapters without ever
teaching the category they belong to.

That gap matters more than it looks. A superordinate is what lets you talk about
something you cannot name: *un animal grande* works when you do not know the
word for the creature in front of you, and a reading paper will use it exactly
that way, in a notice or a sign that must cover every creature at once.

The stress is on the last syllable — *a-ni-MAL* — and there is no written accent,
because a word ending in **-l** is stressed there by default.
