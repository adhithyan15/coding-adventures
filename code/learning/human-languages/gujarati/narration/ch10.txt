Gujarati, chapter 10: Numbers One to Five.
2 lessons. All 2 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 2.
એક, બે, ત્રણ, ચાર, પાંચ — one to five.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Three of these will look familiar from any other Indian language. Two won't — and both have a reason.

You'll want to know: the five.
[a table of 5 rows, read aloud]
1. Gujarati: એક. said: ek.
2. Gujarati: બે. said: be.
3. Gujarati: ત્રણ. said: traṇ.
4. Gujarati: ચાર. said: chār.
5. Gujarati: પાંચ. said: pā̃ch.
Notice the script: Gujarati is Devanagari without the top line. Same letters, same system — the shirorekhā simply isn't drawn. If you've done the Hindi writing track you can feel the relationship immediately.

What you've built: two surprises and a headless script.
Most neighbouring languages have a d in “two,” but Gujarati says બે be. Hindi says tīn for “three,” but Gujarati says ત્રણ traṇ, with an r.
Do not carry both histories while the count is still new. Make the five forms automatic first; the next prerequisite-ordered lesson explains why be and traṇ took those shapes.

Lesson 2 of 2.
Two number histories: be and traṇ — why Gujarati two begins with b and why three regained an r.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Count once: ek, be, traṇ, chār, pā̃ch. Which two forms look least like Hindi or Marathi? (Be and traṇ.)

The word, taken apart — બે from a different ancestor.
Sanskrit had gendered forms of “two,” and its daughters selected different parts of that paradigm:
[a table of 5 rows, read aloud]
language: Sanskrit. “two”: dváu (masc.), dvé (fem. and neut.) route: —.
language: Hindi. “two”: do. route: masculine side.
language: Marathi. “two”: don. route: masculine side, plus an analogical -n.
language: Bengali. “two”: dui. route: disyllabic Prakrit duve.
language: Gujarati. “two”: be. route: feminine/neuter dvé side.
Gujarati બે is not a corruption of do. It is a different inheritance from the same Sanskrit paradigm.
The initial cluster changed too. In dv-, the dental d adopted the labial place of the following v: dv becomes bb becomes b. The first consonant was remade in the shape of its neighbour, then the double simplified.

The word, taken apart — ત્રણ and the r that came back.
[a table of 4 rows, read aloud]
stage: Sanskrit neuter. “three”: trī́ṇi.
stage: Prakrit. “three”: tiṇṇi: r already gone.
stage: Hindi. “three”: tīn.
stage: Gujarati. “three”: traṇ: r present again.
The shared ṇ shows that Hindi and Gujarati both descend from neuter trī́ṇi through Prakrit tiṇṇi. By that stage, the r was gone and its weight had moved into doubled ṇṇ. Hindi later simplified the double and lengthened the vowel.
Gujarati's tr- is generally treated as restored from Sanskrit, not carried through unbroken. Sanskrit remained a literary language and continued to reshape its descendants. Traṇ looks older than tīn, but it is closer because speakers put the r back.
