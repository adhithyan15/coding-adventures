Hindi, chapter 106: Not Feeling Well.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
थकान (thakān) — tiredness.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Hindi for a fever, then the Hindi for pain.

You'll want to know: थकान.
थकान (thakān) — "tiredness".
आज बहुत थकान (thakān) है — "I am very tired today", literally "today there is a lot of tiredness". The बहुत and आज are both yours already.

The word, taken apart.
थकान (thakān) is built on thaknā, "to get tired", with the ending -ān. CDIAL lists thaknā under a reconstructed sthakk, "stop, halt", and calls its link with sthā-, "to stand", probable but not clear (CDIAL 13737).

What you've built.
A first word for how the body feels at the end of a day.

Guided Practice.

[your turn — say: thakān]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: thakān, once more]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: say āj bahut thakān hai]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — recall: say the Hindi for a fever, then the Hindi for pain, then say thakān again]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
What does थकान (thakān) mean? ("Tiredness".) Say it once more.


Lesson 2 of 5.
ज़ुकाम (zukām) — a cold, a runny nose.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Hindi for pain, then the Hindi for tiredness.

You'll want to know: ज़ुकाम.
ज़ुकाम (zukām) — "a cold", the kind with a runny nose.
मुझे ज़ुकाम (zukām) है — "I have a cold", literally "to me there is a cold". The person sits in मुझे, as in मुझे चाय चाहिए.

The word, taken apart.
ज़ुकाम (zukām) is a loan from Arabic zukām, "a cold, catarrh", which reached Hindi through Persian. Its Arabic root is z-k-m (Lane).

What you've built.
An illness, in the to-me frame you already use for wanting.

Lesson 3 of 5.
खाँसी (khā̃sī) — a cough.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Hindi for tiredness, then the Hindi for a cold.

You'll want to know: खाँसी.
खाँसी (khā̃sī) — "a cough". It is feminine.
मुझे खाँसी (khā̃sī) है — "I have a cough". Same frame, new word in the middle.

The word, taken apart.
खाँसी (khā̃sī) (khāṁsī) is a tadbhava, a word inherited from Sanskrit and reshaped by sound change. It comes from kāsa, "cough" (CDIAL 3138). CDIAL notes the kh- as imitating the sound.

What you've built.
A second complaint in the same frame.

Lesson 4 of 5.
बीमार (bīmār) — ill, sick.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Hindi for a cold, then the Hindi for a cough.

You'll want to know: बीमार.
बीमार (bīmār) — "ill, sick".
मैं बीमार (bīmār) हूँ — "I am ill". This one describes you, so it takes मैं... हूँ, not मुझे.

The word, taken apart.
बीमार (bīmār) is a loan, usually traced to Persian bīmār, "ill, sick".

What you've built.
The difference between having an illness and being ill.

Lesson 5 of 5.
तबीयत (tabīyat) — health, how one is feeling.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Hindi for a cough, then the Hindi for ill.

You'll want to know: तबीयत.
तबीयत (tabīyat) — "how one is feeling", one's health on the day.
आपकी तबीयत (tabīyat) कैसी है? — "how are you feeling?", the question to ask someone who has been ill. तबीयत (tabīyat) ठीक नहीं है — "I am not well".

The word, taken apart.
तबीयत (tabīyat) is a loan from Arabic ṭabīʿa, "nature, temperament", which reached Hindi through Persian. Its Arabic root is ṭ-b-ʿ (Lane).

What you've built.
The question after how are you, when you know someone has been unwell.
