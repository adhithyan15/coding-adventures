## You'll want to know: આઠ

> **આઠ** — *āṭh* — **eight**

Two signs: the independent **આ** you have had since chapter one, and the **ઠ**
you made yourself last lesson. Nothing else.

**એક, બે, ત્રણ, ચાર, પાંચ, છ, સાત, આઠ.**

## The word, taken apart — the same road, walked twice
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GU-LEX-AATH, GU-LEX-SAAT] -->

Wiktionary hands **આઠ** down from Prakrit *aṭṭha*, and that from Sanskrit
*aṣṭá*.

Set it beside last lesson's word:

| Sanskrit | Prakrit | Gujarati |
|---|---|---|
| *saptá* | *satta* | **સાત** |
| *aṣṭá* | *aṭṭha* | **આઠ** |

Two numbers, one road, and the same three steps every time: a cluster of two
different consonants, then a doubled consonant, then a single one with a long
vowel standing in front of it. Seven and eight did not each have a history. They
had the same history.

## What you've built: a number that keeps everything
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GU-LEX-AATH, GU-GRAMMAR-ORDINAL-MU, GU-LEX-PAHELU, GU-ETYMON-PAHELU-PRATHAMA] -->

**આઠમું** — *āṭhmũ* — **eighth**. Built, like **સાતમું**, and again from
nothing you had to be told.

Hold that against **પહેલું**. *First* keeps not one sound of **એક**; its **લ**
stands where Sanskrit *prathama* had an *m*, and the number vanished on the way.
**આઠમું** keeps **આઠ** entire. The top of your count and the bottom of it behave
in opposite ways, and now you can see both at once.
