# buenas tardes — good afternoon (and why "late" hides inside it)

## Warm-up

[PAUSE 2s] You just met *buenos días*. This is its afternoon sibling — same
blessing, one new word, and that new word is secretly one you already know
from English.

## You'll want to know first

- [ES-C01-L02 (buenos días)](./ES-C01-L02-buenos-dias.md) — the blessing
  pattern and *bueno* ← *bonus* live here; this lesson reuses both.

## Sounds you'll need

- `diphthong-ue` — same *BWEH* as before, now feminine: **buenas**,
  *BWEH-nas*.
- Regular stress: *TAR-des*, second-to-last syllable (ends in *s*).

## The word, taken apart

**buenas** — good (again). Same Latin **bonus** from the last lesson — but
notice it shifted from *buenos* to *buen**as***. That's because it now
agrees with a *feminine* noun, *tardes*. You're seeing gender-agreement in
the wild two lessons in; the rule itself comes later, but file away that
*bueno* is a shape-shifter that matches its noun.

**tarde** — afternoon.
Root: Latin **tardus** — but *tardus* didn't mean "afternoon." It meant
**"slow, late."** And *that* meaning is the one English kept:

- **tard**y — late.
- re**tard** — to slow down (literally "to make late").
- **tard**igrade — "slow-stepper," the actual scientific name of the little
  water-bear that plods along under a microscope.

So how did "late" become "afternoon"? Naturally: the afternoon *is* the late
part of the day — the day growing old. Spanish took the Latin word for "late"
and let it drift to name the lateness itself. Every time you say *buenas
tardes*, you're calling it "the good late-hours."

## Why it's said this way

Same fossil as *buenos días*: plural, from *"Buenas tardes os dé Dios."* One
extra cultural note worth having — the boundary. *Buenas tardes* starts
around midday (after lunch, not after 12:00 sharp) and runs until it's
genuinely dark, which in much of the Spanish-speaking world is late. Spanish
carves the day differently than English "afternoon/evening" does; the words
follow the daylight, not the clock.
