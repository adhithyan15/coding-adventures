# Brot and bread

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GE-LEX-BROT-01, GE-GRAMMAR-NATIVE-VS-LOAN-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] A word can be interesting for what happened to it. This one is
interesting for what did not.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[GE-ETYMON-BROT-BREAD-01]; assesses=[GE-LEX-BROT-01] -->

*Brot* comes from Germanic *\*braudam*, and English **bread** comes from the
same word. No Latin anywhere in it.

Now the other side of the line. Latin *pānis* went into every Romance language
and stayed there:

| language | the word | from |
|---|---|---|
| German | *Brot* | Germanic *\*braudam* |
| French | *pain* | Latin *pānis* |
| Italian | *pane* | Latin *pānis* |

## Why it's said this way
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GE-ETYMON-BROT-BREAD-01, GE-GRAMMAR-NATIVE-VS-LOAN-01, GE-LEX-VATER-01] -->

Rome sold German a calendar and a clock. It did not sell German bread.

The pattern is the one you have been given four times now and it holds again
here: a thing people handle every single day already has a word, and an incoming
language does not get to replace it. Months were an administrative object and
arrived with their labels. A loaf on a table was not waiting for a name.

That is why *Brot* sits with *Vater* and *Mutter* rather than with *Januar*.
