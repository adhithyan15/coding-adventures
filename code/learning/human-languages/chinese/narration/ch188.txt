Lesson 1 of 6.
有钱 (yǒuqián) — rich; well-off.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Mandarin for at that time, then the Mandarin for in the end.

You'll want to know: 有钱.
有钱 — yǒuqián — "rich; well-off".
他很有钱 tā hěn yǒuqián — He's very well off.
The new character, in pieces: 钱 (qián) — ten strokes in two pieces: on the left the metal piece, five strokes; on the right two short bars, a long slanting hook, a stroke falling to the left across it, and a dot. The next lesson puts it in your hand.

The word, taken apart.
有钱 (yǒuqián) is built from 有 'to have' and 钱 (qián) 'money', both inherited from Middle Chinese, hjuwX and dzjen (Guangyun, in Baxter's transcription). The dictionary explains the second, after an older commentary, as money named after a spring, because it flows everywhere.

Script — the metal piece, then two bars and a hook.
钱 (qián).
Two pieces, left before right. Count the pieces, not the strokes.
The left piece, the metal piece, five strokes. A long stroke falling to the left, two short bars, a short bar rising to the right, then a vertical that turns up to the right at its foot.
The right piece, five strokes. Two short bars, one over the other. A long slanting hook from the top down to the lower right, flicking up at its foot. A stroke falling to the left across it. Then a dot at the top right.
5 + 5 = ten.

Lesson 3 of 6.
huāqián — to spend money.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Mandarin for in the end, then the Mandarin for well-off.

You'll want to know: huāqián.
huāqián — "to spend money".
wǒ mèimei hěn huì huāqián — My little sister really knows how to spend money.
Its characters come later; for now it is a word for the ear.

The word, taken apart.
huāqián is built from huā, the word for a flower, here 'to spend', and 钱 (qián) 'money'. The second is inherited from Middle Chinese dzjen (Guangyun, in Baxter's transcription); the dictionary lists the first only as a flower, so how it came to mean spending is not settled.

Lesson 4 of 6.
língqián — small change; loose coins.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Mandarin for well-off, then the Mandarin for to spend money.

You'll want to know: língqián.
língqián — "small change; loose coins".
nǐ yǒu língqián ma — Have you got any change?
Its characters come later; for now it is a word for the ear.

The word, taken apart.
língqián is built from líng 'zero' and 钱 (qián) 'money', both inherited from Middle Chinese, leng and dzjen (Guangyun, in Baxter's transcription). The dictionary glosses the first 'to fall' and 'a light rain'; the sense of small odd amounts is not given there, so how the pair came to mean change is not settled.

Lesson 6 of 6.

gòu — "enough".

Its characters come later; for now it is a word for the ear.

The word, taken apart.
gòu is inherited from Middle Chinese kuw (Guangyun, in Baxter's transcription), glossed 'many'. The dictionary lists it with a level tone only, so the falling tone of gòu is not the regular outcome, and how it came is not settled.
