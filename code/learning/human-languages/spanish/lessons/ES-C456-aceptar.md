# aceptar — we take that here

## You'll want to know: aceptar
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-C456-CUENTA-03]; assesses=[ES-LEX-PAGAR] -->

**aceptar** — to accept.

> No se **aceptan** tarjetas.

*¿Aceptan tarjeta?* — do you take card? *Acepto* — I accept, on a form or a
screen. *Aceptar las condiciones* — to accept the terms.

It is the verb of agreeing to something offered: money, terms, an invitation,
an apology.

## Grammar Lens: aceptar is not admitir
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C456-CUENTA-03, ES-LEX-C456-CUENTA-02] -->

Both come out as "accept" in English and they part company on a bill:

| what is happening | the verb |
|---|---|
| you agree to take what is offered | **aceptar** |
| you let something in, or own up to it | **admitir** |

*Aceptan tarjeta* — they will take your card. *No se admiten perros* — dogs
are not let in. And *admitir un error* is to own up to one, where *aceptar un
error* would mean putting up with it.

The test is direction: *aceptar* is about **agreeing**, *admitir* about
**letting through**.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C456-CUENTA-03] -->

Latin **accipere**: **ad-**, towards, plus **capere**, **to take**.

So to accept is to take a thing **towards yourself**, and the direction is the
whole meaning. Something you did not take towards yourself, you did not
accept.

*Capere* is one of Latin's most productive verbs and you meet it constantly in
English: **capture**, **capable**, **captive**, **receive** (*re-capere*, take
back), **deceive**, **conceive**, **perceive**. Spanish has *capaz*, *captar*,
*recibir* and *percibir* from the same stem.

The **c** before **e** is soft, so *aceptar* is *a-thep-TAR* in Spain and
*a-sep-TAR* elsewhere — the same rule that gave you *accidente*.
