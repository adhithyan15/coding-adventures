Lesson 1 of 3.
एक, द्व, त्रि, चतुर्, पञ्च (pañca) — one to five.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, because the lesson points at something written down and your eyes for one table that cannot be read aloud. You can listen to everything else now — leave the section called You'll want to know: the five until you have stopped, and I will say so again when we reach it.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
This chapter does something the other Indic chapters can't do alone. Sanskrit preserves the Old Indo-Aryan forms behind the Hindi, Marathi, Bengali, Gujarati and Punjabi ones — and, at the same time, these forms are cousins of the English ones. Learn five and you get a foothold in both directions.

You'll want to know: the five.
There is a table here I cannot read to you — 4 columns and 5 rows, and it has more columns than can be held in the ear at once. Its columns are: one with no heading, stem, masculine, neuter. Come back and look at it when you have stopped.
Three notes, and the third one matters more than it looks.
Sanskrit has a dual — a separate number for exactly two things — which is why "two" is dváu rather than a plural.
Pañca barely inflects in the nominative and accusative. It is also the one you already half-know.
And Sanskrit numbers have gender. Look at the last column. These same words, in Hindi, Marathi, Bengali, Gujarati, and Punjabi, mostly descend from the neuter forms — trī́ṇi and catvā́ri, not tráyaḥ and catvā́raḥ. Keep that column in view; the daughter languages inherit from it.

What you've built: two directions.
These five forms point in two directions. The next lesson lines them up with Latin and English and explains the sound laws. A final short lesson follows pañca into Punjab, pentagon, and the qualified history of punch.

Lesson 2 of 3.
The Sanskrit numbers are English cousins — the Sanskrit, Latin, and English number family and the sound laws between them.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Give the five stems: eka, dva, tri, catur, pañca.

What you've built: one family, not borrowing.
[a table of 5 rows, read aloud]
Sanskrit: éka-. Latin: ūnus. English: one.
Sanskrit: dvá-. Latin: duo. English: two.
Sanskrit: tri-. Latin: trēs. English: three.
Sanskrit: catúr-. Latin: quattuor. English: four.
Sanskrit: páñca. Latin: quīnque. English: five.
Sanskrit, Latin, and English inherited these from one family. Numbers resist replacement, so their relationship remains audible after millennia.
The first row needs one caveat: éka- and ūnus/one use the same root with different suffixes, PIE oy-ko- versus óynos. They are relatives rather than the same complete word. The other four rows continue the same word.

The word, taken apart — rounded k goes three ways.
PIE kʷ was a k with rounded lips. In kʷetwóres “four,” three branches treated it differently:
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
branch: Latin. outcome: kept qu-. example: quattuor.
branch: Indo-Iranian. outcome: merged it with k, then palatalised before front vowels. example: catvāri.
branch: Germanic. outcome: shifted it to hw-. example: what, who.
Sanskrit c- is therefore a regular result, not a random replacement. The same sequence helps explain pañca's ñc.

The word, taken apart — two different English f stories.
The f- of five does not come from kʷ. It comes from initial p, changed to f by Grimm's law, as in pater becomes father and pēs becomes foot.
English four is irregular. By the sound rule it should begin hw- like what. Its f- is usually explained as analogy with neighbouring five. Counting words can reshape one another.

Lesson 3 of 3.
Pañca hiding in familiar words — five inside Punjab, pentagon, and the debated history of punch.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say Sanskrit “five.” (Pañca.) Give its English cousin. (Five.)

The word, taken apart — five waters.
Punjab comes from Persian panj-āb, “five waters,” for the land of five rivers spanning India and Pakistan. Persian panj is the Iranian cousin of Indic pañca: both descend from the same Indo-Iranian and PIE number root.
The place-name is Persian, not Sanskrit, but the family resemblance is real.

The word, taken apart — Greek buildings and contests.
Greek pente is another descendant of the same root. It appears in pentagon, a five-sided figure, and pentathlon, a five-event contest.

The word, taken apart — the qualified punch story.
The usual account derives punch, the drink, from Hindi pāṁch from Sanskrit pañca, because the drink was said to contain five ingredients.
That origin is not unanimous. A rival derives the word from puncheon, a type of cask. Keep the useful connection with a label: “the common five-ingredients account,” not an unquestioned fact.
