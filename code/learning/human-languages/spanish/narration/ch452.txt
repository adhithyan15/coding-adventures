Spanish, chapter 452: El reparto.
6 lessons. All 6 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 6.
el almacén — where the boxes wait.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say la tienda and la caja. The shop sells them; somewhere else keeps them.

You'll want to know: el almacén.
el almacén — the warehouse, and in a shop the stockroom out the back.
Está en el almacén — it's in the stockroom. No queda en almacén — there's none left in stock. Los almacenes — the plural, and note the accent disappears: almacén needs it, almacenes does not.
Almacenar is the verb, to store. A computer stores the same way: datos almacenados.

Grammar Lens: the accent that comes and goes.
The stress never moves. The mark does:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
singular: el almacén. plural: los almacenes.
singular: el examen, which gives: plural: los exámenes.
Almacén ends in n with the stress on the last syllable, which is not the default, so it needs a mark. Add -es and the stress is now on the second-to-last syllable, which is the default — so the mark comes off.
Examen runs the opposite way for the same reason, and having the two side by side is the clearest way to see that the accent is a correction to a rule, not a property of the word.

The word, taken apart.
Arabic al-makhzan, the storehouse — from khazana, to store up.
The al- is the Arabic definite article, and Spanish swallowed it whole. That happened to hundreds of words during the centuries of Arabic rule, and once you know the sign you can spot them: almohada, alcalde, alfombra, algodón, azúcar, aceite. Every al- at the front of a Spanish word is worth a second look.
So el almacén is, strictly, "the the-storehouse". Spanish was not troubled by this and neither is English, which took magazine from the same Arabic word by way of French — a store of articles, and then a store of ammunition.

Lesson 2 of 6.
la ruta — the way through.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say roto and la calle. One of those two is about to turn up inside a word for a road.

You'll want to know: la ruta.
la ruta — the route.
La ruta del reparto — the delivery round. Cambiar de ruta — to change route. La ruta más corta — the shortest way. En ruta — on the way.
It is the word for a planned road, which is why a delivery driver, a bus company and a hiking map all use it. The street itself stays la calle; the line you trace across several of them is la ruta.

Grammar Lens: ruta, camino, calle.
Three words an English speaker would cover with "road" or "way":
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
what it is: the street with the houses on it. the word: la calle.
what it is: the path underfoot. the word: el camino.
what it is: the plan of where to go. the word: la ruta.
Voy por la calle Mayor — I'm going along a particular street. El camino está mojado — the path is wet, something you can stand on. La ruta pasa por tres pueblos — the route goes through three villages, which is a fact about the plan and not about any surface.

The word, taken apart.
Latin via rupta — a broken way.
Rupta is the participle of rumpere, to burst or break, and it is the very word you already have in roto. A via rupta was a road forced through what was in the way: trees felled, rock broken, a line opened where there had been none.
Latin then dropped the via and kept the adjective, which is how a word meaning "broken" ended up meaning "route". English took it from French as route, and also as rout — an army broken open — and routine, a way so well worn that you no longer choose it.
So roto and ruta are one word. A broken plate and a road through a mountain are the same idea applied to two different obstacles.

Lesson 3 of 6.
el seguimiento — where the parcel is.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say seguir and el entrenamiento. Put the ending of the second onto the first.

You'll want to know: el seguimiento.
el seguimiento — tracking, and in a clinic or an office, follow-up.
El número de seguimiento — the tracking number. Hacer seguimiento de un pedido — to track an order. Una consulta de seguimiento — a follow-up appointment.
Whatever is being followed, the word is the same: a parcel across a country, a patient across six months, a complaint across three departments.

Grammar Lens: you could have built this yourself.
Seguir you have had for chapters. -miento you met on entrenamiento. Put them together and the word comes out with its gender attached:
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
verb: entrenar. noun: el entrenamiento.
verb: seguir. noun: el seguimiento.
verb: sentir. noun: el sentimiento.
Masculine, all of them, as every -miento noun is. And note what happened to the stem: seguir has an i that sigo turns into... nothing you need here, because -miento attaches to the calm form of the verb, not the shouting one.
This is the point at which Spanish vocabulary starts paying compound interest. A verb plus a known ending is not a new word to learn; it is two things you own, joined.

The word, taken apart.
Latin sequi, to follow. It is a root you have been walking past for a long time.
Enseguida — en seguida, "in following" — is next-thing-along turned into right-away. And the family in English is enormous: a sequence is things following each other, a consequence is what follows from something, a sequel follows a film, and a second is the one that follows the first.
Perseguir is to follow all the way through, and conseguir — to get hold of something — is to follow it until you have it. Spanish named succeeding after the chasing.

Lesson 4 of 6.
cómodo — comfortable, and convenient.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say el modo and duro. A hard mattress and a comfortable one are about to be the same question.

You'll want to know: cómodo.
cómodo — comfortable, and also convenient.
Un sillón muy cómodo — a very comfortable armchair. ¿Estás cómodo? — are you comfortable? And the other half: una ruta más cómoda — an easier route. A la hora que te sea más cómoda — whenever suits you best.
So it covers both the cushion and the convenience, where English needs two words. La comodidad is the noun, and incómodo the opposite, for a chair and for a silence alike.

Grammar Lens: the false friend runs one way only.
English commodious means roomy, and that is not what cómodo means. The trap is worth naming because it is asymmetric:
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
you want to say: a comfortable chair. Spanish: una silla cómoda.
you want to say: a convenient time. Spanish: una hora cómoda.
you want to say: a roomy flat. Spanish: un piso amplio.
Going from Spanish to English, cómodo is safe — "comfortable" is nearly always right. Going the other way, "commodious" will not get you there and amplio will.

The word, taken apart.
Latin commodus: com-, with, plus modus, a measure — the same modus inside el modo.
Something commodus was "with measure": the right size, fitted to its purpose, neither too much nor too little. From there the road to "comfortable" is short, because a thing that fits is a thing that does not chafe.
The measuring sense survives in English accommodate, to make something fit, and in commodity, which began as a thing that suited you before it meant a sack of wheat. Spanish keeps the plainest version: a chair that fits you is cómodo, and so is a time that fits your day.

Lesson 5 of 6.
(el almacén, la ruta, el seguimiento, cómodo) — from cold.

Warm-up.
[pause 3 seconds]
Close the lessons before this one. Say all four: the warehouse, the route, the tracking, comfortable.

Grammar Lens: three accents, three different jobs.
Two of this chapter's words carry a written accent, and they carry it for opposite reasons:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
word: almacén. why the mark is there: stress on the last syllable, ending in n.
word: cómodo. why the mark is there: stress three syllables from the end.
Spanish defaults to the second-to-last syllable for a word ending in a vowel, n or s. Almacén breaks that by going later; cómodo breaks it by going earlier. Both need a mark to say so, and ruta and seguimiento need none because both obey the default.
That is why almacenes loses the accent while cómodos keeps it. The mark is a correction to a rule, not a decoration attached to a word, so it appears and disappears as the word changes shape.

Grammar Lens: two of the four were already yours.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
new word: la ruta. what it was built on: roto — Latin via rupta, a broken way.
new word: el seguimiento. what it was built on: seguir plus the -miento of entrenamiento.
new word: cómodo. what it was built on: el modo, a measure, with com- in front.
La ruta is the one to keep. You learned roto for a plate in two halves, and the same Latin participle names a road — because a road is what you get when something in the way has been broken through.
Only el almacén arrived from outside the family, and it came from Arabic with its article still stuck to the front.

[your turn — say: where the parcel is going now]

Why it's said this way.
A la hora que le resulte más cómoda is the phrase to take away whole. It is how Spanish says "whenever suits you", and cómoda there has nothing to do with comfort — it is the convenience half of the word, agreeing with la hora.
Then notice what the card does not say. There is no nosotros after the first line: su paquete vuelve, puede recogerlo. Spanish keeps the company out of it and lets the parcel be the subject. The one place the firm appears — hemos pasado — is the one fact it is claiming responsibility for.
And consulte is the usted command again, the register of every counter and every card of this kind.

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Now your turn, out loud and then in writing. Four lines for a delivery card: say nobody was in, say the parcel goes back to the warehouse, give a tracking number to check, and offer a time that suits them.
Then check yourself: did cómoda agree with la hora, and did you use the usted command?
