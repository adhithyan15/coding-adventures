Hindi, chapter 88: Four Adjectives That Do Not Agree.
2 lessons. The first lesson already needs your eyes or your hands, so save this one for when you have stopped.


Lesson 1 of 2.
आसान / मुश्किल (āsān / mushkil) — easy, difficult, and two words that refuse to agree.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, because the lesson points at something written down. You can listen to all of it now and come back to the parts that need looking at once you have stopped.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
You know that काला changes its ending to match the noun and सफ़ेद never does. Before reading on: which of those two do you expect a word borrowed from Persian to behave like?

You'll want to know: आसान and मुश्किल.
आसान (āsān) — "easy." मुश्किल (mushkil) — "difficult."
Both are borrowed, and both behave like सफ़ेद: they sit in front of the noun and do not change ending for anything. Whatever the noun's gender or number, the adjective is the same shape you learned.

The word, taken apart.
आसान comes from Persian āsān, "easy" — a plain everyday word in both languages, with no complication to report, which is fitting.
मुश्किल is the more interesting half. It is Arabic mushkil, from the root ش-ك-ل (sh-k-l), which is about form and shape. A mushkil thing is one whose shape is unclear — something you cannot make out the outline of. The same root gives shakl, "shape," a word many Hindi speakers also use.
So "difficult" in Hindi is not "heavy" or "hard" but "hard to make out." That is a different metaphor from the English one, and worth carrying with you.

Grammar Lens: which adjectives bend, and why.
Line the four up:
[a table of 4 rows, read aloud]
adjective: काला. origin: Sanskrit, ends in -आ. changes ending?: yes.
adjective: सफ़ेद. origin: Persian loan. changes ending?: no.
adjective: आसान. origin: Persian loan. changes ending?: no.
adjective: मुश्किल. origin: Arabic loan. changes ending?: no.
There is a pattern under this, and it is about shape rather than about borrowing. A Hindi adjective bends when it ends in -आ; otherwise it stays put. Loanwords land on the "no" side so often because they mostly arrive ending in a consonant.
That means you do not have to remember a list. Look at the ending.

Wrap-up Recall.

Lesson 2 of 2.
सुंदर / बदसूरत (sundar / badsūrat) — beautiful, ugly, and a prefix you already own.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
You met सुप्रभात as "good morning." Say it again and listen to the first syllable. You are about to meet it a second time, in a word that has nothing to do with mornings.

You'll want to know: सुंदर and बदसूरत.
सुंदर (sundar) — "beautiful." बदसूरत (badsūrat) — "ugly."
Neither ends in -आ, so neither bends. You can put both to work with what you learned last lesson and nothing else.

The word, taken apart.
सुंदर opens with Sanskrit सु- — the prefix meaning "good, well." It is the same सु- sitting at the front of सुप्रभात ("good dawn"). Once you have noticed it, you will keep finding it: सु- marks the favourable version of whatever follows.
बदसूरत is two borrowings glued together. बद is Persian for "bad," and सूरत is Arabic ṣūrat, "face" or "form." Ugly, in Hindi, is literally "bad-faced."
बद and English bad look like the same word and are not related. The resemblance is one of the most famous coincidences in etymology — two unconnected languages landing on nearly the same sound for nearly the same meaning. Enjoy it, and do not build a rule on it.
So the pair is built from opposite halves of Hindi's vocabulary: सुंदर is Sanskrit through and through, बदसूरत is Persian plus Arabic. The language names beauty with its inherited words and ugliness with its borrowed ones, which is an accident of history rather than a judgement, but it is a useful one to notice — it tells you which dictionary each word's relatives live in.
