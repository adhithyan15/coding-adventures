# Herbst and harvest

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GE-LEX-HERBST-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say *Herbst* once more and then say the English word **harvest**.
Those are the same word.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[GE-ETYMON-HERBST-HARVEST-01]; assesses=[GE-LEX-HERBST-01] -->

Germanic *harbistaz*, "the reaping season," went into both languages and then
each did something different with it.

| language | what it kept | what it did next |
|---|---|---|
| German | *Herbst*, the **season** | nothing — it was enough |
| English | *harvest*, the **act** | borrowed Latin *autumn* for the season |

English narrowed the inherited word to the *gathering itself* — you harvest a
field — and then found it had no word left for the time of year, so it took one
from Latin. German never split the meaning and never needed the loan.

## Why it's said this way
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GE-ETYMON-HERBST-HARVEST-01, GE-GRAMMAR-NATIVE-VS-LOAN-01, GE-LEX-JAHRESZEIT-01] -->

This is the clearest example in the book of something worth knowing: two
languages can inherit **the same word** and end up looking unrelated, without
either of them doing anything strange.

Nobody replaced *harvest*. Its meaning drifted a little, a gap opened where the
season used to be, and a Latin word was standing nearby. That is how most
borrowing happens — not because a language lacks a word, but because one of its
words moved.

And it is the last turn of this chapter's argument. The months were bought
outright. The seasons were not, and *Herbst* is the one place you can watch the
English side buy what German kept.
