## The two words — Honest irregularity
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[HI-CONCEPT-C22-GYARAH-BEES-01] -->

Unlike Spanish's transparent *dieciséis*-*diecinueve*, Hindi's 11-18 each
descend from their own Sanskrit number-word (*ekādaśa, dvādaśa,
trayodaśa*...) through individually unpredictable erosion. Most end in
**-rah**, a worn-down echo of Sanskrit's "-daśa" ("ten"), but each front half
has to be memorized one by one, the way English speakers memorize "eleven" and
"twelve".

## The two words — उन्नीस
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[HI-CONCEPT-C22-GYARAH-BEES-03]; assesses=[] -->

Here's the honest exception: **उन्नीस** (*unnīs*, 19) most likely continues
Sanskrit's **own subtractive** name for 19: **ऊनविंशति** (*ūnaviṃśati*), "**one
less than twenty**" (*ūna*, "less," + *viṃśati*, "twenty" — a shortened form of
the Vedic *ekonaviṃśati*, with *eka*, "one," dropped). Sanskrit named every
number ending in 9 up through 99 this way.

That is the logic of **Latin's ūndēvīgintī** ("one from twenty"), from the
numbers-11-20 lesson: Indo-Aryan and Italic, separated for thousands of years,
each landed on nineteen as twenty minus one.

## The two words — बीस
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[HI-CONCEPT-C22-GYARAH-BEES-04]; assesses=[] -->

**बीस** (*bīs*, "twenty") continues Sanskrit **विंशति** (*viṃśati*, "twenty").
