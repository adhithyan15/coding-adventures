Latin, chapter 487: North wind, South wind, West wind, To blow through, Clear weather.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
aquilō, aquilōnis — the north wind; the north.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to be stiff with cold, then the Latin for to feel cold.

You'll want to know: aquilō, aquilōnis.
aquilō, aquilōnis — "the north wind; the north".
Hieme aquilō ventus frīgidus est. In winter the north wind is a cold wind.
Third declension, masculine: aquilō, aquilōnis m. A cold, stormy wind.

The word, taken apart.
The origin of aquilō is not settled. Lewis & Short suggest aqua 'water', for its rain, or aquilus 'dark', for its storm clouds; EtymDB gives no parent.

Lesson 2 of 5.
auster, austrī — the south wind; the south.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to feel cold, then the Latin for the north wind.

You'll want to know: auster, austrī.
auster, austrī — "the south wind; the south".
Auster calidus nūbēs et pluviam fert. The warm south wind brings clouds and rain.
Second declension, masculine: auster, austrī m. English austral comes from it.

The word, taken apart.
auster is inherited; EtymDB lists English east and Latin aurōra 'dawn' as cousins, while Lewis & Short derive it from a root meaning 'to burn', the hot wind. English austral comes from it.

Lesson 3 of 5.
favōnius, favōniī — the west wind (of spring).

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for the north wind, then the Latin for the south wind.

You'll want to know: favōnius, favōniī.
favōnius, favōniī — "the west wind (of spring)".
Vēre favōnius venit, et flōrēs aperiuntur. In spring the west wind comes, and the flowers open.
Second declension, masculine: favōnius, favōniī m. The mild wind that brings spring.

The word, taken apart.
favōnius is built on faveō 'to be kind, to favour' (EtymDB; Lewis & Short): the kindly wind of spring. The Alpine wind name Föhn is usually traced to it.

Lesson 4 of 5.
perflō, perflāre — to blow through, to blow across.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for the south wind, then the Latin for the west wind.

You'll want to know: perflō, perflāre.
perflō, perflāre — "to blow through, to blow across".
Ventus magnus agrōs perflat, et arborēs moventur. A strong wind blows across the fields, and the trees move.
perflō, perflāre, perflāvī, perflātum, with an accusative of the place. The simple verb flō 'to blow' gives English inflate through īnflāre.

The word, taken apart.
perflō is built from per 'through' and flō 'to blow' (Lewis & Short). flō is inherited, and EtymDB lists English blow as a cousin; English inflate and flatulent come from flō.

Lesson 5 of 5.
serēnitās, serēnitātis — clear weather, fair weather; calm.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for the west wind, then the Latin for to blow through.

You'll want to know: serēnitās, serēnitātis.
serēnitās, serēnitātis — "clear weather, fair weather; calm".
Post ventum serēnitās est, et caelum caeruleum est. After the wind the weather is clear, and the sky is blue.
Third declension, feminine: serēnitās, serēnitātis f., from serēnus 'clear'. English serenity comes from it.

The word, taken apart.
serēnitās is built on serēnus 'clear, cloudless' with the suffix -itās (EtymDB; Lewis & Short). English serenity comes from it.
