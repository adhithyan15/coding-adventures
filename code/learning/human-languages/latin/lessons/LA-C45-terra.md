## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[LA-LEX-C45-WORLD-01]; assesses=[] -->

**terra** — "**the earth**," and specifically **dry land**.

That contrast is built into the word. *Terra* is what is **not** sea — and the
Romans named the Mediterranean on exactly that pattern, as *the sea in the middle
of the land*. You will have the second half of that name shortly, and when you do
the whole thing stops being a proper noun and becomes a description you can
read.

English took *terra* freely: **terrain**, **terrestrial**, **subterranean**, a
**terrace** (a raised bank of earth), and a **terrier** — a dog that goes to
**ground** after its quarry. Even **inter**, to bury, is *in* plus *terra*: to
put into the earth.

And *terra firma*, "solid ground," is still said in Latin because no English
phrase does the job as briefly.

English **earth** is Germanic and unrelated. Another coincidence worth naming
rather than assuming.
