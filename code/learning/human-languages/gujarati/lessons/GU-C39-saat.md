# સાત (sāt) — seven, and the place the balance turns

- [YOU RECALL: say *second*, and the shape it shares with *third* — **R2**, five lessons back]
- [YOU RECALL: write **જો** — **R3**, twenty lessons back]

**જો** is two signs. **સાત** is three, and you own all three: **સ**, the
attached **ા**, and **ત**.

## You'll want to know: સાત
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[GU-LEX-SAAT]; assesses=[GU-LEX-CHHA-SIX, GU-LEX-NUMBERS-ONE-TO-FIVE] -->

> **સાત** — *sāt* — **seven**

**એક, બે, ત્રણ, ચાર, પાંચ, છ, સાત.**

This is the first number past the exception list, and that changes what a
number is worth to you. **-મું** does not build on **છ**. It does build on
**સાત**:

> **સાતમું** — *sātmũ* — **seventh**

You were not taught that word. You assembled it, out of a number you learned
thirty seconds ago and an ending you have had for five lessons. **બીજું** and
**ત્રીજું** could not be assembled from anything; they had to be handed to you
whole. From here up, they do not.

## The word, taken apart — a cluster you have seen collapse before
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GU-LEX-SAAT, GU-SOUND-DV-BB-B] -->

Wiktionary hands **સાત** down from Prakrit *satta*, and that from Sanskrit
*saptá*.

Watch the middle of the word: *pt* → *tt* → *t*. The cluster does not simplify
in one step; it first becomes a doubled consonant, and only then thins to a
single one. That is exactly the road chapter twelve walked for **બે** — *dv* →
*bb* → *b*.

And the word did not simply shrink. Where Sanskrit had a short *a* before two
consonants, Gujarati has a long **ા** before one. The syllable kept its weight
and paid for the lost consonant with a longer vowel.
