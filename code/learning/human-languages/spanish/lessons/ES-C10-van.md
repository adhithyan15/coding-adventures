# van — and the four are done

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-GRAMMAR-IR-VERB-PRESENT-1PL, ES-GRAMMAR-AR-PRESENT-3PL] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say "we go." (*Vamos*.) And "they speak." (*Hablan*.)

## Grammar Lens: the last form
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-GRAMMAR-IR-VERB-PRESENT-3PL]; assesses=[ES-GRAMMAR-AR-PRESENT-3PL, ES-LEX-USTEDES, ES-ETYMON-IRE-VADERE] -->

> **van** — *they go*, and **ustedes van**, *you all go*.

Latin's form was *vadunt*. Spanish wore it down to three letters, and the rule
still holds visibly: a final **-n**, and the subject is plural and not you.

## What you've built
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-GRAMMAR-SER-PRESENT-COMPLETE, ES-GRAMMAR-ESTAR-PRESENT-COMPLETE, ES-GRAMMAR-TENER-PRESENT-3PL, ES-GRAMMAR-IR-VERB-PRESENT-3PL] -->

Four chapters ago you could conjugate any *regular* verb in the present, and
none of the irregular ones. Now the four commonest verbs in Spanish are complete
in every person:

**ser** · **estar** · **tener** · **ir** — *to be*, *to be*, *to have*, *to go*.

And each one taught something that outlives it:

- ***ser*** — two Latin verbs fused, so the seam shows at *eres*.
- ***estar*** — one verb, so it is regular; the accent only marks stress.
- ***tener*** — the stem breaks exactly where it is stressed, in **every**
  stem-changing verb, which is why *nosotros* and *vosotros* never break.
- ***ir*** — its present belongs to a different verb entirely.

Three of those four are about the same underlying thing: **a verb that looks
chaotic is usually more than one word**, and the places it looks regular are the
places one word survived intact.
