Malayalam, chapter 111: It Has Happened.
4 lessons. You can do the first 3 of them in the car; after that you will want to have stopped.


Lesson 1 of 4.
വന്നിട്ട് (vanniṭṭŭ) — having come.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say he came.
[pause 2 seconds]
Then say the describing form you made from it — the teacher who came.

You'll want to know.
വന്നിട്ട് (vanniṭṭŭ) — having come.
It is the past form with -ഇട്ട് put on the end.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
വന്നു, he came.
വന്നിട്ട് (vanniṭṭŭ), having come.

Grammar Lens: one past form, two directions.
You have now made two things out of the same past form, and they were made in opposite ways.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
വന്ന, the ു taken off — describes a noun.
വന്നിട്ട് (vanniṭṭŭ), -ഇട്ട് put on — leans toward a verb.
The describing form was made by taking a vowel sign away. This one is made by adding, and what it reaches for is different: വന്ന wants a noun to sit in front of, while വന്നിട്ട് (vanniṭṭŭ) wants something else to follow — another verb, or the word that closes the sentence.
On its own it feels unfinished, and that is the point. Saying vanniṭṭŭ and stopping leaves a listener waiting, the way having come does in English. The next page gives it the word it is waiting for.

Lesson 2 of 4.
വന്നിട്ടുണ്ട് (vanniṭṭuṇṭŭ) — he has come.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say having come.
[pause 2 seconds]
Then say the word that means there is — the one that also does somebody has.

You'll want to know.
അവൻ വന്നിട്ടുണ്ട്. avan vanniṭṭuṇṭŭ — "He has come."
Put the two together and nothing is left over.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
വന്നിട്ട് (vanniṭṭŭ), having come.
ഉണ്ട്, there is.
They run together in speech into വന്നിട്ടുണ്ട് (vanniṭṭuṇṭŭ), and that is the whole of the construction: the waiting form, and the word it was waiting for.

Grammar Lens: the word you already knew did the work.
ഉണ്ട് was given to you as there is and somebody has. That second job is the one showing here. English builds its perfect on have — he has come — and Malayalam reaches for the word it already uses for having.
So the two languages land in the same place from the same starting point, which is worth noticing precisely because so little else about the two verbs lines up.
What it says that a plain past does not is that the coming is done and bears on now. avan vannu reports the event. avan vanniṭṭuṇṭŭ tells you he is here — the arriving happened, and you are being told about its result.

Lesson 3 of 4.
വന്നു — വന്നിട്ടുണ്ട് (vannu — vanniṭṭuṇṭŭ).

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say he came, then he has come.

Grammar Lens: which one a situation asks for.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
അവൻ വന്നു, reports the event.
അവൻ വന്നിട്ടുണ്ട് (vanniṭṭuṇṭŭ), reports where that leaves things.
Both are true of the same arrival. Which you pick depends on what the person listening needs: whether you are telling them what happened, or telling them how matters stand because of it.
A useful test while the difference is settling: if you could follow your sentence with …so he is here now without repeating yourself, the second form was the one you wanted.

Lesson 4 of 4.
(vannu, vanna, vanniṭṭŭ, vanniṭṭuṇṭŭ) (naalu roopam) — from cold.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, because the lesson points at something written down. You can listen to all of it now and come back to the parts that need looking at once you have stopped.

Warm-up.
[pause 3 seconds]
Close the lessons before this one. Say having come, then he has come.

Grammar Lens: four shapes off one past form.
[a table of 4 rows, read aloud]
വന്നു, he came.
വന്ന, that came — a vowel sign taken off.
വന്നിട്ട് (vanniṭṭŭ), having come — -ഇട്ട് put on.
വന്നിട്ടുണ്ട് (vanniṭṭuṇṭŭ), he has come — that, plus ഉണ്ട്.
Every row after the first is the row above it with one change. Nothing in this chapter asked you to learn a word; it asked you to notice what a form you already had could be turned into.

What you've built.
You could say that something happened, and not that it has happened. The difference matters more than it looks: answering is he here? with the plain past reports yesterday, and leaves the question standing.
The word that closed the gap was one you had held for a long time. ഉണ്ട് was given to you as there is and somebody has, and it is the second job that builds this. English does the same thing with have, which is a coincidence worth having noticed, because very little else about the two verbs matches.
