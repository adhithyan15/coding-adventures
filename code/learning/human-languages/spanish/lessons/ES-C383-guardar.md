## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-GUARDAR]; assesses=[] -->

**guardar** — "to keep, to put away."

This one is not Latin. It comes from a Germanic verb **wardōn**, to watch over —
the same root as English **ward**, in the sense of watching and protecting.

The interesting part is the first sound. Latin and its descendants had no **w-**
at the start of a word, so when Germanic words arrived, Romance speakers reached
for the nearest thing they had and produced **gu-**. *Wardōn* became *guardar*.

Now watch what that does to English, which borrowed from both sides.

English already had **ward** from its own Germanic stock. Then it borrowed the
French version of the very same word and got **guard**. Both are in the
dictionary, meaning almost the same thing, and most speakers never notice.

The pattern repeats down the whole family:

- a **warden** and a **guardian**
- a **reward** and a **regard**
- **warranty** and **guarantee**
- and the names **William** and **Guillermo**, which are one name

Each pair is one word that entered English twice, once before the sound change
and once after. Whenever you meet an English pair where one has *w-* and the
other *gu-*, you are looking at this.
