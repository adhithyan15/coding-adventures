# How are you, joined up

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[AR-CONCEPT-C03-PRACTICE-01, AR-CONCEPT-C03-KAYFA-HALUKA-02] -->

[PAUSE 4s] Ask after a woman and answer as yourself, both parts. Then answer again
using one of the joining words this chapter gave you.

## The exchange
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[AR-CONCEPT-C03-PRACTICE-01, AR-CONCEPT-C03-PRACTICE-02, AR-CONCEPT-C03-BI-KHAYR-02, AR-CONCEPT-C03-AL-HAMDU-LILLAH-02] -->

- [YOU SAY: ask a man, then a woman — *kayfa ḥāluka?* … *kayfa ḥāluki?*]
- [YOU SAY: answer with both replies joined — *bi-khayr wa-l-ḥamdu lillāh*]
- [YOU SAY: answer with a reservation — *bi-khayr, lākin …*]
- [YOU SAY: accept what you hear — *ḥasanan*]

For a long stretch this exchange could only be said one way, because there was
nothing to join it with. This chapter is the first time the answer can carry two
clauses.

## Grammar Lens: what the question actually asks
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[AR-CONCEPT-C03-HAL-01, AR-CONCEPT-C03-HAL-02, AR-CONCEPT-C03-KAYFA-02, AR-CONCEPT-C03-KAYFA-HALUKA-02] -->

English asks after **you**. Arabic asks after your **حال** — your *state*.

**حال** *ḥāl* is *ḥāʾ · alif · lām*, with the deep throat *ḥ*, and its root
**ḥ–w–l** means **to turn, to change**. So the question is not *are you well* but
*how stands the thing about you that shifts*.

**كيف** is the question word, partner to **ما**, and the phrase bolts three owned
pieces together: *how* + *state* + *your*. There is no verb in it at all, exactly
as there is none in the name sentence.

## How to answer: two replies, both literal
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[AR-CONCEPT-C03-BI-KHAYR-02, AR-CONCEPT-C03-AL-HAMDU-LILLAH-02] -->

| reply | literally |
|---|---|
| **بخير** *bi-khayr* | **in goodness** — **بـ** (*in*) plus **خير** (*good*) |
| **الحمد لله** *al-ḥamdu lillāh* | **the praise is to God** — root **ḥ–m–d**, to praise |

**خير** was hiding in the morning greeting long before it was named, and *al-ḥamdu
lillāh* comes back more often in real speech than *bi-khayr* does. Neither is a
polite noise: both say something, and both say it with a preposition glued to the
front.
