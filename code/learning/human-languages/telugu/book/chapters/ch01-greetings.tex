\chapter{Greetings}
\label{ch:greetings}
\hlchaptermodality{1}

The Telugu greetings and the words that oil a first conversation --- and,
taught through them, how to read the Telugu script and how Telugu sits inside
the Dravidian family. Each lesson introduces the letters its word needs, so you
learn to read the word and understand it at once.

\section{\te{నమస్కారం} \emph{(namaskāram)} --- ``greetings''}
\label{lesson:namaskaram}

\begin{sounds}
Telugu reads \textbf{left to right}, and every consonant carries a built-in
``a'' (many under a little check-mark hat, the \emph{talakaṭṭu}). \te{న} na,
\te{మ} ma, \te{ర} ra. A \textbf{vowel sign} changes it: \te{క} ka $+$ long-ā
$\to$ \te{కా} kā. A vowel-less consonant \textbf{stacks below} the next as a
conjunct: \te{స} tucks under \emph{ka} $\to$ \te{స్క} ``ska.'' And the
\textbf{anusvāra} \te{ం} adds a final nasal ``-ṁ.'' Read
\te{న·మ·స్·కా·ర·ం} $\to$ \emph{namaskāram}.
\end{sounds}

\begin{cousinweb}
\te{నమస్కారం} (\emph{namaskāram}) is \textbf{Sanskrit}: \emph{namas} (``a bow,
homage'') $+$ \emph{kāra} (``a making,'' root \emph{kṛ}, ``to do''), with a
Telugu ending $=$ ``the making of a bow.'' The same word as Hindi
\emph{namaskār} and Kannada \emph{namaskāra}. Telugu borrowed \emph{heavily}
from Sanskrit --- often called the most Sanskritised Dravidian tongue --- where
its sister Tamil kept the home-grown \emph{vaṇakkam}.
\end{cousinweb}

\subsection*{Across the family --- the same idea, six ways}
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
Language & ``Hello'' & Source \\
\midrule
\textbf{Telugu} & \te{నమస్కారం} \emph{namaskāram} & Sanskrit \\
Kannada & \emph{namaskāra} & Sanskrit \\
Malayalam & \emph{namaskāram} & Sanskrit \\
Hindi & \emph{namaste} & Sanskrit \\
Tamil & \emph{vaṇakkam} & \textbf{native Dravidian} \\
English & \emph{hello} & Germanic \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Tamil alone kept its own word.

\begin{culture}
Said with the palms pressed together and a small bow --- the gesture \emph{is}
the word. Both \textbf{hello and goodbye}, any time, to almost anyone.
\end{culture}

\section{\te{ధన్యవాదములు} --- ``thank you''}
\label{lesson:dhanyavadamulu}

\begin{sounds}
New: \te{ధ} \emph{dha} (aspirated \emph{d}), the conjunct \te{న్య} (\emph{na}
stacked with \emph{ya}), then \te{వా·ద·ము·లు}. Read
\te{ధ·న్య·వా·ద·ము·లు} $\to$ \emph{dhanyavādamulu}.
\end{sounds}

\begin{cousinweb}
The core is \textbf{Sanskrit}: \emph{dhanya} (``worthy,'' from \emph{dhana},
``wealth'') $+$ \emph{vāda} (``a saying,'' root \emph{vad}). Telugu adds its own
\textbf{plural ending \emph{-mulu}}, so \emph{dhanyavāda-mulu} $=$
``worthy-sayings,'' thanks in the plural of courtesy (also the shorter
\emph{dhanyavādālu}). Same Sanskrit stem as Hindi \emph{dhanyavād}.
\end{cousinweb}

\begin{grammarlens}
\textbf{Telugu builds words by gluing endings on.} \emph{-mulu} is your first
taste of Dravidian \emph{agglutination}: meaning is stacked by suffixes on a
stem, not by separate little words. Later endings mark case (``to,'' ``from,''
``with'') the same way. In Telugu, grammar is mostly \textbf{suffixes}.
\end{grammarlens}

\subsection*{Across the family --- the same idea, five ways}
\begin{center}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}llX@{}}
\toprule
Language & ``Thanks'' & Note \\
\midrule
\textbf{Telugu} & \te{ధన్యవాదములు} \emph{dhanyavādamulu} & Sanskrit stem + Telugu plural \\
Kannada & \emph{dhanyavāda} & Sanskrit --- same stem \\
Hindi & \emph{dhanyavād} & Sanskrit --- same stem \\
Tamil & \emph{naṉṟi} & \textbf{native} (``goodness'') \\
Malayalam & \emph{nandi} & \textbf{native} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

\section{\te{అవును} \emph{(avunu)} --- ``yes''}
\label{lesson:avunu}

\begin{sounds}
\te{అ} is the \textbf{independent vowel} ``a'' (word-initial). \te{వు} is
\emph{vu}, \te{ను} is \emph{nu} (each a consonant with the ``u'' sign). Read
\te{అ·వు·ను} $\to$ \emph{avunu}.
\end{sounds}

\begin{cousinweb}
\te{అవును} (\emph{avunu}) is the everyday native ``yes / it is so'' --- close
at root to the verb of being, which is why its ``no'' partner \emph{kādu} (``is
not'') is its exact mirror.
\end{cousinweb}

\begin{grammarlens}
Telugu's yes/no are little \textbf{statements of being}: \emph{avunu} (``it is
[so]'') vs.\ \emph{kādu} (``it is not''). A Dravidian instinct --- answer by
asserting or denying existence, not with a bare particle. You'll also hear a
``yes'' echoed as the question's own verb.
\end{grammarlens}

\section{\te{లేదు} \emph{(lēdu)} --- ``no,'' where Telugu parts ways with its sisters}
\label{lesson:ledu}

\begin{sounds}
\te{లే} is \emph{lē} (\te{ల} \emph{la} with the long-``ē'' sign); \te{దు} is
\emph{du}. Read \te{లే·దు} $\to$ \emph{lēdu}.
\end{sounds}

\begin{cousinweb}
\te{లేదు} (\emph{lēdu}) means ``there is not, it is absent'' --- native
Dravidian, but on a \textbf{different root} (\emph{lē-}) from the \emph{il-} of
Tamil and Kannada. Telugu also has \te{కాదు} (\emph{kādu}), ``it is not
[that],'' so it splits ``no'' in two: \emph{lēdu} denies \textbf{existence}
(\emph{pustakam lēdu}, ``there is no book''); \emph{kādu} denies
\textbf{identity} (\emph{idi pustakam kādu}, ``this is not a book'').
\end{cousinweb}

\begin{grammarlens}
\textbf{Two ``no''s, existence vs.\ identity.} English leans on one flat
``no''; Telugu keeps \emph{lēdu} (``there isn't'') and \emph{kādu} (``isn't
that''). Choosing between them is choosing \emph{what} you deny --- a Dravidian
trait Telugu draws most sharply, and with a different root-word from its
sisters.
\end{grammarlens}

\subsection*{Across the family --- the same idea, five ways}
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
Language & ``No / isn't'' & Note \\
\midrule
\textbf{Telugu} & \te{లేదు} / \te{కాదు} \emph{lēdu / kādu} & native, \textbf{different root} \\
Tamil & \emph{illai} & native, root \emph{il-} \\
Kannada & \emph{illa} & native, root \emph{il-} \\
Malayalam & \emph{illa} & native, root \emph{il-} \\
Hindi & \emph{nahīṁ} & Indo-European (unrelated) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Three sisters share \emph{il-}; Telugu alone picked \emph{lē-} / \emph{kā-} ---
the family is real, but not uniform.

\section{\te{సరే} \emph{(sarē)} --- ``okay,'' the family word in Telugu dress}
\label{lesson:sare}

\begin{sounds}
\te{స} is \emph{sa}; \te{రే} is \emph{rē} (\te{ర} \emph{ra} with the long-``ē''
sign you just met on \emph{lē}). Read \te{స·రే} $\to$ \emph{sarē}.
\end{sounds}

\begin{cousinweb}
\te{సరే} (\emph{sarē}) is native Dravidian ``correct, right'' $\to$ ``okay,
agreed'' --- the verbal nod of Telugu. It is the same family word as Tamil and
Kannada \emph{sari}, worn to \emph{sarē}; doubled, \emph{sarē sarē} $=$
``alright, alright.''
\end{cousinweb}

\begin{grammarlens}
\textbf{One Dravidian word, three scripts.} \emph{sari / sarē} is essentially
one word and meaning dressed in Tamil, Kannada, and Telugu letters. Spotting a
shared word change its shape but not its sense is the fastest way to feel the
family --- and the whole point of learning these four side by side.
\end{grammarlens}

\section{Recap, and how Telugu says goodbye}
\label{lesson:te-c01-practice}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}llll@{}}
\toprule
Read & & Meaning & Origin \\
\midrule
\te{నమస్కారం} & \emph{namaskāram} & hello & Sanskrit \\
\te{ధన్యవాదములు} & \emph{dhanyavādamulu} & thank you & Sanskrit + Telugu plural \\
\te{అవును} & \emph{avunu} & yes & native \\
\te{లేదు} & \emph{lēdu} & no / isn't & native (root \emph{lē-}, not \emph{il-}) \\
\te{సరే} & \emph{sarē} & okay & native (= \emph{sari}) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The familiar pattern --- Sanskrit for the greetings, native for the everyday
grammar --- with Telugu's own twist: its ``no'' takes a different native root
from its sisters.
