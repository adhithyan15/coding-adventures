# torcerse — the ankle that turns the wrong way

<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C438-CUERPO-02, ES-LEX-C314-BODY-03, ES-LEX-C293-BODY-05] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say **la natación**, **el pie** and **el tobillo**. Getting out of the pool, the tiled
edge is wet. Name the part of you that is about to go wrong.

## You'll want to know: torcerse
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-C438-CUERPO-03]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C314-BODY-03, ES-LEX-C293-BODY-05] -->

**torcer** — to twist something. **torcerse** — to twist a part of yourself.

*Me he torcido el pie* — I have twisted my foot. This is the sentence a Spanish
doctor's waiting room hears twenty times a week, and it is the one you want
ready before you need it.

Spanish does not say *my* foot here. It says **el** pie and lets **me** carry the
ownership: *me torcí **el** tobillo*, not *mi tobillo*. When the body part is
yours and the verb is reflexive, the possessive is redundant and Spanish drops it.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C438-CUERPO-03] -->

Latin **torquēre**, to twist. It is one of those roots that stayed busy.

| word | the twist in it |
|---|---|
| torcer | a joint, the wrong way |
| torcido | crooked, not straight |
| antorcha | a torch, of twisted rags |

English took the same root twice over: **torque**, the twisting force on a
shaft, and **torture**, which needs no explanation once you know the root.

## Grammar Lens: the stem that breaks
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C438-CUERPO-03, ES-GRAMMAR-STEM-O-UE-06] -->

**dormir** taught you that some verbs split their vowel when the stress lands on
it: *dormir* but *duermo*. **torcer** does the same thing.

| form | vowel |
|---|---|
| torcer, torcemos | **o**, unstressed |
| me tuerzo, se tuerce | **ue**, stressed |

What decides it is stress, not person. Wherever the stress falls on the
stem vowel, the **o** breaks into **ue**; wherever the ending pulls the stress
away, the **o** holds. That is why *torcemos* keeps its **o** while *tuerce*
does not — and why you never have to memorise which forms change.
