\section*{The three facts}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Consonants carry a built-in ``a.''} \gu{ક} ka, \gu{ન} na,
    \gu{મ} ma --- written left to right. The shapes are Devanagari's, minus one
    thing (see below).
  \item \textbf{A vowel sign changes it.} \gu{કા} k\=a, \gu{કિ} ki, \gu{કી}
    k\=\i, \gu{કુ} ku, \gu{કે} ke, \gu{કો} ko. The \emph{i}-sign \gu{િ} is
    written \emph{before} the consonant but read \emph{after} it. An
    \emph{anusv\=ara} \gu{ં} nasalises the vowel.
  \item \textbf{A \emph{virama} removes the vowel}, and the bare consonant joins
    the next as a conjunct: \gu{સ} $+$ \gu{્} $+$ \gu{ત} $\to$ \gu{સ્ત}.
\end{enumerate}

\section*{Worth flagging}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{No top line.} This is the one thing to remember. Devanagari,
    Bengali, and Gurmukhi hang their letters from a horizontal bar (the
    \emph{shirorekh\=a}); Gujarati cut it away, so its letters stand free. It is
    the fastest way to tell Gujarati from Hindi at a glance --- ``the headless
    script.''
  \item \textbf{The extra letter \gu{ળ} (\d{l}).} A \textbf{retroflex l}, tongue
    curled back --- shared with Marathi and the Dravidian south, absent from
    Hindi. It appears in \emph{ma\d{l}vũ} (``to meet'').
  \item \textbf{Three genders.} Gujarati keeps Sanskrit's masculine, feminine,
    \emph{and} neuter (Hindi collapsed to two), marking gender on adjectives and
    possessives: \emph{s\=aro} (m.) / \emph{s\=ar\=\i} (f.) / \emph{s\=arũ} (n.).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Gujarati in the family}

Gujarati is \textbf{Indo-Aryan} (from Sanskrit, like Hindi, Marathi, Punjabi,
Bengali), spoken in Gujarat and across a large diaspora --- the mother tongue of
Mahatma Gandhi. Its Sanskrit core is shared with Hindi; its heavy
\textbf{Perso-Arabic} layer (and a few Portuguese words from the coastal ports)
is the mark of centuries as a great trading language across the Arabian Sea. The
lessons trace both threads.
