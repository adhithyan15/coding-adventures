Latin, chapter 425: Roadbed, Flint, Kerb, Wheel rut, To pave.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
agger, aggeris — a raised roadbed; a mound, an embankment.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a winding, then the Latin for to bend.

You'll want to know: agger, aggeris.
agger, aggeris — "a raised roadbed; a mound, an embankment".
Via in aggere altō per campum it. The road runs across the plain on a high embankment.
Third declension, masculine: agger, aggeris m. A Roman road was laid on a raised bank of earth and stones. English exaggerate first meant 'to heap up'.

The word, taken apart.
agger is built on ad- 'to' and gerō 'to carry' (EtymDB; Lewis & Short): earth and stones carried to one spot. English exaggerate comes from Latin exaggerāre 'to heap up', built on it.

Lesson 2 of 5.
silex, silicis — a flint; a hard paving stone.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to bend, then the Latin for a raised roadbed.

You'll want to know: silex, silicis.
silex, silicis — "a flint; a hard paving stone".
Haec via ē silice facta est, itaque in pluviā quoque bona est. This road is made of hard stone, so it is good in the rain too.
Third declension, usually masculine: silex, silicis m. Roman roads were paved with blocks of it. English silica and silicon come from it.

The word, taken apart.
The origin of silex is not settled: Lewis & Short tentatively link it with a root meaning 'firm', as in solidus, and EtymDB gives no parent. EtymDB lists English silica and silicon among the words made from it.

Lesson 3 of 5.
crepīdō, crepīdinis — a raised edge; a kerb, a raised footway; a quay.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a raised roadbed, then the Latin for a flint.

You'll want to know: crepīdō, crepīdinis.
crepīdō, crepīdinis — "a raised edge; a kerb, a raised footway; a quay".
Viātōrēs in crepīdine ambulant, plaustra in mediā viā eunt. The travellers walk on the raised footway, the wagons go in the middle of the road.
Third declension, feminine: crepīdō, crepīdinis f. Any raised edge: the kerb of a street, the base of a building, the stone edge of a quay.

The word, taken apart.
crepīdō was borrowed from Greek krēpis, krēpidos 'foundation, base' (EtymDB; Lewis & Short). The Greek word also meant a kind of boot, the source of Latin crepida 'sandal'.

Lesson 4 of 5.
orbita, orbitae — a wheel rut, a track.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a flint, then the Latin for a raised edge.

You'll want to know: orbita, orbitae.
orbita, orbitae — "a wheel rut, a track".
In viā vetere altae orbitae sunt, et plaustrum lentē it. On the old road there are deep ruts, and the wagon goes slowly.
First declension, feminine: orbita, orbitae f. English orbit is the same word: the track a planet runs in.

The word, taken apart.
orbita is built on orbis 'circle, wheel' (EtymDB; Lewis & Short): the track a wheel leaves. English orbit comes from it.

Lesson 5 of 5.
sternō, sternere — to spread, to lay out; to pave (a road).

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a raised edge, then the Latin for a wheel rut.

You'll want to know: sternō, sternere.
sternō, sternere — "to spread, to lay out; to pave (a road)".
Fabrī viam novam saxīs sternunt. The workmen pave the new road with stones.
sternō, sternere, strāvī, strātum. Viam sternere, to pave a road; lectum sternere, to make a bed. Its participle strāta gives English street.

The word, taken apart.
sternō is inherited from Proto-Italic stornō, from PIE sterh₃- 'to stretch out, to spread' (EtymDB). Its participle in via strāta 'paved road' was borrowed into Germanic and became English street (EtymDB).
