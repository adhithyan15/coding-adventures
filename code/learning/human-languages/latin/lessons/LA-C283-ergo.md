# ergō, itaque, igitur — and so

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[LA-LEX-COGITO-01, LA-LEX-C113-WHY113-04] -->

[PAUSE 2s] The *cōgitō* lesson gave you a famous sentence. Say it. (*Cōgitō,
ergō sum.* I think, therefore I am.) The sentence is Descartes', written
long after Rome, but every word of it is Latin.

[PAUSE 1s] You also own **itaque**, "and so", and **igitur**, "therefore".
This lesson sorts all three.

## Grammar Lens: what happened next, and what follows
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[LA-LEX-C283-ERGO-01]; assesses=[LA-LEX-C178-CAUSA178-01, LA-GRAMMAR-C281-SCAENA-01] -->

**itaque** tells a story forward. It stands first, and what comes after it
is the next thing that happened, because of the last:

> **Pluēbat; itaque in vīllā mānsimus.** — It was raining, and so we stayed
> in the farmhouse.

[PAUSE 2s] **igitur** and **ergō** draw a conclusion. They belong to an
argument more than to a story. In Cicero's prose *igitur* usually comes
second, after the first word: *Fessus igitur es.* — So you are tired.

[PAUSE 1s] **ergō**, "therefore, so", often stands first, and it has the
sound of someone reaching the point: *Fessus es; ergō dormī.* — You are
tired; so sleep.

[PAUSE 1s] So: a story's *and so* is *itaque*; an argument's *therefore* is
*igitur* or *ergō*.

## The word, taken apart: ergō
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[LA-ETYMON-C283-ERGO-02]; assesses=[] -->

Before **ergō** meant "therefore", it meant "on account of", and it followed
a genitive: *virtūtis ergō*, "on account of courage", an old formula for
decorating a soldier (Lewis & Short). Lewis & Short build the word from
*ex*, "out of", and a root meaning "to stretch, to direct", but the origin
is not settled. The sense moved from "on account of this" to "so, then".
