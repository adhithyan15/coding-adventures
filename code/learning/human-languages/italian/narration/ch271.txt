Italian, chapter 271: If It Rains --- Real Conditions, and It Depends.
4 lessons. All 4 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 4.
se — if.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say "yes". (Sì.) Say "I don't know". (Non so.)
[pause 1 second]
Every sentence so far has said what is, or what was. This chapter says what follows if something else is true, and it begins with two letters.

Grammar Lens: se opens a condition.
se — "if".
Se sei stanco, dormi. — If you're tired, sleep. Se il treno è in ritardo, leggo. — If the train is late, I read.
Whatever follows se is the condition; the rest of the sentence is what follows from it. The condition can come first, with a comma, or second, without one: Leggo se il treno è in ritardo.
se also means "whether", after a verb of knowing or asking, and here your non so comes in useful:
Non so se è vero. — I don't know if it's true.

The word, taken apart: three small words.
Listen to how close these are:
se — "if", sì — "yes", with its accent, si — "oneself", as in come si chiama?
se is inherited from Latin sī, "if" (EtymDB 2.1; Lewis & Short, under si); the vowel opened from i to e. sì, "yes", is a different Latin word: chapter twenty-nine traced it to sīc, "thus". se has its own vowel, so the ear can tell it from the other two. sì and si sound exactly alike, and the accent on sì is there to keep those two apart on the page, where the ear cannot help.

[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Which half of se sei stanco, dormi is the condition? (Se sei stanco.) What does se mean after non so? (Whether, "if".) Which Latin words are behind se and sì? (Sī, "if", and sīc, "thus".)


Lesson 2 of 4.
se piove, resto a casa — the condition that really can happen.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say "if". (Se.) Say "it was raining". (Pioveva.) Now the present: "it's raining". (Piove.)

Grammar Lens: both halves in the present.
Se piove, resto a casa. — If it rains, I stay at home. Se il treno è in ritardo, prendo l'autobus. — If the train is late, I take the bus.
This is a real condition: it may well happen, and if it does, the result follows. Italian puts both halves in the plain present, as English does. There is nothing new to build. se plus a sentence you could already say, then another sentence you could already say.
The result half can also be advice, with the dovresti of chapter two hundred and sixty-six:
Se sei stanco, dovresti dormire. — If you're tired, you should sleep.
And it can be a plain instruction, as in the last lesson: se sei stanco, dormi.

Why it's said this way.
A real condition is a plan with a branch in it. Travel is full of them, and the station gives you two at once:
Se perdo il treno, prendo l'autobus. Se perdo l'autobus, resto qui.
Compare quindi. Piove, quindi resto a casa says it is raining, and here is the consequence. Se piove, resto a casa says it might, and here is what happens if so. Same consequence; the second one has not decided yet whether the cause is true.

The word, taken apart.
piovere is inherited from spoken Latin plŏvere (EtymDB 2.1), a reshaping of classical pluere, "to rain" (Lewis & Short, under pluo). The Latin pl became Italian pi, as in più from plūs and piegare from plicāre: three words, one sound change.

Lesson 3 of 4.
dipende — the condition you do not have to finish.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say "if it rains, I stay at home". (Se piove, resto a casa.)
[pause 1 second]
That sentence names its condition. Sometimes you want the condition without saying what it is.

You'll want to know: dipende.
dipende — "it depends".
— Vai alla festa domani? — Dipende. — Are you going to the party tomorrow? — It depends.
You have dipendere, "to depend". Dipende is its "it" form, and on its own it is a complete answer: it says a condition exists and declines to say which. That is exactly why it is so useful to a question you are not ready to answer.

Grammar Lens: dipende da.
Add da and you can name the condition after all:
Dipende da te. — It depends on you. Dipende dal treno. — It depends on the train.
da + il is dal, the same welding as al and del. English says depend on; Italian says dipende da, "hangs from", which is the older picture.
Or name it with a whole se: Dipende. Se piove, resto a casa. The dipende announces a condition; the se sentence states it.

The word, taken apart.
dipendere comes from Latin dēpendēre, "to hang from, to hang down" (Lewis & Short, under dependeo): dē, "down from", and pendēre, "to hang". To depend on something is to hang from it, and the answer swings until the condition settles. English pendant (a thing that hangs), pending (still hanging) and suspend share the root.

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
What does dipende say, and what does it not say? (That there is a condition — not which.) What do you add to name it? (Da: dipende da te.) What is hanging, in dēpendēre? (The answer, from the condition.)


Lesson 4 of 4.
Review — plans with a branch in them.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Someone asks Vai alla festa domani? Give the answer that decides nothing. (Dipende.)

What you've built.
Three ways now to talk about a cause, and they differ in how sure you are.
Piove, quindi resto a casa. — It is raining: decided. Se piove, resto a casa. — It might: the branch is ready. Dipende. — Not even the branch is named yet.
Put them together at a ticket window and you have a whole conversation:
— Vorrei un biglietto per Roma. — Andata e ritorno? — Dipende. Se torno domani, andata e ritorno. Se resto, solo andata.
