Lesson 1 of 6.
pruīna, pruīnae — hoarfrost, frost.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for stormy, then the Latin for turbulent.

You'll want to know: pruīna, pruīnae.
pruīna, pruīnae — "hoarfrost, frost".
Mane in prātīs pruīna alba est. In the morning there is white frost on the meadows.
First declension, feminine: pruīna, pruīnae f. In poetry, also winter.

The word, taken apart.
pruīna is inherited from PIE prews- 'to frost, to freeze' (EtymDB). English freeze and frost are Germanic cousins from the same root.

Lesson 2 of 6.
frīgus, frīgoris — the cold, cold weather.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for turbulent, then the Latin for hoarfrost.

You'll want to know: frīgus, frīgoris.
frīgus, frīgoris — "the cold, cold weather".
Hieme frīgus magnum est, et pauperēs ignem nōn habent. In winter the cold is great, and the poor have no fire.
Third declension, neuter: frīgus, frīgoris n. The adjective frīgidus 'cold' gives English frigid.

The word, taken apart.
frīgus is inherited from PIE sriHgos 'cold, coldness' (EtymDB), with Greek rhigos 'cold' as a cousin. English frigid comes from frīgidus, built on it (Lewis & Short).

Lesson 3 of 6.
gelō, gelāre — to freeze; (passive) to be frozen.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for hoarfrost, then the Latin for the cold.

You'll want to know: gelō, gelāre.
gelō, gelāre — "to freeze; (passive) to be frozen".
Nocte aqua in puteō gelātur. At night the water in the well freezes.
gelō, gelāre, gelāvī, gelātum. English congeal, jelly and gelatin come from this family.

The word, taken apart.
gelō is built on gelū 'frost, chill', from PIE gel- 'cold' (EtymDB; Lewis & Short). English congeal comes from congelāre, and jelly from gelāta 'frozen' through French.

Lesson 4 of 6.
rigeō, rigēre — to be stiff with cold, to be numb; to be stiff.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for the cold, then the Latin for to freeze.

You'll want to know: rigeō, rigēre.
rigeō, rigēre — "to be stiff with cold, to be numb; to be stiff".
Manūs viātōrum frīgore rigent. The travellers' hands are stiff with cold.
rigeō, rigēre, riguī, second conjugation, no supine. English rigid comes from rigidus.

The word, taken apart.
The origin of rigeō is not settled: Lewis & Short suggest a link with Greek rhigeō 'to shiver' and frīgeō, and EtymDB gives no parent. English rigid comes from rigidus, built on it.

Lesson 5 of 6.
algeō, algēre — to feel cold, to be cold.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to freeze, then the Latin for to be stiff with cold.

You'll want to know: algeō, algēre.
algeō, algēre — "to feel cold, to be cold".
Servus sine palliō alget. The slave without a cloak feels cold.
algeō, algēre, alsī, second conjugation. Of a person feeling cold; frīgus is the cold itself.

The word, taken apart.
The origin of algeō is not settled: Lewis & Short report an old link with Greek algeō 'to feel pain', and EtymDB gives no parent.
