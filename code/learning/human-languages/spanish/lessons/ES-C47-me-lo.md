# me lo — both at once

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-GRAMMAR-WHICH-OBJECT, ES-GRAMMAR-DIRECT-OBJECT-LO, ES-GRAMMAR-ME-DIRECT-OBJECT] -->

[PAUSE 2s] You have a pronoun for the **thing** and a pronoun for the
**person**. A sentence often needs both. What happens then?

## Grammar Lens: they stand together, in one order
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-GRAMMAR-DOUBLE-OBJECT-ORDER]; assesses=[ES-LEX-DECIR, ES-GRAMMAR-DECIR-PRESENT-SINGULAR, ES-GRAMMAR-DIRECT-OBJECT-LO, ES-GRAMMAR-ME-DIRECT-OBJECT, ES-GRAMMAR-TE-DIRECT-OBJECT] -->

They queue up in the same slot, both still in front of the verb:

> ***Me lo** dices.* — You tell **it** **to me**.
> ***Te lo** digo.* — I tell **it** **to you**.

Two small words, one after the other, and then the verb. Nothing has moved.

**The order never varies.** The person comes first, then the thing:

> **indirect, then direct.** *Me lo*, never *lo me*.

That is the entire rule, and it is worth noticing what it saves you. English
lets you choose — *give it to me* or *give me it* — and the choice carries a
faint difference in emphasis you have to feel your way into. Spanish took the
choice away. There is one order, it is always right, and you never have to
decide.

A fixed order is a smaller thing to learn than a free one.
