French, chapter 48: Writing --- Four Lines, And The Joiner That Moves Nothing.
1 lesson. The first lesson already needs your eyes or your hands, so save this one for when you have stopped.


Lesson 1 of 1.
Four lines, and the joiner that moves nothing — four lines to one reader, and the joiner that leaves the sentence exactly where it was.

Before we start: this one needs your hands, so it is not a driving lesson. You will want a pen and something to write on. You can listen to all of it now and come back to the parts that need looking at once you have stopped.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
The last lesson asked for writing finished on time. This one asks for writing that holds together.
[pause 1 second]
Pick one person who will read it.

You'll want to know.
[pause 2 seconds]
Connected writing is not longer writing. Four short lines can be connected; one long line can fail to be. What makes it connected is that a sentence stops standing on its own.
[pause 2 seconds]
You hold three joiners. et adds. mais sets against. parce que gives the reason.
[pause 2 seconds]
And here is the thing to notice, which is easiest to see by contrast. In German, the word for because drags the verb to the end of its clause: the join is visible in the word order. French does nothing of the kind.
[pause 2 seconds]
After parce que, the words stand in exactly the order they would have stood in on their own — subject, verb, the rest. Nothing moves.
[pause 2 seconds]
That makes French easier to write and harder to hear. Nothing in the shape of the second clause tells you a reason has started; only the two little words do. Which is why parce que has to be written in full, and why it is two words rather than one.

Writing — connected composition.
[pause 2 seconds]
Write four lines to the person you picked.
At least one must use et or mais. At least one must use parce que — both words, spelled out.
[pause 3 seconds]
Use only words you already write. This lesson adds no vocabulary; the whole exercise is the joining.

Wrap-up recall.
[pause 2 seconds]
Cover your first line and read the second alone. Does it still make sense without the first? If it does, you wrote a list — which is where you were one lesson ago.
[pause 2 seconds]
Then find your parce que line and read the words after it. They should be in ordinary order. If you moved something, you were remembering a different language's rule.
