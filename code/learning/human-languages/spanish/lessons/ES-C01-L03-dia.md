# día — your first noun, and how Spanish tags it

## Warm-up

[PAUSE 2s] *hola* and *bien* are behind you. Now your first **noun** — *día*,
"day." Naming words like this carry two pieces of information in Spanish, and
both come straight from Latin: a **gender** and a **number**. *día* is where
you learn both — you'll use them on every noun from here on.

## You'll want to know first

- [ES-C01-L02 (bien / bueno)](./ES-C01-L02-bien.md) — you'll pair *día* with
  *bueno* next lesson, and the gender you learn here is *why* it takes the
  shape it does.

## Sounds you'll need

- `accent-i` — the accent sits on the **í**: *día* is *DEE-a*, two beats. →
  [reference](../pronunciation-reference.md)
- `d-soft` — the **d** between vowels is soft, toward the *th* in *this*.

## The word, taken apart

**el día** — "the day." Root: Latin **dies** ("day"). The family fans across
English, mostly through French:

- **di**ary, **di**urnal ("of the day").
- **jour**nal, **jour**ney — French *jour*, "a day" (a *journey* was once "a
  day's travel").
- quoti**di**an ("every-day"), meri**di**an ("mid-day"), and *dismal*
  (*dies mali*, "evil days").

## Grammar Lens: a Spanish noun carries two tags — gender and number

A **noun** is a naming word — a person, place, thing, or idea (*day, house,
friend*). Every Spanish noun comes stamped with two things:

**1. Gender — masculine or feminine.** This is a grammatical *class*, not a
statement about biological sex: a "day" isn't male; the label is just how the
language sorts its nouns. Where does it come from? **Latin.** Latin sorted
every noun into *three* genders — masculine, feminine, and neuter. Spanish
simplified to **two**, masculine and feminine, folding most old neuter nouns
into the masculine pile. A Spanish noun's gender is usually inherited
directly from its Latin gender — not random, but history.

You *see* a noun's gender in the word for "the," which changes to match it:
**el** (masculine), **la** (feminine). *el día*, but *la casa* ("the house,"
feminine). From now on every noun in this book arrives with its *el*/*la*
attached, because you can't reliably guess a noun's gender — you have to
learn it *with* the word. (Even *el* and *la* are Latin: from *ille* / *illa*,
"that one.")

*día* is **masculine** — *el día* — even though it ends in *-a*, which usually
signals feminine. Why the exception? Because Latin *dies* was masculine, and
*día* inherited that gender whole, spelling be damned. The Latin origin
*is* the explanation.

**2. Number — singular or plural.** Same as English, one rule for now: a noun
ending in a vowel adds **-s**. *día → días* ("days"). (Consonant endings take
*-es* — later.)
