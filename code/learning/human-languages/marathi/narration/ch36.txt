Marathi, chapter 36: When, After, and Too.
4 lessons. All 4 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 4.
जेव्हा … तेव्हा … (jevhā … tevhā …) — when, then.

Warm-up.
[pause 3 seconds]
Retrieve the two items before this one by name: की, which hangs a whole sentence on a thinking verb, and -ण्यासाठी, which turns a verb into an aim. Both put a clause underneath. This one does it in time.

Grammar Lens: the two-word frame.
जेव्हा मी वाचतो तेव्हा मला समजतं. — jevhā mī vācto tevhā malā samajtaṁ. — When I read, then I understand.
Marathi does not leave when on its own. It opens with जेव्हा and answers with तेव्हा in the second half, and the second word is not optional padding — it is what tells you the first clause has finished.
You have met this machinery before without being told its name. जेव्हा is built on the relative stem ज-, तेव्हा on the demonstrative त-, and the same two stems sit under जो/तो. English keeps the pair in when … then and drops the then; Latin kept both in cum … tum. Marathi keeps both, always.
Because the frame is spoken in the order the events happen, it is also the easiest long sentence in the book to say: first clause, तेव्हा, second clause.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: jevhā mī vācto tevhā malā samajtaṁ]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: the pair on its own — jevhā … tevhā]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: which of the two stems is the relative one — ज-]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[question — say your answer, then pause 25 seconds]
Say: when I read, then I understand.

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
What must follow a जेव्हा clause? (तेव्हा.) What does that second word do for the listener? (Tells them the first clause has ended.)

Lesson 2 of 4.
-ल्यावर (-lyāvar) — after doing.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say the two-clause frame you got last lesson: जेव्हा मी वाचतो तेव्हा मला समजतं. Nine syllables of scaffolding for two events. Marathi has a shorter way.

Grammar Lens: one ending instead of two clauses.
वाचल्यावर मला समजतं. — vācalyāvar malā samajtaṁ. — After reading, I understand.
The swap is the same shape as -ण्यासाठी: take -णे off the verb and put -ल्यावर (-lyāvar) on. The result is one word that means after doing it, and it stands at the front of the sentence with no second half required.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
dictionary form: वाचणे. after-doing form: वाचल्यावर.
dictionary form: शिकणे. after-doing form: शिकल्यावर.
Taken apart, -ल्यावर (-lyāvar) is a past participle in -ल- with वर ("on, upon") behind it. After reading is literally upon having-read. English reaches for the same preposition in upon reading, which makes this one of the few Marathi endings you can feel your way into rather than memorise.
जेव्हा says the two things happen at once; -ल्यावर (-lyāvar) says one finished before the other began. Choose by which you mean, not by which is shorter.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: vācṇe becomes vācalyāvar, then shikṇe becomes shikalyāvar]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: vācalyāvar malā samajtaṁ]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: which of the two says at the same time — jevhā]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[question — say your answer, then pause 30 seconds]
Say the same idea twice: as a jevhā frame, then packed into -lyāvar.

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
What does -ल्यावर (-lyāvar) replace? (A whole जेव्हा … तेव्हा frame.) What is the वर in it doing? (It means upon — upon having done it.)


Lesson 3 of 4.
सुद्धा (sudhā) — too, and "not even".

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
आणि joins two things in one sentence. But often the first thing was said a minute ago, by somebody else. There is no gap to put आणि in.

You'll want to know: सुद्धा.
मला दूध सुद्धा आवडतं. — malā dūdh sudhā āvaḍtaṁ. — I like milk too.
सुद्धा (sudhā) goes straight after the word it adds — not between two things, but behind one. That is what lets it reach back to something already said. It is the joining word for a CONVERSATION rather than for a sentence.

Grammar Lens: the same word in front of a denial.
मला दूध सुद्धा आवडत नाही. — malā dūdh sudhā āvaḍat nāhī. — I don't even like milk.
Nothing about सुद्धा (sudhā) changed. The sentence around it turned negative, and too became not even on its own. English does the identical thing: I like it too against I don't even like it. One word, two readings, and the negation picks which.
सुद्धा (sudhā) is Sanskrit शुद्ध (śuddha), "pure." Purely that hardened into even that, which is the ordinary road a purity word travels.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: malā chahā āvaḍto — then malā dūdh sudhā āvaḍtaṁ]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: malā dūdh sudhā āvaḍat nāhī]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: where सुद्धा (sudhā) stands — right after the word it adds]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[question — say your answer, then pause 30 seconds]
Say that you like milk too, then that you do not even like milk.

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Where does सुद्धा (sudhā) stand? (Directly after the word it adds.) What turns too into not even? (Negating the verb; सुद्धा (sudhā) itself does not change.)
