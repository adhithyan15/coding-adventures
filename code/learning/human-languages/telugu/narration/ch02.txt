Lesson 1 of 15.
పేరు (pēru) — "name," the native Dravidian word.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, for letter shapes on the page. You can listen to everything else now — leave the section called The letters in this word until you have stopped, and I will say so again when we reach it.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
"Name" — native Dravidian, even in Sanskrit-loving Telugu.

The letters in this word.
(Skim if you read Telugu.) ప (pa) + ే (the ē-sign) becomes పే (pē); రు (ru). Read పే, రు becomes pēru.

The word, taken apart.
పేరు (pēru, "name") is native Dravidian, from Proto-Dravidian pēr — the same word as Tamil peyar and Malayalam pēr, and a cousin of Kannada hesaru (which softened p becomes h). It is not Sanskrit nāma (Hindi nām, English name). Even Telugu — the most Sanskritised of the Dravidian tongues — kept its home-grown word for "name."

Across the family — the same idea, six ways.
[a table of 6 rows, read aloud]
Language: Telugu. "Name": పేరు pēru. Source: native Dravidian (pēr).
Language: Tamil. "Name": peyar. Source: the same Dravidian root.
Language: Malayalam. "Name": pēr. Source: the same Dravidian root.
Language: Kannada. "Name": hesaru. Source: Dravidian (pesar, p becomes h).
Language: Hindi. "Name": nām. Source: Sanskrit (Indo-European).
Language: English. "Name": name. Source: Indo-European.
Four Dravidian tongues, one inherited word — even Kannada's hesaru, once you know the old p went soft. Only the last two rows come from somewhere else.

Sounds you'll need.
ప (pa) + ే (the ē-sign) becomes పే (pē); రు (ru). Read పే, రు becomes pēru.

Lesson 5 of 15.
నా పేరు … (nā pēru …) — "my name is…," with no "is".

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Assemble the two atoms — and note, as in Tamil and Kannada, no word for "is."

The phrase, assembled.
nā (my) + pēru (name) + your name.
నా పేరు … (nā pēru …) = "my name …" becomes "my name is…"
Nā pēru Arun. becomes "My name is Arun."

Grammar Lens: the zero copula.
There is no verb. Telugu sets "my name" beside "Arun" and lets the "is" be understood — the zero copula of the Dravidian family. Nā pēru Arun = literally "my name Arun."

The dialogue.
[a table of 4 rows, read aloud]
నా పేరు మీరా. (nā pēru Mira) means My name is Mira.
మీ పేరు ఏమిటి? (mī pēru ēmiṭi?) (mī pēru ēmiṭi) means What's your name?
నా పేరు అరుణ్. (nā pēru Arun) means My name is Arun.
సంతోషం. (santōṣam) means Pleased to meet you.
Every atom traced:
pēru from Dravidian pēr (not name; twin of Tamil peyar).
nā from nēnu ("I").
ēmiṭi from Dravidian question-stem yā-/e-.
santōṣam from Sanskrit (Telugu borrows for "pleased"; Tamil keeps native magiḻcci).
The Telugu habit: the zero copula (no "is").
