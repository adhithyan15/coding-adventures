# ماں! (māṅ!) — calling somebody

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[UR-LEX-MAAN, UR-LEX-SALAM, UR-FUNC-HAI-NA] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say **mother**. Then say the greeting from chapter one, and ask
yourself who you said it *to*.

## You'll want to know: the kin term as an address
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[UR-FUNC-VOCATIVE]; assesses=[UR-LEX-MAAN, UR-LEX-SALAM] -->

> **ماں! سلام** — *māṅ! salām* — **Mother! Hello.**

This book has taught **ماں** since chapter seventeen as a noun — a person you can
*talk about*. Not once has anybody been **called**.

That matters more in Urdu than in English. An Urdu speaker addresses almost
nobody by name. A mother is **ماں**, an older brother is *bhāī*, a shopkeeper is
*bhāī*, a stranger of your parents' age is *uncle* or *auntie*. Using a first
name where a kin term is expected is the mistake, not the other way round.

## Grammar Lens: what the vocative does to the noun
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[UR-FUNC-VOCATIVE, UR-GRAMMAR-OBLIQUE, UR-GRAMMAR-OBLIQUE-PLURAL, UR-LEX-KAMRA] -->

Urdu kept a fragment of the old vocative case, and it is one letter's worth:

| the noun | talking about | calling |
|---|---|---|
| ends in *-ā* | **کمرہ** | **کمرے** |
| plural | **کمروں** (before a postposition) | **کمرو** |

The call form of a plural is **-و**, not the **-وں** you learned in chapter
twenty-five. The nasal drops. That is the whole difference between shouting to a
group and saying something about them.

**ماں** does not end in **-ا**, so it does not change — which is why calling
your mother is the easiest vocative in the language.
