# fallar — to fail somebody

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C324-TRUE-02, ES-LEX-C448-MUDA-04] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say **falso** and **estropeado**. One is about truth, one about
machines. The verb next covers both.

## You'll want to know: fallar
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-C464-CITA-03]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C448-MUDA-04] -->

**fallar** — to fail, to go wrong, to let somebody down.

*Me falló el coche* — the car let me down. *Ha fallado el sistema* — the system
has gone down. *No me falles* — don't let me down.

**El fallo** is the noun: a fault, a glitch, and in an exam a wrong answer.

## Grammar Lens: fallar is not estropearse
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C464-CITA-03, ES-LEX-C448-MUDA-04] -->

Both are about things going wrong, and they are not the same:

| what happened | the verb |
|---|---|
| it stopped working, and stayed that way | **estropearse** |
| it did not do its job at the moment needed | **fallar** |

*Se ha estropeado el coche* — it is broken, in the garage. *Me falló el coche*
— it let me down, this morning, when I needed it. A car can *fallar* once and
be fine afterwards.

That is why *fallar* is the verb for people too. *Me falló* about a friend is a
reproach: they were not there when it counted.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C464-CITA-03, ES-LEX-C324-TRUE-02] -->

Latin **fallere**, to deceive — and that is the same verb inside **falso**,
which you already have.

The move from deceiving to failing runs through disappointment: something that
*fails* you has, in effect, misled you about what it would do. Latin *fallere*
already carried both, and Spanish kept the pair — *falso* for the lie, *fallar*
for the letting-down.

English took it twice as well: **false** from the participle, and **fail**
through French. **Fault**, **fallacy** and **failure** are all here, and so is
**default**, which is a failing to act.

A second Spanish branch is **faltar**, to be lacking — *me falta tiempo*, I'm
short of time — from the same root by a different road.
