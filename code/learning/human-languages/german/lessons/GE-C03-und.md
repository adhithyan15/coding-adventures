# und — "and," and how to hand the question back

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GE-LEX-DIR-01, GE-LEX-IHNEN-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Someone asks how you are; you answer. A conversation only starts when
you ask back — and German returns the question in two words.

## You'll want to know: und
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[GE-LEX-UND-01]; assesses=[] -->

> **und** — "and."

It is English **and** with the vowel darkened, and it has barely changed in a
thousand years — one of the oldest words either language still uses daily.

## Sounds you'll need
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[GE-SOUND-FINAL-DEVOICING-D-01]; assesses=[GE-LEX-UND-01] -->

- `d-t-final` — say *oont*, not *oond*. German hardens a **d** at the end of a
  word into a **t**. It is the same rule that makes *Tag* come out as *tock*
  rather than *tahg*: whatever the spelling, a final consonant goes voiceless.
  So *und* rhymes with English *hunt*, not with *shunned*.

## Grammar Lens: returning the question
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[GE-LEX-UND-DIR-02]; assesses=[GE-LEX-UND-01, GE-LEX-DIR-01, GE-LEX-IHNEN-01, GE-GRAMMAR-DATIVE-01] -->

Hand the question back by naming the other person — in the **to-whom** form,
because it is short for "and how goes it *to you*?"

| To a friend | To a stranger |
|---|---|
| *und dir?* | *und Ihnen?* |

Not *und du* — the dative is doing real work here, and using the doer-form is
the giveaway of a beginner. The same shape appears across the series: Spanish
*¿y tú?*, French *et toi?*

<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GE-LEX-UND-01, GE-LEX-UND-DIR-02, GE-SOUND-FINAL-DEVOICING-D-01] -->
