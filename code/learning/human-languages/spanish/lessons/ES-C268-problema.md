# problema — the word for when it goes wrong (and the -a that is masculine)

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Travel goes wrong. This is the word that starts the conversation about
it — and it hides a grammatical surprise you should meet on purpose rather than
by accident.

## You'll want to know first
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[] -->

- [habitación](./ES-C268-habitacion.md) — and the gender rule it followed.

## Sounds you'll need
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[] -->

- Four syllables, stress in the middle: *pro-BLE-ma*. No written accent, because
  that is where a word ending in a vowel is stressed by default. →
  [reference](../pronunciation-reference.md)

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-PROBLEMA]; assesses=[] -->

**problema** — "problem." A near-twin of the English word, and you will be
understood the moment you say it.

**Where it comes from.** Greek *próblēma*, "a thing thrown forward" — *pro-*
("forward") plus *bállein* ("to throw"). A problem is something pitched into your
path. That same *bállein* is inside English **ballistic** and **symbol**.

## What you've built
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-GRAMMAR-MASCULINE-MA-NOUNS]; assesses=[] -->

Here is the surprise, and it is worth more than the word itself. You learned that
nouns ending in *-a* are usually feminine. *Problema* is **masculine**: *el
problema*, *un problema*. Never *la problema*.

It is not an exception you have to memorise blindly — it is a whole family with a
shared history. Greek nouns ending in *-ma* were neuter, and when Latin borrowed
them the neuter words landed on the masculine side. Every one of them came into
Spanish the same way: *el problema*, *el sistema*, *el tema*, *el programa*, *el
idioma*, *el clima*. If a noun ends in *-ma* and sounds Greek and academic, guess
*el* and you will usually be right.
