# വന്നിട്ടുണ്ട് — he has come

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ML-GRAMMAR-C111-ITTU-01, ML-CONCEPT-C32-UNDU-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say *having come*.

[PAUSE 2s] Then say the word that means *there is* — the one that also does
*somebody has*.

## You'll want to know
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ML-GRAMMAR-C111-PERFECT-01]; assesses=[ML-GRAMMAR-C111-ITTU-01, ML-LEX-C71-AVAN-AVAL-01] -->

> **അവൻ വന്നിട്ടുണ്ട്.**
>
> *avan vanniṭṭuṇṭŭ* — "**He has come.**"

**Put the two together and nothing is left over.**

| | |
|---|---|
| **വന്നിട്ട്** | having come |
| **ഉണ്ട്** | there is |

They run together in speech into **വന്നിട്ടുണ്ട്**, and that is the whole of the
construction: the waiting form, and the word it was waiting for.

## Grammar Lens: the word you already knew did the work
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ML-GRAMMAR-C111-PERFECT-01, ML-CONCEPT-C32-UNDU-01, ML-CONCEPT-C32-VARUKA-02] -->

**ഉണ്ട്** was given to you as *there is* and *somebody has*. That second job is
the one showing here. English builds its perfect on **have** — *he has come* —
and Malayalam reaches for the word it already uses for having.

So the two languages land in the same place from the same starting point, which
is worth noticing precisely because so little else about the two verbs lines up.

**What it says that a plain past does not** is that the coming is done and bears
on now. *avan vannu* reports the event. *avan vanniṭṭuṇṭŭ* tells you he is
here — the arriving happened, and you are being told about its result.
