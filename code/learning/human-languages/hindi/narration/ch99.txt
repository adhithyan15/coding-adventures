Hindi, chapter 99: The Noun, And The Ending That Means Two Things.
9 lessons. You can do the first 3 of them in the car; after that you will want to have stopped.


Lesson 1 of 9.
बच्चे (bacce) — more than one, and an ending you have seen four times.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
You can say बच्चा for one child. Try to say children, and notice the track has taught you a great many words and never how to have two of anything.

You'll want to know: बच्चे.
बच्चे (bacce) — children.
एक बच्चा — one child. दो बच्चे (bacce) — two children.

The word, taken apart.
The ending moved from -आ to -ए, and you have watched that happen four times already:
[a table of 4 rows, read aloud]
where: adjectives. singular: काला. plural or masculine-plural: काले.
where: the habitual. singular: बोलता. plural or masculine-plural: बोलते.
where: the past copula. singular: था. plural or masculine-plural: थे.
where: a noun. singular: बच्चा. plural or masculine-plural: बच्चे (bacce).
Nouns bend the same way adjectives do, which is not a coincidence: to Hindi they are the same kind of word wearing the same endings. Learning noun plurals is not a new system — it is the one system arriving at a new place.
This applies to masculine nouns ending in -आ and to those only. A noun shaped otherwise does something else, and the next lessons take those in turn.

Grammar Lens: what changes with it.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
बच्चा लंबा है। means The child is tall.
बच्चे (bacce) लंबे हैं। means The children are tall.
Three words moved and the reason is one. The noun went plural, so the adjective followed it and the copula followed the adjective. Agreement travels outward from the noun, and once the noun is right the rest is automatic.

[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: which other words in the sentence had to move, and why]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
What does a masculine -आ noun do in the plural? (Ends in -ए.) Which other words follow it? (The adjective and the copula.) Is this a new system? (No — it is the adjective ending arriving on a noun.)


Lesson 2 of 9.
किताबें (kitābeṁ) — nothing to change, so something is added.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
बच्चा ends in -आ and swapped it for -ए. किताब ends in a consonant. Ask yourself what there is to swap.

You'll want to know: किताबें.
किताबें (kitābeṁ) — books.
एक किताब — one book. दो किताबें (kitābeṁ) — two books.

The word, taken apart.
A word with no vowel at the end has nothing to exchange, so Hindi adds a syllable: -एँ, the same -ए as before with a nasal on it.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
shape of the noun: ends in -आ. how it makes a plural: swaps the ending for -ए.
shape of the noun: ends in a consonant. how it makes a plural: adds -एँ.
That is the whole difference, and it follows from the shape rather than from a list to memorise: change what is there, or add where nothing is.
Feminine nouns ending in a consonant, like किताब, are much the commonest case, and the nasal is what your ear should catch.

Grammar Lens: the nasal you already write.
The mark over the ending is the anusvāra, which you have been writing since हैं and मिलेंगे. It is doing the same job here that it does there: this is a plural, and the nasal is the sign of it.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
singular: किताब. plural: किताबें (kitābeṁ).
singular: रात. plural: रातें.
Say both aloud and let the nasal fall on the last syllable rather than swallowing it — that is what a listening paper is asking you to hear.

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
What does a consonant-final noun add? (-एँ.) Why does it add instead of swapping? (There is no final vowel to swap.) Which mark carries the plural? (The nasal you already write in हैं.)


Lesson 3 of 9.
भाषाएँ (bhāṣāẽ) — an -आ ending that does not behave like the others.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
भाषा ends in -आ, exactly like बच्चा. Guess its plural from that, and then read on to find out why the guess is wrong.

You'll want to know: भाषाएँ.
भाषाएँ (bhāṣāẽ) — languages.
दो भाषाएँ (bhāṣāẽ) — two languages. That phrase is printed in a practice paper, in a personal account: she speaks two languages.

The word, taken apart.
भाषा is feminine, and that is the whole story. The -आ becomes -ए swap belongs to masculine nouns; a feminine noun adds instead, exactly as किताब did.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
noun: बच्चा. gender: masculine. plural: बच्चे (bacce).
noun: किताब. gender: feminine. plural: किताबें (kitābeṁ).
noun: भाषा. gender: feminine. plural: भाषाएँ (bhāṣāẽ).
So the question to ask a noun is not "how does it end" on its own. It is gender first, ending second — and the two feminine rows do the same thing despite ending differently.
The ए is written as a full letter here rather than as a mark, because भाषा ends in a vowel and there is nothing for a mark to sit on. That is the same rule that gave you आऊँगा and आओगे.

Grammar Lens: the three shapes, in the order to ask them.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
ask this: is it masculine? then this: -आ swaps to -ए.
ask this: is it feminine? then this: it adds a nasal ending.
Two questions and you have the plural of any A1 noun. The number in front — दो, तीन — does not change either way; Hindi marks the plural on the noun, not on the numeral.

[your turn — say: the plurals of बच्चा, किताब and भाषा in a row]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: which question you ask a noun first]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: why the ए is a full letter in this word]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Why does भाषा not become भाषे? (It is feminine; the swap is for masculine nouns.) What do both feminine nouns do? (Add a nasal ending.) Which question comes first? (Gender.)


Lesson 4 of 9.
दो आदमी (do ādmī) — the plural you cannot see.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, because the lesson points at something written down. You can listen to all of it now and come back to the parts that need looking at once you have stopped.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
आदमी is masculine and ends in -ई, not -आ. You have a rule for -आ and a rule for feminine nouns. Notice this word fits neither.

You'll want to know: दो आदमी.
एक आदमी — one man. दो आदमी (do ādmī) — two men.
The word does not change.

The word, taken apart.
A masculine noun that does not end in -आ has no plural form at all. It stands still, and the sentence around it does the work:
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
what tells you: the numeral. how: दो, तीन.
what tells you: the verb. how: हैं rather than है.
what tells you: an adjective. how: लंबे rather than लंबा.
दो आदमी (do ādmī) लंबे हैं। — Two men are tall. The noun is the only word in that sentence with nothing to show for it.
This is worth expecting rather than being surprised by, because a reading paper will give you an unchanged noun and ask you to count. The number lives in the sentence, not always in the word.

Grammar Lens: the whole plural system, now complete.
[a table of 4 rows, read aloud]
noun: बच्चा (m, -आ). plural: बच्चे (bacce).
noun: आदमी (m, other). plural: आदमी.
noun: किताब (f). plural: किताबें (kitābeṁ).
noun: भाषा (f). plural: भाषाएँ (bhāṣāẽ).
Four rows and every A1 noun you will meet is in one of them. Two of the four add a nasal, one swaps a vowel, and one does nothing.

[your turn — say: "दो आदमी (do ādmī) लंबे हैं"]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: the three things in that sentence that tell you it is plural]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: all four rows of the table]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
What does a masculine noun ending in -ई do in the plural? (Nothing.) So what tells you there is more than one? (The numeral, the verb and any adjective.) How many shapes does the whole system have? (Four.)


Lesson 5 of 9.
को (ko) — the word that was inside आपको.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say आपको. Say it slowly, in two pieces, and you have already said this lesson's word.

You'll want to know: को.
को (ko) — "to," and the marker a definite or human object wears.
आदमी को (ko) — to the man, and also the man when he is the thing acted on.

The word, taken apart.
You met it fused. आपको is आप + को (ko), and it was named as such when the oblique pronouns arrived. Here it stands on its own, in front of a noun instead of a pronoun, and it is the third postposition the track now has.
It does two jobs that English keeps apart:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
job: a recipient. example: आदमी को (ko) — to the man.
job: a definite or human object. example: आदमी को (ko) — the man, as the thing acted on.
The second is the strange one to an English ear. English marks a direct object by word order and nothing else. Hindi puts को (ko) on an object when it is a person or a particular thing, and leaves it bare otherwise. Spanish does the same with a before a human object, so the demand is not unique to Hindi — only the marker is.

Grammar Lens: three postpositions, one habit.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
postposition: पर. what it marks: at a point.
postposition: से. what it marks: from it, or by way of it.
postposition: को (ko). what it marks: to it, or it as a definite object.
All three trail the noun. All three are one syllable. And all three do something to the noun in front of them, which is what the next lesson is about.

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
What is आपको made of? (आप plus को (ko).) When does a direct object take को (ko)? (When it is a person, or a particular thing.) Which European language marks a human object the same way? (Spanish, with a.)


Lesson 6 of 9.
बच्चे को (bacce ko) — the same ending, and this time it is not a plural.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
You know बच्चे as children. Read बच्चे को (bacce ko) and notice it cannot mean to the children and one child at the same time — so which is it?

You'll want to know: बच्चे को.
बच्चे को (bacce ko) — to the child. One child.
A noun standing in front of a postposition changes shape, and for a masculine -आ noun that shape is -ए — the same ending the plural uses.

The word, taken apart.
This is not a new rule. It is the one the possessives taught you, arriving on a noun:
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
word: मेरा. plain: मेरा. in front of a postposition: मेरे पास.
word: उसका. plain: उसका. in front of a postposition: उसके बाद.
word: बच्चा. plain: बच्चा. in front of a postposition: बच्चे को (bacce ko).
Hindi calls this shape the oblique. Every -आ word in the language wears it — possessives, adjectives, and now nouns — and it is always the same -ए.
So the track has taught this bend four times on four kinds of word, and this is the fourth and last kind.

Grammar Lens: how to tell the two -ए apart.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
what you see: बच्चे (bacce) हैं. what it is: plural — nothing follows the noun.
what you see: बच्चे को (bacce ko). what it is: singular oblique — a postposition follows.
The postposition is the whole signal. If one is standing behind the noun, the -ए is oblique and the noun may well be singular. If nothing is, the -ए is a plural.
A plural in front of a postposition takes yet another shape, which is one step past A1 — recognise बच्चों को (ko) on a page and leave it there for now.

Wrap-up Recall.

How many children is बच्चे को (bacce ko)? (One.) What forces the noun into that shape? (The postposition behind it.) Which earlier words wore the same -ए? (The possessives, in front of a postposition.)


Lesson 7 of 9.
यह किताब (yah kitāb) — the same word, one step to the left.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
You have यह and वह as answers to which one — standing alone, meaning this one and that one. Try putting one in front of a noun.

You'll want to know: यह किताब.
यह किताब (yah kitāb) — this book. वह आदमी — that man.
Nothing is added and nothing bends. The word you already have is placed in front of a noun, and that is the whole of it.

The word, taken apart.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
यह means this one.
यह किताब (yah kitāb) means this book.
English does exactly the same thing with the same two words — this is a pronoun in this is mine and a determiner in this book — so there is nothing here to translate differently. The reason it needs saying is that the track taught these words only as standalone answers, and a reading paper uses them in front of nouns far more often.
There is a second reason it matters. Hindi has no article, so यह and वह are what a writer reaches for when the would have been enough in English. A sentence that begins वह आदमी is often best read as the man rather than that man.

Grammar Lens: what fills the space before a noun.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
in front of the noun: nothing. what it does: a book, or the book.
in front of the noun: एक. what it does: one book, a book.
in front of the noun: यह / वह. what it does: this book, that book.
Three choices and the reader picks from context. That is the whole of Hindi definiteness at this level, and it is why the track has spent no time on articles: there are none to spend it on.

Wrap-up Recall.

What changes when यह moves in front of a noun? (Nothing — it is placed there.) Why does Hindi lean on these two words more than English does? (It has no article.) How is वह आदमी often best read? (The man.)


Lesson 8 of 9.
saṁjñā kī tālikā — the noun, on one page.

Warm-up.
[pause 3 seconds]
Name the first question you ask a noun before making it plural, and say why it comes first.

Grammar Lens: the four plurals.
[a table of 4 rows, read aloud]
noun: बच्चा (m, -आ). plural: बच्चे (bacce).
noun: आदमी (m, other). plural: आदमी.
noun: किताब (f, consonant). plural: किताबें (kitābeṁ).
noun: भाषा (f, -आ). plural: भाषाएँ (bhāṣāẽ).
Gender first, ending second. One row swaps a vowel, two add a nasal, and one does nothing at all.

Grammar Lens: the ending that means two things.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
what you see: बच्चे (bacce) लंबे हैं. how many: more than one. why: nothing follows the noun.
what you see: बच्चे को (bacce ko). how many: one. why: a postposition follows.
The postposition is the signal. That is the single most useful thing in this chapter, because -ए is everywhere and a reader who cannot tell the two apart will miscount every sentence that has one.

Grammar Lens: the space in front of the noun.
[a table of 1 row, read aloud]
before the noun: nothing, एक, यह, वह. after the noun: पर, से, को (ko).
A Hindi noun phrase is a noun with an optional word in front and an optional word behind. Everything this chapter taught fits in that one picture.

Lesson 9 of 9.
दो भाषाएँ बोलती है (do bhāṣāẽ boltī hai) — counting from one word.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Read this and say how many languages, before reading on.
दो भाषाएँ बोलती है (do bhāṣāẽ boltī hai)।.

Grammar Lens: the line a paper prints.
That clause is lifted from a personal account in the second practice paper. Until this chapter, भाषाएँ (bhāṣāẽ) was an unreadable shape.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
word: दो. what it does: the number.
word: भाषाएँ (bhāṣāẽ). what it does: the noun, carrying the plural nasal.
word: बोलती है. what it does: the habit, and the speaker is a woman.
Two things say plural here and neither is the verb: the numeral and the ending on the noun. The verb agrees with the person, not with the count.

The exchange.
The other half of the chapter, in two lines that look almost alike:
— बच्चे कहाँ हैं? — बच्चे को (bacce ko) घर भेजा।.
The first बच्चे (bacce) is a plural and the second is one child in the oblique. Nothing but the postposition separates them, and a reader who misses it counts wrong.

How to answer.
Say each of these out loud, as a plural:
one child, then two children.
one book, then two books.
one language, then two languages.
one man, then two men.
[pause 5 seconds]
The fourth is the one that sounds wrong and is right. Say it twice.

Wrap-up Recall.

Does the verb tell you how many? (No — it agrees with the person.) What separates a plural बच्चे (bacce) from an oblique one? (A postposition behind it.) Which noun shape shows no plural at all? (A masculine noun not ending in -आ.)
