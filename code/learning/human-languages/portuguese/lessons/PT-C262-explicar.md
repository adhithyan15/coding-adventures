# explicar — to unfold

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[PT-GRAMMAR-ACHO-QUE-01, PT-GRAMMAR-DUAS-SETAS-01, PT-LEX-DEVIA-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Four pieces are in your hands: *acho que* for an opinion,
*porque* and *por isso* for the two arrows, *devia* for advice. This lesson
puts them in a row, under the verb that names the act.

## Grammar Lens: the shape of an explanation
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[PT-GRAMMAR-EXPLICACAO-01]; assesses=[PT-LEX-C231-VERBOS231-04, PT-LEX-PORQUE-01, PT-LEX-FAZIA-FRIO-01, PT-LEX-C187-QUAL187-04] -->

**explicar**, "to explain", is a regular *-ar* verb with one spelling catch.
Its event past for "I" is **expliquei**: the *c* becomes *qu* before an *e*,
so that it keeps its hard sound. The rest is plain: *explico*, *explicas*,
*explica*, *explicou*.

Here is a whole explanation, every word of it taught:

> — Porque é que não sais?
> — **Acho que** vai chover. E estou constipado, **porque** ontem fazia frio.
> **Por isso** não saio. **Devia** descansar.
>
> *— Why aren't you going out?*
> *— I think it's going to rain. And I've got a cold, because it was cold
> yesterday. So I'm not going out. I ought to rest.*

Four moves, and a word for each: an **opinion** (*acho que*), a **cause**
pointing back (*porque*), a **consequence** pointing forward (*por isso*), and
**advice** (*devia*). With both arrows, you can build it cause first or claim
first.

## The word, taken apart: folded, and unfolded
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[PT-ETYMON-EXPLICAR-02]; assesses=[PT-LEX-C81-VERBOS7-05] -->

**explicar** is a learned borrowing from Latin *explicāre*, "to unfold, to
spread out" (EtymDB 2.1; Lewis & Short, under *explico*), which is *ex-*,
"out", plus *plicāre*, "to fold". **To explain is to unfold**: something was
folded shut, and you open it out.

The same *plicāre* also came down into Portuguese the slow way, by mouth, and
wore into **chegar**, "to arrive" (EtymDB 2.1): Latin *pl* became Portuguese
*ch*. The usual story of how folding became arriving is the sailor's, folding
the sails at the end of a voyage; neither source tells it, so it is offered
as the usual story only. English kept the Latin in **explicit**, and
**complicated** is the opposite: folded together.
