Latin, chapter 62: Writing --- Four Lines, And The Word That Has A Twin.
1 lesson. The first lesson already needs your eyes or your hands, so save this one for when you have stopped.


Lesson 1 of 1.
Four lines, and the word that has a twin — four lines to one reader, and the two words Latin has for because.

Before we start: this one needs your hands, so it is not a driving lesson. You will want a pen and something to write on. You can listen to all of it now and come back to the parts that need looking at once you have stopped.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
The last lesson asked for writing finished on time. This one asks for writing that holds together.
[pause 1 second]
Pick one person who will read it.

You'll want to know.
[pause 2 seconds]
Connected writing is not longer writing. Four short lines can be connected; one long line can fail to be. What makes it connected is that a sentence stops standing on its own.
[pause 2 seconds]
You hold four joiners. et adds. sed sets against. aut offers. quia gives the reason.
[pause 2 seconds]
Use quia for your reason, and use it deliberately, because Latin has a second word that also means because — quod — and that one is doing two jobs at once. quod is also the ordinary word for which.
[pause 2 seconds]
So a sentence built on quod hands the reader a small decision the writer may not have noticed making: is this a reason, or is it a relative clause? quia hands them nothing to decide. At this level, write the word that can only mean one thing.
[pause 2 seconds]
That is a real habit and not a beginner's crutch. Choosing the unambiguous word where one exists is most of what clear writing is, in any language.

Writing — connected composition.
[pause 2 seconds]
Write four lines to the person you picked.
At least one must use et, sed or aut. At least one must use quia.
[pause 3 seconds]
Use only words and forms the book has shown you. This lesson adds no vocabulary; the whole exercise is the joining.

Wrap-up recall.
[pause 2 seconds]
Cover your first line and read the second alone. Does it still make sense without the first? If it does, you wrote a list — which is where you were one lesson ago.
[pause 2 seconds]
Which of Latin's two words for because can also mean which? (quod.) And which one did you write? (quia — the one that cannot be mistaken.)
