Latin, chapter 432: To ride, Saddle cloth, Spur, To jump down, To kick.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
equitō, equitāre — to ride (on horseback).

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to bridle, then the Latin for a whip.

You'll want to know: equitō, equitāre.
equitō, equitāre — "to ride (on horseback)".
Mīles per campum equitat. The soldier rides across the plain.
equitō, equitāre, equitāvī, equitātum. English equitation comes from it.

The word, taken apart.
equitō is built on eques 'horseman' (EtymDB; Lewis & Short), which comes from equus 'horse'. English equitation comes from it.

Lesson 2 of 5.
ephippium, ephippiī — a saddle cloth, a horse blanket.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a whip, then the Latin for to ride.

You'll want to know: ephippium, ephippiī.
ephippium, ephippiī — "a saddle cloth, a horse blanket".
Servus ephippium in equum pōnit. The slave puts the saddle cloth on the horse.
Second declension, neuter: ephippium, ephippiī n. Riders sat on a padded cloth rather than a modern saddle.

The word, taken apart.
ephippium was borrowed from Greek ephippion 'saddlecloth', built from epi 'on' and hippos 'horse' (EtymDB; Lewis & Short).

Lesson 3 of 5.
calcar, calcāris — a spur.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to ride, then the Latin for a saddle cloth.

You'll want to know: calcar, calcāris.
calcar, calcāris — "a spur".
Eques calcar habet, sed equus lentē it. The rider has a spur, but the horse goes slowly.
Third declension, neuter: calcar, calcāris n. Figurative too: a spur to effort.

The word, taken apart.
calcar is usually linked with calx, calcis 'heel': Lewis & Short compare calx and quote an ancient explanation that the spur is tied to the heel. EtymDB lists the word but gives no parent.

Lesson 4 of 5.
dēsiliō, dēsilīre — to jump down, to leap down.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a saddle cloth, then the Latin for a spur.

You'll want to know: dēsiliō, dēsilīre.
dēsiliō, dēsilīre — "to jump down, to leap down".
Ad portam eques ex equō dēsilit. At the gate the rider jumps down from his horse.
dēsiliō, dēsilīre, dēsiluī, dēsultum, with ex or dē. English desultory comes from dēsultor, a circus rider who leapt from horse to horse.

The word, taken apart.
dēsiliō is built from dē- 'down' and saliō 'to leap, to jump' (EtymDB; Lewis & Short). English desultory comes from dēsultōrius, built on its participle.

Lesson 5 of 5.
calcitrō, calcitrāre — to kick (with the heels); to resist.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a spur, then the Latin for to jump down.

You'll want to know: calcitrō, calcitrāre.
calcitrō, calcitrāre — "to kick (with the heels); to resist".
Mūlus calcitrat, et servus fugit. The mule kicks, and the slave runs away.
A first-conjugation verb; the perfect is rare. English recalcitrant comes from recalcitrāre 'to kick back'.

The word, taken apart.
calcitrō is built on calx, calcis 'heel' (EtymDB; Lewis & Short): to strike out with the heels. English recalcitrant comes from the compound recalcitrāre.
