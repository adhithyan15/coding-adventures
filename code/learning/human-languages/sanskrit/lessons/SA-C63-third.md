## You'll want to know: तृतीयः
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[SA-LEX-C63-ORDINAL-04]; assesses=[SA-LEX-NUMBERS-ONE-TO-FIVE, SA-SCRIPT-RECOG-225] -->

**तृतीयः** (*tṛtīyaḥ*) — "third".

Neither **-म** nor **-थ**. This is a third ending, and the number under it has moved as well.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[SA-GRAMMAR-C63-ORDINAL-TIYA-05]; assesses=[SA-LEX-C63-ORDINAL-04, SA-SCRIPT-RECOG-225] -->

Macdonell prints it **tri-tīya**, and the printed form is **तृतीयः**. Two things happened:

1. The ending is **-तीय**, longer than the other two and unlike either.
2. The **इ** of **त्रि** became **ऋ** — the vowel you learned to read two chapters ago, here doing ordinary work inside an ordinary word.

That second point is why this lesson is worth its place even for a reader who only wanted the vocabulary. **ऋ** arrived in this book as the first letter of **ऋतुः**, a word where it simply is what it is. Here it is the RESULT of something: a vowel that shifted when an ending was put on. The letter has a job, not just a shape.

The English cousin kept the **-t-** ending instead: *thir-d*, from an older *thrid-*. Latin took the same road with *tertius*.

<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[SA-LEX-C63-ORDINAL-04, SA-GRAMMAR-C63-ORDINAL-TIYA-05] -->
