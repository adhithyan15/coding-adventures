German, chapter 428: Ärger im Bad.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
der Abfluss — the drain, the plughole.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the German for to turn up, then the German for to shut off.

You'll want to know: der Abfluss.
der Abfluss — "the drain, the plughole".
Im Abfluss der Dusche sind viele Haare. There is a lot of hair in the shower drain.
Plural: die Abflüsse.

The word, taken apart.
Abfluss is built from ab 'away' and Fluss 'flow, river', inherited from Old High German fluz (EtymDB), from the verb for flowing.

What you've built.
One more word for telling, explaining and describing at B1.

Lesson 2 of 5.
verstopfen — to block, to clog.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the German for to shut off, then the German for the drain.

You'll want to know: verstopfen.
verstopfen — "to block, to clog".
Die Haare verstopfen den Abfluss. The hair blocks the drain.
Die Toilette ist verstopft — the toilet is blocked. Verstopft also means constipated.

The word, taken apart.
verstopfen is inherited from Middle High German verstophen and Old High German firstopfōn (EtymDB), built on stopfen 'to plug, to stuff'.

Lesson 4 of 5.
undicht — leaky, not watertight.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the German for to block, then the German for to drip.

You'll want to know: undicht.
undicht — "leaky, not watertight".
Das Dach über dem Bad ist undicht. The roof over the bathroom leaks.
Dicht means tight, sealed; undicht is its opposite.

The word, taken apart.
undicht is built from un- 'not' and dicht 'tight, dense', an inherited Germanic word (EtymDB lists dicht).

Lesson 5 of 5.
der Klempner — the plumber.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the German for to drip, then the German for leaky.

You'll want to know: der Klempner.
der Klempner — "the plumber".
Der Klempner kommt erst am Montag. The plumber is not coming until Monday.
Same form in the plural. In Austria: der Installateur.

The word, taken apart.
Klempner first meant a tinsmith, someone who hammers sheet metal. It is usually traced to klempern 'to bang on metal'; EtymDB lists the word without a source, so the origin is hedged.
