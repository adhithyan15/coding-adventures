Latin, chapter 423: Footpath, Mountain track, Side path, Slope, Step.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
sēmita, sēmitae — a footpath; a narrow way, a lane.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a ditch, then the Latin for a rampart.

You'll want to know: sēmita, sēmitae.
sēmita, sēmitae — "a footpath; a narrow way, a lane".
Viātor nōn viā sed sēmitā per silvam ambulat. The traveller walks through the wood not on the road but on a footpath.
First declension, feminine: sēmita, sēmitae f. Narrower than a via: room for feet, not for carts.

The word, taken apart.
sēmita is inherited: EtymDB traces it to Proto-Italic sēmitā and lists trāmes 'by-path' as a cousin. Lewis & Short explain it as a way that goes 'aside' (sē-) from the main road.

Lesson 2 of 5.
callis, callis — a mountain track; a rough path made by cattle.

[pause 2 seconds]

You'll want to know: callis, callis.
callis, callis — "a mountain track; a rough path made by cattle".
Pastor gregem per callem in montem dūcit. The shepherd leads his flock up the mountain along a rough track.
Third declension, usually masculine: callis, callis m. (sometimes feminine).

The word, taken apart.
callis is inherited. EtymDB connects it with PIE kel- 'to drive, to set in motion' and with celer 'quick'; Lewis & Short give no etymology, so the link stays a likely one rather than a sure one.

Lesson 3 of 5.
trāmes, trāmitis — a side path, a by-way; a cross path.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a footpath, then the Latin for a mountain track.

You'll want to know: trāmes, trāmitis.
trāmes, trāmitis — "a side path, a by-way; a cross path".
Mercātor trāmitem scit et ante aliōs ad oppidum pervenit. The merchant knows a side path and reaches the town before the others.
Third declension, masculine: trāmes, trāmitis m. A short cut across country, off the high road.

The word, taken apart.
trāmes is inherited. Lewis & Short link it with trāns 'across', so it is a path that cuts across country, and EtymDB lists sēmita as a cousin.

Lesson 4 of 5.
clīvus, clīvī — a slope; a sloping street, an uphill road.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a mountain track, then the Latin for a side path.

You'll want to know: clīvus, clīvī.
clīvus, clīvī — "a slope; a sloping street, an uphill road".
Clīvus longus est, et mūlus lentē ascendit. The slope is long, and the mule climbs slowly.
Second declension, masculine: clīvus, clīvī m. A street that climbs a hill is also a clīvus. English declivity and proclivity come from the same root.

The word, taken apart.
clīvus is inherited from PIE ḱleywós, built on ḱley- 'to lean, to incline' (EtymDB). English declivity and proclivity come from the related Latin adjectives dēclīvis and prōclīvis (Lewis & Short).

Lesson 5 of 5.
gradior, gradī — to step, to walk.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a side path, then the Latin for a slope.

You'll want to know: gradior, gradī.
gradior, gradī — "to step, to walk".
In viā angustā senex lentē graditur. On the narrow road the old man walks slowly.
Deponent: gradior, gradī, gressus sum. Passive endings, active meaning. Its compounds give English ingredient, progress and egress.

The word, taken apart.
gradior is inherited from PIE gʰredʰ- 'to walk, to go' (EtymDB), and gradus 'a step' is built on the same root. English gradient and grade go back to this family (Lewis & Short).
