Spanish, chapter 318: Review --- Four Things Worth Saying Twice.
4 lessons. All 4 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 4.
Review — hasta pronto, and the word inside it.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Two words, and you have had both for a long time. What does hasta mean? (Until.) And pronto? (Soon — and also "quickly", and "ready".)

What you've built.
Say it once, slowly: hasta pronto, AHS-tah PRON-toh. "Until soon."
[pause 2 seconds]
Now take the second half on its own. Pronto came from Latin promptus, "brought forth, ready at hand". Something prompt is brought out without delay — and that is the whole distance between "ready" and "soon".
[pause 2 seconds]
English has the same word, unchanged in the root: prompt, promptly, and the computer prompt that sits there ready for you to type. Italian took it too, and says Pronto! — "Ready!" — when it answers the phone.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: hasta pronto, then pronto on its own]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: pronto, then English prompt, promptly — one word, two languages]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[repeat that twice]
[question — say your answer, then pause 8 seconds]
Say see you soon in Spanish.
[question — say your answer, then pause 14 seconds]
What Latin word is pronto from, and what did it mean?

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Which of your goodbyes says you expect to meet again shortly? (Hasta pronto.) And what is pronto on its own? (Soon, and "ready" — the sense English kept in prompt.)


Lesson 2 of 4.
Review — two sorries, and how to tell them apart.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
What does lo siento say, word for word? ("I feel it" — siento from Latin sentīre, "to feel".)

What you've built.
English has one word where Spanish has two, and the split follows the etymologies you already hold.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
phrase: lo siento. word for word: "I feel it". points at: your feeling.
phrase: perdón. word for word: "give [it] completely". points at: their forgiveness.
[pause 2 seconds]
Lo siento names a feeling — regret, sympathy — and stretches from stepping on a foot to hearing that someone has died. Perdón asks for something back, which is why it is also what you say squeezing past a stranger in a corridor.
[pause 2 seconds]
Be honest about the edges, as the first pass was: speakers reach for whichever fits, the boundary is soft, and mixing them is a shade off rather than a mistake.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: lo siento, then perdón — feeling, then request]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: lo siento mucho — I feel it a lot]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[repeat that twice]
[question — say your answer, then pause 10 seconds]
You need to get past somebody in a narrow corridor. Which word?
[question — say your answer, then pause 10 seconds]
What does lo siento mean word for word?
[question — say your answer, then pause 14 seconds]
What are the two Latin pieces inside perdonar?

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Which one leans toward sympathy rather than fault? (Lo siento.) Which one is asking the other person for something? (Perdón — a pardon is a thing granted.)


Lesson 3 of 4.
Review — four nouns, easiest gender to hardest.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Two you can answer without thinking. Say "the head." (La cabeza.) Say "the wine." (El vino.) The ending told you both times.

What you've built.
Line up four nouns you already own, in order of how much the ending can be trusted.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
noun: la cabeza. ending: -a. gender: feminine, as expected.
noun: el vino. ending: -o. gender: masculine, as expected.
noun: la mano. ending: -o. gender: feminine anyway.
[pause 2 seconds]
Cabeza and vino are the ordinary case, and they are worth saying out loud precisely because nothing about them is interesting: the ending points one way and the article agrees.
[pause 2 seconds]
La mano is the famous one. Latin manus sat in the fourth declension, whose -us nouns were mostly masculine — and manus was one of the handful that were feminine instead. So it broke the pattern twice: once inside Latin, and once again against Spanish's usual -o.

Grammar Lens: when the ending will not tell you.
The fourth noun is the one where even the article is unhelpful.
Bebo el agua. — I drink the water. La bebo. — I drink it.
[pause 2 seconds]
The article said el to keep two a sounds from colliding, which is a fact about sound and not about gender. The object pronoun is under no such pressure, so it reports the gender straight — and it reports la.
[pause 2 seconds]
That is a test you can run on any noun whose article looks suspicious: listen for which pronoun a speaker reaches for.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: la cabeza, el vino, la mano — two obedient, one not]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: el agua, then la bebo — the article, then the witness]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[repeat that twice]
[question — say your answer, then pause 8 seconds]
Say the hand in Spanish, with its article.
[question — say your answer, then pause 12 seconds]
An article looks like it is hiding a noun's gender. What can you listen for instead?
[question — say your answer, then pause 10 seconds]
Give the article and noun for the wine, then for the head.

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Which of the four nouns ends in -o and is feminine anyway? (La mano.) El agua — which pronoun replaces it? (La.) And why does that not contradict the article? (The article changed for sound, not gender.)


Lesson 4 of 4.
Review — two marks that were added, and when.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Why does Spanish open a question with ¿? (Because the words do not move — so the reader needs an early warning to lift their voice.)

What you've built.
The ¿ is younger than the language, and it has a date.
[pause 2 seconds]
The Spanish Royal Academy made the opening mark official in 1754. English can usually tell you a question is coming by reordering the words — Are you…, Do you… — so its final? arrives late and costs nothing. Spanish does not reorder: Tienes hambre and ¿Tienes hambre? are the same words. Something had to carry the warning, and the Academy put it at the front.
[pause 2 seconds]
A melody cue placed before the melody. That is the whole idea, and it is the same instinct as the written accent showing a stress you could not otherwise see.

Why it's said this way: a letter that was never a sound.
Now the second mark, and it is quieter.
[pause 2 seconds]
Latin germānus is stressed on its third syllable, which leaves the opening ger- unstressed — and there the g simply fell away, giving Old Spanish ermano, with no h at all. The h on today's hermano was added later, in spelling only, to point back at the Latin g-.
[pause 2 seconds]
Keep it apart from the other silent h. In hijo and hacer the h stands where Latin had an f, and that h really was breathed aloud for centuries. Hermano's never was. Same silent letter, two histories, and only one of them ever involved a sound.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: germānus, then ermano, then hermano — the three stages]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: a question of your own, and start lifting your voice where the ¿ sits]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[repeat that twice]
[question — say your answer, then pause 12 seconds]
Who made the opening question mark official, and in what year?
[question — say your answer, then pause 10 seconds]
Was the h in hermano ever pronounced?

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
What year did the Academy make ¿ official? (1754.) And what happened to germānus's opening letter? (Its g dropped away in an unstressed syllable — the h was put on the page afterwards, and never spoken.)
