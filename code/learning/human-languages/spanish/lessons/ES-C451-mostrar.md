## You'll want to know: mostrar
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-C451-TURNO-04]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C451-TURNO-03] -->

**mostrar** — to show.

> Es obligatorio **mostrar** el carné a la entrada.

*Muéstrame la lista* — show me the list. *El gráfico muestra tres años* — the
chart shows three years.

In speech Spaniards more often say **enseñar** for this — *enséñame el
carné*. **Mostrar** is what the sign says and what the form says, so it is a
word you read more than you speak. Knowing which register a word belongs to is
part of knowing the word.

## Grammar Lens: the o that breaks again
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C451-TURNO-04, ES-LEX-C449-TALLER-03] -->

You have watched a stressed **o** turn into **ue** in *la rueda*. Here it is
inside a verb, doing exactly the same thing:

| stress on the stem | stress on the ending |
|---|---|
| m**ue**stro, m**ue**stras | mostr**a**mos |
| m**ue**stra, m**ue**stran | mostr**á**is |

The infinitive *mostrar* has the plain **o** because the stress is on the
*-ar*. Push the stress back onto the stem and the vowel breaks.

So this verb is not irregular in any interesting sense. It is one vowel
obeying one condition, and the same condition you used to explain a noun now
explains a conjugation.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C451-TURNO-04] -->

Latin **monstrare**, to point out — from **monstrum**, and that is where it
gets interesting.

A *monstrum* was an omen: something strange that the gods had **shown** you as
a warning. The horror came later, from the strangeness of the sign. So
**mostrar** and **monstruo** are the same root, one keeping the showing and
the other keeping the thing shown.

English has both halves as well, in **demonstrate** and **monster**, and the
pair is a clean example of a root splitting into a neutral word and a
frightening one while the idea inside stays the same: something put where you
cannot miss it.
