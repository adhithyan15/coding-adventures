# conocer — "to meet", because knowing has a first moment

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-JUGAR-07, ES-GRAMMAR-STEM-U-UE-09] -->

[PAUSE 2s] *Juego* — the one *u→ue* in the language. Now the chapter's last verb,
and its oddity is not in the vowel but in what the word means.

## Sounds you'll need
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[] -->

- `soft-c` — *conoces* has a soft second *c*: a *th* in Spain, an *s* elsewhere.
- `k-hard` — but *conozco* is *ko-NOTH-ko*, soft then hard, back to back.

## The word, taken apart: conocer
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-CONOCER-10, ES-ETYMON-CONOCER-11]; assesses=[ES-LEX-PARENTS-01, ES-ETYMON-PARENTS-02] -->

**conocer** ← Latin **cognōscere**, *com-* + *(g)nōscere*. Its *-sc-* is Latin's
**"begin to" marker**, so *nōscere* was never "to know" — it was "**to come to
know**". Latin said *nōvī*, "I have come to know", for actually knowing a thing.

Spanish built its verb from the *beginning* half. That is the whole lesson:
*conocer a alguien* is **to meet** someone, and Spanish puts a small **a** before
a person who is the object — *conozco a María*.

The root fills English: **cognition**, **recognize**, **incognito**, and through
French **notice**, **notion**, **notorious** and **noble**, "the knowable one".
English **know** and **ken** are its Germanic siblings — the same standing that
*padre* has beside English *father*, neither one borrowed from the other.

## Grammar Lens: conocer and saber
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-GRAMMAR-CONOCER-SABER-12]; assesses=[ES-LEX-CONOCER-10, ES-GRAMMAR-OIR-Y-03] -->

| what you know | verb |
|---|---|
| a person, a city, a book | **conocer** |
| a fact, or how to do something | **saber** |

English says *know* for both. Spanish will not: **conocer** is being
**acquainted** — you have met the thing. **Saber** is having the information.
*Conozco Madrid* means I have been there; *sé dónde está* means I have the fact.

The *yo*-form keeps a hard cluster the rest lost: **conozco, conoces, conoce,
conocemos, conocen**. Every *-cer* verb after a vowel does the same, and it is a
third kind of odd *yo*-form beside *traigo* and *oigo*.
