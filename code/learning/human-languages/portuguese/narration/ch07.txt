Portuguese, chapter 7: Days of the Week.
2 lessons. Both can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 2.
segunda to sexta — the week that got numbered.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Here Portuguese does something no other Romance language does. French, Italian, and Spanish all name their weekdays for planet-gods (lundi, lunedì, lunes = the Moon). Portuguese threw the pagan gods out and numbered the days instead — and the numbers are the very ones you just learned.

Why it's said this way: The story: a bishop renames the week.
In the 6th century, Martinho de Braga, a bishop in what is now northern Portugal, refused to let Christians name days after Mars and Jupiter. Borrowing the Church's word for the numbered holy days of Easter week, he called them feiras — and Portuguese alone kept the system for every week.
feira from Latin fēria, "a feast-day / a day free of work." (The same fēria drifted, elsewhere, into "market/fair" — English fair is its cousin, because markets were held on feast-days.)

Grammar Lens: The pattern: [ordinal] + feira.
[a table of 5 rows, read aloud]
Portuguese: segunda-feira (Mon). means: "2nd feast-day". from ordinal of (and the cardinal you learned): secundus "second"; dois (2).
Portuguese: terça-feira (Tue). means: "3rd". from ordinal of (and the cardinal you learned): tertius "third"; três (3).
Portuguese: quarta-feira (Wed). means: "4th". from ordinal of (and the cardinal you learned): quartus "fourth"; quatro (4).
Portuguese: quinta-feira (Thu). means: "5th". from ordinal of (and the cardinal you learned): quintus "fifth"; cinco (5).
Portuguese: sexta-feira (Fri). means: "6th". from ordinal of (and the cardinal you learned): sextus "sixth"; seis (6).
So quarta-feira is literally "fourth feast-day," and you can hear quatro (4) inside quarta. In everyday speech the "-feira" is often dropped: "Até segunda!" — "See you Monday!" (Why does Monday start at two, not one? Because Sunday is counted as the first day — the next lesson closes that loop.)

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: "segunda, terça, quarta, quinta, sexta" — Monday through Friday]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: hear the number in each — "quarta from quatro (4), quinta from cinco (5), sexta from seis (6)"]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: drop the tail — "Até sexta!" ("See you Friday!")]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
How is Portuguese unique among Romance languages here? (It numbers its weekdays — feiras — instead of naming planet-gods.) What does feira mean, and its English cousin? ("Feast-day," from fēria; English fair.) What number hides in quinta-feira? (Five — quinta from quintus, cousin of cinco.) Next: the two days that kept their old names — Saturday and Sunday.


Lesson 2 of 2.
sábado and domingo — and why Monday is "the second".

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Five weekdays got numbered — but two kept their names. Sábado and domingo are the religious anchors of the Portuguese week, and domingo is the key that explains why the counting started where it did.

The two days, taken apart.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
Portuguese from Latin: sábado from Sabbatum from Hebrew shabbāt. meaning: the "Sabbath," rest-day. shared with: Spanish sábado, Italian sabato, French samedi.
Portuguese from Latin: domingo from (diēs) Dominica from Dominus. meaning: "the Lord's day". shared with: Spanish domingo, Italian domenica.
sábado is the Sabbath, from Hebrew shabbāt "to rest" — the one weekend name every Romance language shares (even numbering-mad Portuguese kept it).
domingo is "the Lord's day," diēs Dominica, from Dominus "lord, master" ( becomes English dominion, dominate, dame).

Grammar Lens: domingo is "day one" — so Monday is "day two".
Now the loop closes. The Church counted domingo (Sunday) as the first day of the week — prima feria, the Lord's day, the start. That's why:
domingo = 1st becomes segunda-feira (Monday) = 2nd becomes terça (3rd) becomes … becomes sexta (Friday) = 6th becomes sábado (Saturday) = 7th.
Monday isn't the first weekday in this system — it's the day after the Lord's day, the second. So segunda-feira ("second feast-day") makes perfect sense once you know Sunday was counted first. The whole week is a countdown anchored on domingo.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: the full week — "domingo, segunda, terça, quarta, quinta, sexta, sábado"]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: the anchors — "sábado = Sabbath, domingo = the Lord's day"]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: the logic — "domingo is 1st, so segunda (Monday) is 2nd"]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Give all seven Portuguese days. (Domingo, segunda … sábado.) What does domingo mean, and why does that make Monday "segunda"? ("The Lord's day"; Sunday is counted first, so Monday is the second.) Which weekend name do all the Romance languages share? (Sábado — the Sabbath.) Next chapter builds on this toward telling the time.
