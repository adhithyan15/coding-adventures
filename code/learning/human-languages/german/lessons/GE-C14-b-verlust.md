# The b that fell out

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GE-GRAMMAR-HABEN-DU-HAST-01, GE-GRAMMAR-HABEN-ER-HAT-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] *Hast* and *hat* both lost a *b*. That is not two accidents.

## Grammar Lens: one shortcut, in exactly two cells
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[GE-GRAMMAR-HABEN-B-DROP-01]; assesses=[GE-GRAMMAR-HABEN-REGULAR-01] -->

> *Haben* keeps its **b** everywhere except before a *-st* or a *-t* ending,
> where the *b* drops and the vowel shortens with it.

Say *hab-st* and *hab-t* at speed and you can feel why: a *b* squeezed between a
vowel and a hard *t* is work, and centuries of speakers declined to do it. The
consonant wore away and the two shortest forms are what is left.

Now listen to English, which is the same verb:

| person | English | German |
|---|---|---|
| infinitive | *have* | *haben* |
| second person, older | *thou ha**st*** | *du ha**st*** |
| third person | *he ha**s*** | *er ha**t*** |

English lost the same consonant, in the same two cells, of the same verb — and
kept the archaic *hast* long enough for us to see it. Two languages, one
shortcut, inherited together rather than invented twice.
