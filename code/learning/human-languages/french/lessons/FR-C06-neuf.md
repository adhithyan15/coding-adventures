# neuf — nine

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[] -->

[PAUSE 2s] You met a final letter that goes silent before a consonant. Nine
shows you the other move: a final letter that **changes shape** to reach a vowel.

## Sounds you'll need
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[FR-LEX-NEUF-11]; assesses=[] -->

- **neuf** = *nuhf*. The vowel is the rounded *eu* you learned in **deux**, and
  the **f** is sounded.

## Grammar Lens: the f that turns into a v
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[FR-PHON-LIAISON-NUMBERS-08] -->

Standing before a vowel, the **f** of *neuf* softens into a **v**:

- **neuf ans** ("nine years") = *neu-**v**-ans*
- **neuf heures** ("nine o'clock") = *neu-**v**-eures*

This is the same instinct you saw in *six ans*: French will not let a consonant
sit awkwardly against a following vowel, so it bends the sound to bridge them.
With *six* the ending woke up as a *z*; with *neuf* it softens to a *v*. Both
are liaison, doing the one job of keeping the line smooth.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[] -->

**neuf** (9) comes from Latin **novem**. Once again the month kept the number
visible: **novembre** wears its *nov-* on the outside, and English has
**novena**, a devotion of nine days. Spanish made *nueve*.
