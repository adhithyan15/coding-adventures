# seis, siete, ocho, nueve, diez — and the calendar's secret

## Warm-up

[PAUSE 2s] The rest of the way to ten — and these four hide a fact that explains
the English calendar: **September through December are just Latin for 7, 8, 9,
10.**

## Sounds you'll need

- `diphthong-ie` — **siete** = *SYE-teh*, **nueve** = *NWE-veh* (the *ie/ue*
  glide, a Spanish signature).

## The numbers, taken apart

| Spanish | ← Latin | English cousins |
|---|---|---|
| **seis** (6) | *sex* | **sextet, sextant, semester** (six months) |
| **siete** (7) | *septem* | **September** (the *7th* month!), septet, septuagenarian |
| **ocho** (8) | *octō* | **October** (8th), **octopus, octave, octagon** |
| **nueve** (9) | *novem* | **November** (9th), novena |
| **diez** (10) | *decem* | **December** (10th), **decimal, decade, dime, decimate** |

Why are the "7th–10th" months numbered 7–10 when they sit at 9–12? Because the
old **Roman year began in March** — so *September* really was the 7th month.
Julius and Augustus later inserted **July** and **August** (named for
themselves), shoving the counting-months out of place, but the Latin numbers
stuck. Every time you say *December*, you're saying Roman "**ten**."

## Grammar Lens: building the teens (a fusion)

Past ten, Spanish **fuses** "ten and X" into one word:

- *diez y seis* ("ten and six") → **dieciséis** (16).
- *diez y siete* → **diecisiete** (17), and so on to 19.

English did the identical thing: *six + ten* → **sixteen**. So *dieciséis* and
*sixteen* are built the same way, from the same two numbers.
