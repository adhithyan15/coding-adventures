# Mittag and midi

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GE-LEX-MITTAG-01, GE-ETYMON-MITTE-MEDIUS-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] French says *midi* for noon, and it means exactly what *Mittag*
means. That is not a borrowing in either direction.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[GE-ETYMON-MITTAG-MIDI-01]; assesses=[GE-LEX-MITTAG-01] -->

French *midi* comes from Latin ***medius diēs*** — "middle day." German
*Mittag* is *Mitte* plus *Tag* — "middle day."

The **same thought**, assembled **twice**, out of different material:

| language | built from | result |
|---|---|---|
| Latin, then French | *medius* + *diēs* | *midi* |
| German | *Mitte* + *Tag* | *Mittag* |

Both families looked at the sun overhead and named the moment for the middle of
the day. Neither copied the other; the idea is obvious enough that two languages
reached it independently, and each used the words it had.

## Why it's said this way
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GE-ETYMON-MITTAG-MIDI-01, GE-GRAMMAR-NATIVE-VS-LOAN-01, GE-LEX-UHR-01] -->

This is the counterweight to *Uhr*, and the two are worth holding together.

For the **clock** — a machine that arrived from outside — German took the
foreign word whole. For **noon** — something anyone with a sky already has —
German built its own out of pieces it owned.

A language borrows what arrives and builds what was always there. One chapter,
both halves of that.
