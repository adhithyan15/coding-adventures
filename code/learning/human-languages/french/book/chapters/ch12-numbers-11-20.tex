\chapter{Numbers Eleven to Twenty}
\label{ch:numbers-11-20}

The French teens are not built the way you would guess. Six of them came out of
Latin already welded together; then, at exactly seventeen, the language changes
its mind in front of you. \emph{Practice lessons:
\texttt{lessons/FR-C12-*.md}.}

\section{\emph{onze} $\to$ \emph{seize} --- six numbers welded together}
\label{lesson:nombres-11-16}

French did not say ``ten-one, ten-two.'' It inherited six numbers already
\textbf{fused} in Latin, and they came through almost unrecognisable.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}llll@{}}
\toprule
& French & $\leftarrow$ Latin & literally \\
\midrule
11 & \emph{onze} & \emph{\=undecim} & ``one-ten'' \\
12 & \emph{douze} & \emph{duodecim} & ``two-ten'' \\
13 & \emph{treize} & \emph{tr\=edecim} & ``three-ten'' \\
14 & \emph{quatorze} & \emph{quattuordecim} & ``four-ten'' \\
15 & \emph{quinze} & \emph{qu\={\i}ndecim} & ``five-ten'' \\
16 & \emph{seize} & \emph{s\=edecim} & ``six-ten'' \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{cousinweb}
Look at the ending they all share: \textbf{-ze}. That is Latin \emph{decem}
(``ten''), worn down to almost nothing --- the \emph{same} ten that hides in
\emph{dix} (10) and in \emph{d\'{e}cembre}, the old tenth month. One root,
three disguises.
\end{cousinweb}

\begin{grammarlens}
\textbf{The digit is still in there.} The front of each word is the small
number it is built on: \emph{un} $\to$ \emph{on-}, \emph{deux} $\to$
\emph{dou-}, \emph{trois} $\to$ \emph{trei-}, \emph{quatre} $\to$
\emph{quator-}, \emph{cinq} $\to$ \emph{quin-}, \emph{six} $\to$ \emph{sei-}.
Say them slowly and the small number surfaces. Note the order: the \emph{digit}
comes first and the \emph{ten} second --- \emph{tr\=edecim} is ``three-ten,''
not ``ten-three.''
\end{grammarlens}

\section{\emph{dix-sept} $\to$ \emph{vingt} --- where French changes its mind}
\label{lesson:nombres-17-20}

Here is something you can \emph{watch happen}. Up to \emph{seize} (16), French
used the old welded-together Latin numbers. Then, at \textbf{17}, it simply
gives up --- and starts saying the numbers plainly, the way a child would.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
& French & how it is built \\
\midrule
16 & \emph{seize} & one fused word ($\leftarrow$ \emph{s\=edecim}) \\
17 & \emph{dix-sept} & ``ten-seven'' --- two words, plainly \\
18 & \emph{dix-huit} & ``ten-eight'' \\
19 & \emph{dix-neuf} & ``ten-nine'' \\
20 & \emph{vingt} & $\leftarrow$ \emph{v\={\i}gint\={\i}} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{grammarlens}
\textbf{Two things change at the seam.} In \emph{seize}, the digit came
\emph{first} (``six-ten''). In \emph{dix-sept}, the \emph{ten} comes first
(``ten-seven''). So at exactly 17 French switched both its \textbf{strategy}
(fused $\to$ transparent) and its \textbf{direction} (digit-first $\to$
ten-first) --- a visible seam in the counting system. Latin itself wobbled
here: it had \emph{septendecim} for 17, but for 18 and 19 it counted
\emph{down} from twenty --- \emph{duod\=ev\={\i}gint\={\i}}, ``two-from-twenty.''
The daughter languages all abandoned that subtraction trick.
\end{grammarlens}

\begin{cousinweb}
\textbf{vingt} (20) $\leftarrow$ Latin \emph{v\={\i}gint\={\i}}, the root
behind English \textbf{vigesimal} (``counting by twenties'') --- and the reason
French later builds \emph{quatre-vingts} (80 $=$ ``four twenties'').
\end{cousinweb}
