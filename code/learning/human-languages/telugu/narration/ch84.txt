Telugu, chapter 84: When You Are Not Well.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
జ్వరం (jvaraṁ) — a fever.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Telugu for a disease, then the Telugu for health.

You'll want to know: జ్వరం.
జ్వరం (jvaraṁ) — "a fever".
నాకు జ్వరం (jvaraṁ) వచ్చింది — "I have a fever", literally "to me a fever has come".
Notice the shape. The person sits in the dative నాకు and the fever arrives, on the same come-verb as నాకు తెలుగు వచ్చు. In Telugu an illness is something that comes to you.

The word, taken apart.
జ్వరం (jvaraṁ) is Sanskrit jvara, "fever", taken over whole as a learned loan, a tatsama, with the Telugu noun ending -ṁ (CDIAL 5303).

What you've built.
Your first word for what is wrong, on the pattern you already use for knowing Telugu.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: jvaraṁ]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: jvaraṁ, once more]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: say nāku jvaraṁ vaccindi, I have a fever]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — recall: say the Telugu for a disease, then the Telugu for health, then say jvaraṁ again]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
What does జ్వరం (jvaraṁ) mean? ("A fever".) Say it once more.


Lesson 2 of 5.
నొప్పి (noppi) — a pain, an ache.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Telugu for health, then the Telugu for a fever.

You'll want to know: నొప్పి.
నొప్పి (noppi) — "a pain, an ache".
Put a body part in front of it and you have the complaint: తల నొప్పి (tala noppi), a headache, and కడుపు నొప్పి (kaḍupu noppi), a stomach ache. Both body words are ones you already own.

The word, taken apart.
నొప్పి (noppi) is native Dravidian, one word with Tamil nō and Kannada nō (DEDR 3793). DEDR lists Telugu noppi, "pain, ache".

What you've built.
One word that turns every body part you know into something you can complain about.

Lesson 3 of 5.
అలసట (alasaṭa) — tiredness.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Telugu for a fever, then the Telugu for a pain.

You'll want to know: అలసట.
అలసట (alasaṭa) — "tiredness".
నాకు అలసటగా ఉంది — "I feel tired", literally "to me it is tired-ly".
The ending -గా turns the noun into a manner, and ఉంది says "it is". This frame, నాకు... గా ఉంది, is how Telugu says many of the things English says with I am.

The word, taken apart.
అలసట (alasaṭa) is native Dravidian. DEDR 236 lists alasaṭa, "weariness", with alayu, "to be tired", beside Tamil ala, "to suffer, be in distress". DEDR suggests that Sanskrit alasa, "lazy, tired", came from this Dravidian root.

What you've built.
The frame నాకు... గా ఉంది, which the next words keep using.

Lesson 4 of 5.
దాహం (dāhaṁ) — thirst.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Telugu for a pain, then the Telugu for tiredness.

You'll want to know: దాహం.
దాహం (dāhaṁ) — "thirst".
నాకు దాహంగా ఉంది — "I am thirsty". Same frame as tiredness, a new noun in the middle.
And you can already ask for the cure: నాకు నీళ్ళు కావాలి, "I want water".

The word, taken apart.
దాహం (dāhaṁ) is Sanskrit dāha, "burning; fever", taken over whole as a learned loan, a tatsama, with the Telugu noun ending -ṁ (CDIAL 6321). Telugu uses it for thirst.

What you've built.
Thirst, and the request that answers it.

Lesson 5 of 5.
విశ్రాంతి (viśrānti) — rest.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Telugu for tiredness, then the Telugu for thirst.

You'll want to know: విశ్రాంతి.
విశ్రాంతి (viśrānti) — "rest".
విశ్రాంతి (viśrānti) తీసుకోండి — "please take some rest", what you say to someone with a fever or a long day behind them. The polite ending -అండి is the one you already put on raṇḍi.

The word, taken apart.
విశ్రాంతి (viśrānti) is Sanskrit viśrānti, "rest, repose", taken over whole as a learned loan, a tatsama. CDIAL 11955 lists the related viśrānta, "rested".

What you've built.
Something to say to the person who is unwell, not only about yourself.
