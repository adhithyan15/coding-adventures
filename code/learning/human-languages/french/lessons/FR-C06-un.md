# un — one

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Numbers are the most-reused words in any language, and French
inherited them from Latin almost unchanged. You already half-know them through
English. We take them **one at a time**, starting with the first.

## Sounds you'll need
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[FR-LEX-UN-01]; assesses=[] -->

- `nasal-un` — **un** is a **nasal vowel**, close to "uh" hummed through the
  nose. No *n* is ever pronounced. The letters *u-n* are a single sound, not two.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[] -->

**un** (1) comes from Latin **ūnus**, and that root is everywhere in English:

- **unit** — one thing counted
- **union** — many made one
- **unique** — one of its kind
- **universe** — everything turned into one

English **one** and **a/an** descend from the same ancient word, worn down at
home rather than borrowed. Spanish keeps the Latin shape more plainly: *uno*.

## Grammar Lens: one word for "one" and "a"
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[FR-GRAM-UN-UNE-02]; assesses=[] -->

French draws no line between "one" and "a/an." **un** does both jobs, and it
takes the gender of the noun it stands in front of:

- **un** before a masculine noun — *un jour*, "a day" **or** "one day"
- **une** before a feminine noun — *une nuit*, "a night" **or** "one night"

You met that same masculine/feminine split in *le* and *la*. It is the same
split, on a different word. Spanish does exactly this too (*un* / *una*). The
counting words that follow never change shape; this first one is the only one
that does.
