# se chove, não saio — the condition that really happens

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[PT-GRAMMAR-SE-01, PT-LEX-FAZIA-FRIO-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say "if". (*Se.*) Say "it was raining". (*Chovia.*) Now the present:
"it rains". (*Chove.*)

## Grammar Lens: both halves in the present
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[PT-GRAMMAR-SE-PRESENTE-01]; assesses=[PT-LEX-C55-VERBOS5-02, PT-LEX-C143-VERBOS143-02, PT-LEX-DEVIA-01] -->

> **Se chove, não saio.** — If it rains, I don't go out.
> **Se o comboio é caro, apanho o autocarro.** — If the train is expensive, I
> take the bus.

This is a **real** condition: something true right now, or true whenever it
happens. Portuguese puts both halves in the plain present, as English does,
so there is nothing new to build.

The result half can also be advice, with the *devias* of chapter two hundred
and sixty-two:

> *Se estás constipado, **devias** descansar.* — If you've got a cold, you
> should rest.

## Why it's said this way
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[PT-LEX-PERDI-01, PT-GRAMMAR-TENHO-UM-PROBLEMA-02, PT-GRAMMAR-DUAS-SETAS-01] -->

A real condition is a plan with a branch in it. Someone who takes the train
to work every day lives by two of them:

> *Se perco o comboio, apanho o autocarro. Se perco o autocarro, volto para
> casa.*

Compare *por isso*. *Chove, por isso não saio* says it **is** raining, and
here is the consequence. *Se chove, não saio* says it **might**, and here is
what happens if so. Same consequence; the second one has not decided yet
whether the cause is true.

## Grammar Lens: one occasion still to come
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[PT-NOTICE-SE-CHOVER-02]; assesses=[PT-LEX-C59-TEMPO-01] -->

The present is right for a habit, or for what holds now. For **one**
particular occasion still to come, standard Portuguese bends the verb after
*se* into a form of its own, the **future subjunctive**:

> *Se **chover** amanhã, não saio.* — If it rains tomorrow, I won't go out.

*Chover*, not *chove*. For a regular verb it sounds just like the infinitive;
irregular verbs have forms of their own, which a later chapter builds. For
now, **recognise** it. Brazil and Portugal share it.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[] -->

**chover** is inherited from spoken Latin *plovere* (EtymDB 2.1), a reshaping
of classical *pluere*, "to rain" (Lewis & Short, under *pluo*). Latin *pl-*
became Portuguese *ch-*: **chuva** from *pluvia* and **chegar** from *plicāre*
(both EtymDB 2.1), and **cheio**, "full", usually traced to *plēnus* (Lewis &
Short). Four words, one sound
change.
