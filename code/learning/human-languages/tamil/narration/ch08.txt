Lesson 2 of 12.
தயவுசெய்து (tayavu seytu) — please (do the kindness).

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Across a whole band of Asia, the word for please is really the word compassion, dressed as a small request. Tamil says it most literally of all: "do the kindness."

You'll want to know: The phrase.
தயவுசெய்து (tayavu seytu) = please, literally "do the kindness / grace." It is two pieces snapped together:
தயவு (tayavu) = kindness, compassion, grace — from Sanskrit daya "compassion."
செய்து (seytu) = "having done," from the verb செய் (sey) "to do."
So tayavu seytu is an invitation: do me the kindness (of…). You place it in front of a request the way English puts "please" in front of one.
This is the same instinct as Arabic min faḍlik ("from your grace") and Hindi kṛpayā (from kṛpā, "compassion"). Naming the kindness you hope for is one of the great strategies of politeness — different from French's "if it pleases you," and from Latin/German's "I ask."

The word, taken apart - The Dravidian family, side by side.
All four Dravidian cousins build please the very same way — daya plus a light verb "do / place / become":
Tamil தயவுசெய்து tayavu seytu — kindness + do.
Kannada ದಯವಿಟ್ಟು dayaviṭṭu — compassion + having placed.
Telugu దయచేసి dayacēsi — compassion + having done.
Malayalam ദയവായി dayavāyi — compassion + as / having become.
One idea, four scripts. Learn one and you can almost read the others.

The consonant ச begins with ca. Add the short-o sign ொ, and it reads so:
ச + ொ becomes சொ (so).
The short-o sign reaches around its consonant. Part appears on the left and part on the right, but together they are one vowel sign. Read the whole joined shape as one syllable: சொ, so.
Do not invent a stroke order from the printed font: the strip gives a sourced one, in written order — the left part of ொ, then ச, then the right part.

Lesson 8 of 12.
தயவுசெய்து (tayavu seytu) or -உங்கள்? — politeness in phrase or verb.

Before we start: you can do this one in the car, but part of it needs your eyes. For that part you will want your eyes, for letter shapes on the page. You can listen to everything else now. I will say so again when we reach them, but leave these sections until you have stopped: Script you'll notice: Read the join in செய்து and Guided Practice.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Tamil has a phrase meaning "do the kindness," but everyday courtesy often sits somewhere English speakers do not expect: inside the verb.

Why it's said this way: Choose by emphasis.
A standalone தயவுசெய்து (tayavu seytu) is more emphatic and formal than English "please." Ordinary polite commands use respectful -உங்கள் (-uṅgaḷ):
உட்காருங்கள் uṭkāruṅgaḷ — please sit.
சொல்லுங்கள் solluṅgaḷ — please tell me — the request you can now say, read, and write from hearing.
Use tayavu seytu to mark "please, do me the kindness" warmly or formally. Let the verb ending carry routine courtesy.

Script you'll notice: Read the join in செய்து.
[once you have stopped driving — this part needs your eyes or your hands; if you are driving, skip ahead to the next part]
செ is ca with the ெ sign for e. ய் is ya shut off by puḷḷi, a bare y, before து tu. The same dot that built numbers now makes the consonant seam in seytu visible.

ந (na) — the third n, and the only one built from uprights.

Before we start: this one needs your hands, so it is not a driving lesson. You will want a pen and something to write on and your eyes, for letter shapes on the page. Warm-up.
[pause 1 second]
Before the new one: say the sound of ன.
[pause 2 seconds]
One letter this time. Just one.
The third n, and the one that finally looks different: no spiral loop at all. ந is built from a top bar and two uprights, with a right stroke that curls below the baseline. If you can see that it has no loop, you can already tell it from the other two.

Script you'll notice: ந.
[once you have stopped driving — this part needs your eyes or your hands; if you are driving, skip ahead to the next part]
ந — na.
What it is made of:
a left upright joined to the top bar.
a middle upright hanging from the top bar.
a right bowl that leaves the middle upright, curls below the baseline, and sweeps left into a descender.
நன்றி — thank you — opens with it, and so does நான்.

Writing: ந.
1. start at the lower left and draw the left upright upward.
2. without lifting, carry the top bar to the right.
3. without lifting, make the short return left to the middle upright.
4. without lifting, draw the middle upright straight down.
5. without lifting, climb back up to the junction and descend around the outside of the right bowl.
6. without lifting, turn through the bottom and sweep left into the below-baseline tail — and only now lift.
Pen lifts: 0. The pen never leaves the paper.
Stroke order is one attested teaching order, not a national standard — Tamil handwriting is taught with school-to-school variation. Module 5 notes that the extended final curve may be omitted. Source: Sankaran Radhakrishnan, Tamil Script Learners Manual, Appendix I: Hand-movements, Frame 5, ந (University of Texas at Austin), p. 193.
