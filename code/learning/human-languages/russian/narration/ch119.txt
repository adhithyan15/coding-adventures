Russian, chapter 119: Ankle, Wrist, Cheek, Lips, Chest.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
лодыжка (lodýzhka) — an ankle.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Russian for a dolphin, then the Russian for an elbow.

You'll want to know: лодыжка.
лодыжка (lodýzhka) — "an ankle".

The word, taken apart.
лодыжка (lodýzhka) is built on лодыга, "an ankle bone; a hollow stalk", with -ка. Vasmer calls лодыга unclear and finds the proposed sources, a Polish loan, Latin lactūca, "lettuce", or German, unconvincing.

Lesson 2 of 5.
запястье (zapyástye) — a wrist.

[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Russian for an elbow, then the Russian for an ankle.

You'll want to know: запястье.
запястье (zapyástye) — "a wrist".

The word, taken apart.
запястье (zapyástye) is за-, "beyond", with пясть, "the palm, the metacarpus": the part beyond the hand. Vasmer relates пясть to Old High German fûst, "fist", so English fist is a cousin.

Lesson 3 of 5.
щека (shcheká) — a cheek.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Russian for an ankle, then the Russian for a wrist.

You'll want to know: щека.
щека (shcheká) — "a cheek".

The word, taken apart.
щека (shcheká) from Proto-Slavic ščeka — inherited. It is thought to be related to Old Norse skegg, "beard", and skagi, "a cape" (Vasmer); dialects use it for a steep river bank.

Lesson 4 of 5.
губы (gúby) — lips.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Russian for a wrist, then the Russian for a cheek.

You'll want to know: губы.
губы (gúby) — "lips".

The word, taken apart.
губы (gúby) is the plural of губа, "lip", Proto-Slavic gǫba. Its deeper origin is unclear: Vasmer weighs Greek gamphaí, "jaws", and the губа that means "sponge".

Lesson 5 of 5.
грудь (grud) — the chest.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Russian for a cheek, then the Russian for lips.

You'll want to know: грудь.
грудь (grud) — "the chest".

The word, taken apart.
грудь (grud) from Proto-Slavic grǫdĭ — inherited. Vasmer compares Latin grandis, "great", and Greek brénthos, "pride"; Vaillant, in a note, links it instead with гряда, "a beam, a bed", so the origin is open.
