Lesson 1 of 5.
vās, vāsis — a vessel, a container.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for an axe, then the Latin for a ladder.

You'll want to know: vās, vāsis.
vās, vāsis — "a vessel, a container".

The word, taken apart.
vās has no sure origin; no proposal is generally accepted. It is a different word from vas, vadis, "a guarantor, bail". English vase comes from vās through French.

Lesson 2 of 5.
amphora, amphorae — a two-handled jar.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a ladder, then the Latin for a vessel.

You'll want to know: amphora, amphorae.
amphora, amphorae — "a two-handled jar".

The word, taken apart.
amphora was borrowed from Greek amphoreus, "a two-handled jar" (Lewis & Short), from amphi, "on both sides", and pherō, "to carry": a jar carried by both handles.

Lesson 5 of 5.
nummus, nummī — a coin.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a flask, then the Latin for a purse.

You'll want to know: nummus, nummī.
nummus, nummī — "a coin".

The word, taken apart.
nummus probably came from the Greek of Sicily and south Italy, where noummos, "a coin", was in use; Lewis & Short connect it with Greek nemō, "to distribute". English numismatic, "of coins", comes from the Greek family.
