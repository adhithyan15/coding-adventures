Latin, chapter 290: Would.
4 lessons. You can do the first 3 of them in the car; after that you will want to have stopped.


Lesson 1 of 4.
velim — I would like.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say "I want to see Rome". (Rōmam vidēre volō.)
[pause 1 second]
Volō states a fact about you: I want. Said to a stranger it can sound blunt. Latin softens it by changing the verb's mood.

Grammar Lens: velim, the softer want.
Velim tē vidēre. — I would like to see you.
[pause 2 seconds]
velim is the subjunctive of volō: not "I want", but "I would want, I would like". All six persons:
velim, velīs, velit, velīmus, velītis, velint.
[pause 2 seconds]
The other wanting and able verbs do the same, with the same endings: possim, "I could, I might be able"; mālim, "I would rather".
Hoc facere possim. — I could do this. Mālim manēre. — I would rather stay.
[pause 1 second]
Cicero writes velim scire, "I should like to know", when he asks a friend a question in a letter (Lewis & Short quote it).

Why it's said this way: a mood, not a tense.
[pause 2 seconds]
Velim is not past or future. It is a different way of holding the same present: the indicative, volō, says what is; the subjunctive, velim, says what would be, or could be. Sum has one too, sim, "I may be", and the next lesson puts sum's other subjunctive to work.

The word, taken apart: the -i- of velim.
The -i- of velim, possim and sim is usually explained as the mark of an old wishing mood, which Greek kept as a mood of its own and Latin folded into the subjunctive. Neither EtymDB nor Lewis & Short records endings, so this is the account histories of Latin give, offered as one.

Lesson 2 of 4.
sī hīc essēs — if it were so.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say "if it rains, we stay in the farmhouse". (Sī pluit, in vīllā manēmus.) Then "I would like to see you". (Velim tē vidēre.)
[pause 1 second]
The first may well come true. Now a condition you know is false, about right now.

Grammar Lens: the infinitive plus an ending.
Sī hīc essēs, laetus essem. — If you were here, I would be happy.
[pause 2 seconds]
You are not here, and both halves say so by their mood. essem and essēs are the imperfect subjunctive of sum, and they are made the easiest way Latin has: take the infinitive, esse, and add the endings: essem, essēs, esset, essēmus, essētis, essent.
[pause 2 seconds]
The other verbs follow the same recipe: habēre, habērem, I would have; posse, possem, I could; velle, vellem, I would want.
Sī pecūniam habērem, nāvem emerem. — If I had money, I would buy a ship.
[pause 1 second]
Emerem is emere with -m. In English the "if" half sounds past, "if I had"; Latin agrees, and calls this the imperfect.

Why it's said this way: real or not.
[pause 2 seconds]
Set the two side by side:
Sī pluit, in vīllā manēmus. — If it rains, we stay in. (It may.) Sī nōn plueret, nāvigārēmus. — If it were not raining, we would be sailing. (It is.)
[pause 2 seconds]
Plain verbs leave the question open. The subjunctive tells the listener the "if" is false right now, and the speaker knows it.

Guided Practice.

[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: Sī hīc essēs, laetus essem.]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: Sī pecūniam habērem, nāvem emerem.]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: Sī nōn plueret, nāvigārēmus.]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: one thing that is not true now, and what would follow if it were]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[question — say your answer, then pause 10 seconds]
Build 'I would have' from habere.

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Say "if you were here, I would be happy". (Sī hīc essēs, laetus essem.) What is essem built on? (The infinitive, esse.) Say "I would have". (Habērem.) Which kind of condition says the "if" is false? (The one with the subjunctive.)


Lesson 3 of 4.
Review — what is, what may be, what would be.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say "I would like to see you". (Velim tē vidēre.) Then "if you were here, I would be happy". (Sī hīc essēs, laetus essem.)

What you've built.
Two chapters, and three ways to hold a thought. What is, and what may well be: plain verbs, sī pluit, manēmus. What depends: ex tempestāte pendet. What would be: the subjunctive, velim and possim for the polite and the possible, essem and habērem for an "if" that is not true now.
Sī pluit, in vīllā manēmus. Sed sī nōn plueret, nāvigārēmus, et laetus essem.
[pause 2 seconds]
The first sentence leaves the weather open. The second knows it is raining, and wishes it were not.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: Sī pecūniam habērem, nāvem emerem.]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: Hoc facere possim, sed mālim manēre.]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: In tuā potestāte est. — then Spērō tē venīre.]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: Sī nōn plueret, nāvigārēmus.]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: one real condition and one unreal one about today]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Say "I could". (Possim.) Say "if I had money". (Sī pecūniam habērem.) In sī pluit, manēmus, may it rain? (Yes, it is open.) In sī nōn plueret, is it raining? (Yes: that is why the verb is subjunctive.) What is the -i- of velim usually said to be? (An old wishing mood.)


Lesson 4 of 4.
A letter about a journey — a six-line letter to one friend about a journey — the Roman opening, a scene and its events, a feeling, and a condition at the end.

Before we start: this one needs your hands, so it is not a driving lesson. You will want a pen and something to write on. You can listen to all of it now and come back to the parts that need looking at once you have stopped.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Your four lines with joiners held together. These six lines are an epistula, a letter, and they tell a story.
[pause 1 second]
Pick one friend who will read it.

You'll want to know.
[pause 2 seconds]
A Roman letter opened with two names and two letters: Mārcus Iūliae S. D., Marcus to Julia, S. D., salūtem dīcit, "sends greeting". Your name goes first, in the nominative; your friend's in the dative.
[pause 2 seconds]
Then, often, a real condition: Sī valēs, bene est; ego valeō. "If you are well, good; I am well." Seneca calls it an old custom for the first words of a letter (Lewis & Short quote him), and it was common enough to be written as initials.
[pause 2 seconds]
The middle tells the story: the scene in the imperfect, the events in the perfect, in order. The end looks forward, and the last word is valē.

Writing — connected composition.
[pause 2 seconds]
On paper, write six lines to your friend about a journey, real or invented, one line for each of these:
the opening: your name, your friend's, S. D., and sī valēs, bene est;
the scene: when it was, with abhinc, and the weather, with erat or -bat;
the first events, with prīmum and deinde: iter faciō, a ship, the fare;
what went wrong, with subitō or quid accidit, in the perfect;
how you felt and why, with eram and quia or quod, and one comparison with quam;
one line forward, with sī … essēs, … essem, or velim, or spērō, then valē.
[pause 3 seconds]
Use only words you already write. The exercise is choosing the right past for each line.

Wrap-up recall.
[pause 2 seconds]
Now go through your lines as you went through the rainy day in your first story. Mark every verb S for scene or E for event. Line two should be all S; lines three and four mostly E; line five mostly S. A line about the weather marked E is the commonest slip: change pluit to pluēbat.
[pause 2 seconds]
Then two endings to check. The perfect for "they" ends in -ērunt; the imperfect has -ba- before its ending. And a feeling word agrees with whoever felt it: laeta eram if a woman is writing.
[pause 2 seconds]
Last, read line six on its own. If its sī is about something that may be true, are both verbs plain? If it is about something false, are both subjunctive, essēs and essem? Then the letter looks forward, and it is finished.
