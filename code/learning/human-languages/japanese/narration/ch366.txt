Lesson 1 of 6.
さいあく (saiaku) — the worst.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Japanese for a pension, then the Japanese for an average.

You'll want to know: さいあく.
さいあく — saiaku — "the worst".
さいあく、でんしゃが とまったら、あるいて かえります。 — saiaku, densha ga tomattara, aruite kaerimasu — "If the worst comes to the worst and the trains stop, I will walk home."
A na-adjective: な before a noun.

The word, taken apart.
さいあく (saiaku) is Sino-Japanese (kango): two Chinese characters read with their on'yomi, readings borrowed from Chinese. They are sai, "utmost" (Middle Chinese tswajH) and aku, "bad" ('ak) (KANJIDIC2; Middle Chinese in Baxter's transcription).

Lesson 2 of 6.
けっしんする (kesshin suru) — to make up one's mind.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Japanese for an average, then the Japanese for the worst.

You'll want to know: けっしんする.
けっしんする — kesshin suru — "to make up one's mind".
ははが さんせいしたら、けっしんします。 — haha ga sansei shitara, kesshin shimasu — "If my mother agrees, I will make up my mind."
Polite けっしんします, kesshinshimasu; a する verb. Past (polite): あには けっしんしました。, ani wa kesshin shimashita.

The word, taken apart.
けっしんする (kesshin suru) is built from a Sino-Japanese noun and the native verb suru, "to do". The noun, kesshin, is two Chinese characters read with their on'yomi, readings borrowed from Chinese: ketsu, "decide" (Middle Chinese kwet) and shin, "heart" (sim) (KANJIDIC2; JMdict; Middle Chinese in Baxter's transcription). The two join as kes-shin.

Lesson 5 of 6.
あせる (aseru) — to be impatient; to be in a rush.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Japanese for the sequence, then the Japanese for to put first.

You'll want to know: あせる.
あせる — aseru — "to be impatient; to be in a rush".
あせったら、まちがえるでしょう。 — asettara, machigaeru deshō — "If you rush, you will probably make mistakes."
Polite あせります, aserimasu; a godan verb. Past (polite): おとうとが あせりました。, otōto ga aserimashita.

The word, taken apart.
あせる (aseru) is a native Japanese word (wago), inherited rather than borrowed from Chinese, and its older origin is not settled. Its kanji was attached to it later as a kun'yomi, a native reading given to a Chinese character (JMdict, KANJIDIC2).
