Telugu, chapter 36: Son and Daughter, Two Different Roots.
2 lessons. All 2 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 2.
కొడుకు (koḍuku) — "son," and the unexpected word it shares a root with.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Kuṭumbaṁ, family; snēhituḍu, friend. Now the two words for your own children — and Telugu does not build them the tidy way you might expect.

You'll want to know: కొడుకు.
కొడుకు — koḍuku — son.
వాడి కొడుకు వయసెంత? — vāḍi koḍuku vayasenta? — "How old is his son?" — Chapter 19's verbless age question, asked about someone else's child.

The word, taken apart - the root that points sideways, not across.
కొడుకు is inherited from Proto-Dravidian kōẓ-, "young, tender." You might expect "daughter," Telugu's other child-word, to share that same root with a different ending — that is exactly the shape Kannada's magu/maga/ magalu takes. Telugu does not do that. The very same kōẓ- root instead gives Telugu కోడలు (kōḍalu), "daughter-in-law" — the woman a son marries, not the daughter a parent has. So koḍuku and kōḍalu are true cognates, one root apart; koḍuku and kūturu — son and daughter — are not related to each other at all. The next lesson takes up kūturu's own, separate root.

[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: "koḍuku" — son]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: "vāḍi koḍuku vayasenta?" — how old is his son?]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: the unexpected cognate — "koḍuku … kōḍalu" — son, daughter-in-law]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: three words for people — "kuṭumbaṁ … snēhituḍu … koḍuku"]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Say "son." (Koḍuku.) What Proto-Dravidian root does it come from, and what other Telugu word shares that exact root? (kōẓ-, "young, tender" — shared with కోడలు, "daughter-in-law," not with "daughter" itself.) Ask "how old is his son?" (Vāḍi koḍuku vayasenta?)


Lesson 2 of 2.
కూతురు (kūturu) — "daughter," on a root all its own.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Koḍuku's root turned up again inside a completely different Telugu word. This word's root does something even less expected: it turns up inside a different language.

You'll want to know: కూతురు.
కూతురు — kūturu — daughter.
నా కూతురు. — nā kūturu. — "My daughter."

The word, taken apart - a second root, and an unexpected cousin.
కూతురు (kūturu) is inherited from Proto-Dravidian kūnttu — a root with no connection to koḍuku's kōẓ-. Telugu's two child-words are not a matched pair; they are two separate inheritances that happen to sit side by side in the same sentence.
Kūturu's cousin is not in Telugu at all. Kannada ಕೂಸು (kūsu), "infant, maiden," answers to the very same root. So the word a Telugu speaker uses for "my daughter" and the word a Kannada speaker uses for "the baby" are, underneath, one and the same — a reminder that a cognate does not have to keep the same sense as it crosses a language boundary, only the same root.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: "nā kūturu" — my daughter]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: son, then daughter — "koḍuku … kūturu" — two unrelated roots]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: the cross-language cousin — "kūturu … kūsu" — daughter, infant]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: friend, son, daughter — "snēhituḍu … koḍuku … kūturu"]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Say "my daughter." (Nā kūturu.) Does kūturu share a root with koḍuku? (No — two separate Proto-Dravidian roots.) What word in another Dravidian language answers to kūturu's own root, and what does it mean there? (Kannada కూసు kūsu, "infant, maiden.")
