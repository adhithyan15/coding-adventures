Sanskrit, chapter 44: Colours.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
श्वेतः (śvetaḥ) — white.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new word: what did saralaḥ mean?

You'll want to know: श्वेतः.
श्वेतः (śvetaḥ) — "white".
श्वेतः (śvetaḥ) is white, and bright along with it.
English white is the same word by the Germanic road, and Russian svet, "light", is another of its brothers — brightness and whiteness were one idea for a long time.
हिमम् is श्वेतम्, and so is दधि.

What you've built.
White, and the brightness that comes with it.

Lesson 2 of 5.
श्यामः (śyāmaḥ) — dark, black.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new word: what did śvetaḥ mean?

You'll want to know: श्यामः.
श्यामः (śyāmaḥ) — "dark, black".
श्यामः (śyāmaḥ) is dark — black, but also the blue-black of a rain cloud and the deep green of standing water.
It is the darkness of a thundercloud rather than of soot, which is why the god whose name means "the dark one" is also called Śyām with affection.
No European cousin has been agreed. The word stayed home, and Śyām and Śyāmā are ordinary names in India today.

What you've built.
Dark, with a rain cloud in it rather than ash.

Lesson 3 of 5.
पीतः (pītaḥ) — yellow.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new word: what did śyāmaḥ mean?

You'll want to know: पीतः.
पीतः (pītaḥ) — "yellow".
पीतः (pītaḥ) is yellow — turmeric, ripe grain, a marigold, the silk a bridegroom wears.
Its older force is "made yellow", so a पीतम् वस्त्रम् is a cloth that has been dyed rather than one that grew that colour.
Hindi pīlā is the same word with an ending on it, and it is the yellow of every wall and every taxi.

What you've built.
Yellow, understood as a thing that was dyed.

Lesson 4 of 5.
हरितः (haritaḥ) — green.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new word: what did pītaḥ mean?

You'll want to know: हरितः.
हरितः (haritaḥ) — "green".
हरितः (haritaḥ) is green — and before it settled it covered yellow-green and gold as well.
English yellow and gold are its cousins, from ǵhel-, a root that runs across the whole band from green to yellow. One family, one word, two colours pulled apart by different daughters.
तृणम् is हरितम्, and so is a young पत्रम् on a तरुः.

What you've built.
Green, from a root that could not decide between green and gold.

Lesson 5 of 5.
नीलः (nīlaḥ) — blue.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the last word of the chapter: what did pītaḥ mean, and what did haritaḥ mean?

You'll want to know: नीलः.
नीलः (nīlaḥ) — "blue".
नीलः (nīlaḥ) is blue, and dark blue at that — the blue of the समुद्रः rather than of the आकाशः at noon.
The indigo plant is nīlī, and when Europe began buying that dye the word travelled with the cargo: Arabic nīl, Portuguese anil, and the aniline English still uses for the dyes that replaced it.
So a Sanskrit colour word is sitting inside a modern chemistry term, carried there by trade.

What you've built.
Five colours: white, dark, yellow, green and blue.
