German, chapter 453: Berge und Täler.
6 lessons. All 6 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 6.
die Klippe — the cliff; the rock (in the sea).

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the German for to deliver, then the German for to exchange.

You'll want to know: die Klippe.
die Klippe — "the cliff; the rock (in the sea)".
Von der Klippe sieht man weit über das Meer. From the cliff you can see far out over the sea.
Plural: die Klippen.

The word, taken apart.
Klippe goes back to medieval German klippe 'rock, cliff' (EtymDB) and came into the standard language from Low German; Dutch klip 'cliff' is a cousin.

What you've built.
One more word for telling, explaining and describing at B1.

Lesson 3 of 6.

Warm-up.

You'll want to know: der Gipfel.
der Gipfel — "the summit, the peak; a summit meeting".
Nach vier Stunden sind wir auf dem Gipfel. After four hours we are at the summit.
Same form in the plural. Also a political summit.

The word, taken apart.
The origin of Gipfel is not settled. EtymDB lists the word with the sense 'summit' but gives no source.

Lesson 4 of 6.
der Abhang — the slope, the hillside.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the German for the plateau, then the German for the summit.

You'll want to know: der Abhang.
der Abhang — "the slope, the hillside".
Am steilen Abhang wachsen nur kleine Bäume. Only small trees grow on the steep slope.
Plural: die Abhänge. Also der Hang.

The word, taken apart.
Abhang is built from ab 'down' and Hang 'slope', related to hängen 'to hang' (EtymDB lists Abhang): ground that hangs downwards.

Lesson 5 of 6.
die Schlucht — the gorge, the ravine.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the German for the summit, then the German for the slope.

You'll want to know: die Schlucht.
die Schlucht — "the gorge, the ravine".
Unten in der tiefen Schlucht ist ein kleiner Fluss. Down in the deep gorge there is a small river.
Plural: die Schluchten.

The word, taken apart.
Schlucht came into the standard language from Low German; it is usually linked to schlucken 'to swallow', and EtymDB lists Old Norse slok 'gully, channel' as a cousin.
