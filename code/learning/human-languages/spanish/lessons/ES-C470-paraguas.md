# el paraguas — the umbrella

<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C330-WAY-12, ES-LEX-C440-FIESTA-02] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say **la parada** and **el cumpleaños**. One lends this word its
first half, the other lends it its shape.

## You'll want to know: el paraguas
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-C470-PARAGUAS-01]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C440-FIESTA-02] -->

**el paraguas** — the umbrella.

> Me he dejado el **paraguas** en el ascensor.

Look at it before you are told anything: **para** plus **aguas**. A verb and a
plural noun fused into one word, which is the move *el cumpleaños* taught you
— *cumple* plus *años*, a sentence that hardened.

And like *cumpleaños*, it wears a plural **-s** and is singular: **el**
paraguas, one umbrella.

## Grammar Lens: the plural that does not move
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C470-PARAGUAS-01] -->

So what is the plural? Nothing changes.

| one | more than one |
|---|---|
| **el** paraguas | **los** paraguas |

This is not a quirk of this word. [El análisis](./ES-C447-analisis.md) gave you
the rule: a word already ending in **s**, with its stress somewhere other than
the last syllable, takes no plural ending. *El lunes* / *los lunes* works the
same way.

The article is doing all the counting. Drop it and the sentence cannot tell you
how many umbrellas there are.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C470-PARAGUAS-01, ES-LEX-C330-WAY-12] -->

The **para-** is the one *la parada* already unpacked: Latin **parare**, to
**make ready** — and *parada* reminded you it is not the Greek *para-* of
*parallel*. A **parasol** is made ready against the sun, a **parachute**
against a fall. A **paraguas** is made ready against the waters.

Which is worth a second look, because English named the same object from the
opposite direction:

| language | what the word notices |
|---|---|
| Spanish **paraguas** | the **water** it keeps off |
| English **umbrella** | the **shade** it makes |

*Umbrella* is Latin *umbra*, a shadow, with a diminutive on it — a little
shade, and English took it from Italian. So one language named the object for
the shade it casts and the other for the water it turns away, and both are
describing the same thing held over the same head.
