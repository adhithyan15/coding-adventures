Lesson 1 of 5.
nūper — recently.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for which, then the Latin for and not.

You'll want to know: nūper.
nūper — "recently". Latin's past tense, the perfect, has its own endings: vīdī — "I saw", vēnī — "I came". Nūper librum lēgī. — "I recently read a book."

Lesson 2 of 5.
nudiustertius — the day before yesterday.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for and not, then the Latin for recently.

You'll want to know: nudiustertius.
nudiustertius — "the day before yesterday". It is nunc diēs tertius, "now (is) the third day": counting today, yesterday is the second. Nudiustertius epistulam scrīpsī. — "The day before yesterday I wrote a letter."

Lesson 3 of 5.
ōlim — once, long ago; one day.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for recently, then the Latin for the day before yesterday.

You'll want to know: ōlim.
ōlim — "once, long ago; one day". It points to a time far off, usually long past: Ōlim Rōma parva erat. — "Long ago Rome was small." erat is "was", from sum.

Lesson 4 of 5.
anteā — before, previously.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for the day before yesterday, then the Latin for once.

You'll want to know: anteā.
anteā — "before, previously". It is ante, "before", with eā. Anteā Rōmae habitāvī. — "Before, I lived in Rome." habitāvī is the perfect of habitō.

Lesson 5 of 5.
accidō, accidere — to happen.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for once, then the Latin for before.

You'll want to know: accidō, accidere.
accidō, accidere — "to happen". Quid accidit? — "What happened?" Its perfect is spelled like its present in this form: accidit. It is ad + cadō, "to fall towards", the source of English accident.
