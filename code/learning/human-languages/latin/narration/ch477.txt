Latin, chapter 477: Livestock, Flock, Herd, To pasture, Pasture.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
pecus, pecoris — livestock, cattle, a herd of animals.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a stalk, then the Latin for a granary.

You'll want to know: pecus, pecoris.
pecus, pecoris — "livestock, cattle, a herd of animals".
Agricola multum pecus habet: ovēs, caprās, bovēs. The farmer has a lot of livestock: sheep, goats, oxen.
Third declension, neuter: pecus, pecoris n., the animals as a group. Another word pecus, pecudis f. is one beast. Pecūnia 'money' comes from the same root: wealth was once counted in cattle.

The word, taken apart.
pecus is inherited from PIE peḱu- 'livestock' (EtymDB); Lewis & Short list Gothic faihu, German Vieh and English fee as cousins. pecūnia 'money' is built on the same root (Lewis & Short).

Lesson 2 of 5.
grex, gregis — a flock, a herd; a band of people.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a granary, then the Latin for livestock.

You'll want to know: grex, gregis.
grex, gregis — "a flock, a herd; a band of people".
Pastor gregem ovium ad flūmen dūcit. The shepherd leads the flock of sheep to the river.
Third declension, masculine: grex, gregis m. Also a group of people. English gregarious, congregate and egregious (out of the flock) come from it.

The word, taken apart.
grex is inherited from PIE h₂ger- 'to assemble, to gather' (EtymDB). English gregarious, congregate and egregious come from it.

Lesson 3 of 5.
armentum, armentī — a herd (of cattle or horses).

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for livestock, then the Latin for a flock.

You'll want to know: armentum, armentī.
armentum, armentī — "a herd (of cattle or horses)".
Armenta bovum in campīs sunt. Herds of cattle are on the plains.
Second declension, neuter: armentum, armentī n. Large animals, cattle and horses; grex goes with sheep and goats.

The word, taken apart.
armentum is usually linked with arō 'to plough', since Lewis & Short give its first sense as cattle for ploughing; EtymDB connects it with arma instead, so the origin is not settled.

Lesson 4 of 5.
pāscō, pāscere — to pasture, to put out to graze; to feed.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a flock, then the Latin for a herd.

You'll want to know: pāscō, pāscere.
pāscō, pāscere — "to pasture, to put out to graze; to feed".
Puer caprās in colle pāscit. The boy pastures the goats on the hill.
pāscō, pāscere, pāvī, pāstum. Passive pāscor, 'to graze': ovēs pāscuntur. Pāstor 'shepherd' comes from it.

The word, taken apart.
pāscō is inherited from PIE peh₂- 'to protect, to feed' (EtymDB); pāstor 'shepherd' and pābulum 'fodder' come from the same root (Lewis & Short). English food is a Germanic cousin.

Lesson 5 of 5.
pāscuum, pāscuī — a pasture, grazing land.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a herd, then the Latin for to pasture.

You'll want to know: pāscuum, pāscuī.
pāscuum, pāscuī — "a pasture, grazing land".
Vēre pecus in pāscua dūcitur. In spring the livestock is taken to the pastures.
Second declension, neuter: pāscuum, pāscuī n., mostly plural. The neuter of the adjective pāscuus 'for grazing'.

The word, taken apart.
pāscuum is the neuter of the adjective pāscuus 'for grazing', built on pāscō 'to pasture' (Lewis & Short).
