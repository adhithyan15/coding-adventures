# el circo — the ring, and the two c's in one word

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C420-ARTS-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] *Circo* has two **c**'s and they are not the same sound. Before
reading on, say the word and listen to which one is soft.

## Sounds you'll need
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[] -->

- `ci-th-s` — the first **c**, before **i**, is soft. →
  [reference](../pronunciation-reference.md)
- `c-hard` — the second **c**, before **o**, is hard, as in *cat*.
- `stress-penultimate` — *CIR-co*.

One word, one letter, two jobs. The vowel that follows decides, every time.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-C420-ARTS-03]; assesses=[] -->

**el circo** — "the circus." Latin *circus*, "a ring," borrowed from Greek
*kírkos*. The Romans' *Circus Maximus* was a ring you raced around; the tent
came much later and kept the shape and the name.

The family is large and all of it is round:

- **circ**le, **circ**uit, **circ**ular, **circ**umference.
- **circ**a — "around" a date.
- Spanish *cerca* ("near") and *cercar* ("to surround") come from the same ring.

So a circus is not named for clowns or animals. It is named for **the shape of
the floor**.

## Grammar Lens: the rule you can see inside one word
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C420-ARTS-03, ES-LEX-C420-ARTS-01, ES-LEX-C420-ARTS-02] -->

Most words make you check the letter against the vowel that follows it.
*Circo* does the checking for you, because it contains both cases:

| letter | followed by | sound |
|---|---|---|
| **c**ir- | i | soft |
| -**c**o | o | hard |

Keep this word as your reference. Whenever you are unsure how a **c** should
sound, say *circo* and listen to which half matches.
