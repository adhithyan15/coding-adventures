# … है ना? — "isn't it?"

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[HI-GRAMMAR-KYA-POLAR-01, HI-CONCEPT-C07-NAHIN-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] **क्या** at the front asks something you do not know. Often you DO
know, and want it confirmed. Different move, different word, other end.

## Grammar Lens: hanging a check on the end
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[HI-JOIN-TAG-NA-01]; assesses=[HI-CONCEPT-C37-CHAI-01, HI-CONCEPT-C03-THIK-01, HI-CONCEPT-C07-HAAN-01] -->

> **चाय ठीक है ना?** — *chāy ṭhīk hai nā?* — **The tea is fine, isn't it?**

Take any statement you can make, put **ना** on the end, and it becomes a check:
you say what you believe and invite the listener to agree. The answer comes back
as **हाँ** or **नहीं**.

Set the two questions side by side:

- **क्या चाय ठीक है?** — *is the tea fine?* — I do not know
- **चाय ठीक है ना?** — *the tea is fine, isn't it?* — I think so; agree with me

Use the tag when you mean the particle and you have claimed to know the answer
already.

One syllable buys a whole conversational turn: every sentence in this book is
now something you can offer for agreement. The tag is the old negative **न** used
as an invitation — a negative asking for a *yes*, exactly as English does in
*isn't it?* and Marathi with the same syllable.
