# el análisis — loosened apart

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C300-BODY-04, ES-LEX-MEDICO, ES-LEX-EXAMEN] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say **la sangre**, **el médico** and **el examen**. The doctor wants
to know what is in your blood. Name what he orders.

## You'll want to know: el análisis
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-C447-JUICIO-01]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C300-BODY-04] -->

**el análisis** — the analysis, and in a clinic **the test**.

*Un análisis de sangre* — a blood test. *Me han hecho unos análisis* — they have
run some tests on me. *Los resultados del análisis* — the test results.

This is the word on the form, and it is worth having before a waiting room
rather than during one.

## Grammar Lens: a noun that does not change
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C447-JUICIO-01, ES-LEX-EXAMEN] -->

**el análisis** and **los análisis** are spelled identically. Only the article
tells you how many.

Spanish does this to every noun ending in unstressed **-is**:

| one | more than one |
|---|---|
| el análisis | **los** análisis |
| la crisis | **las** crisis |
| el paréntesis | **los** paréntesis |

The reason is a rule you can use elsewhere: a word already ending in **s**, when
its stress is *not* on the last syllable, takes no plural ending. The same
applies to *el lunes* / *los lunes* and *el paraguas* / *los paraguas*.

Contrast **el examen**, which does change — *los exámenes* — and gains a written
accent when it does, because the stress must stay where it was.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C447-JUICIO-01] -->

Greek **análysis**: **ana-** up, through, plus **lýein**, **to loosen**.

To analyse something is to **loosen it apart** — to undo the knots and see what
the pieces are. That is a physical picture, and it is exactly what a blood test
does to blood.

The same *lýein* is in **paralysis**, a loosening of the body's control, and in
**catalyst**, which loosens a reaction. Its opposite in Greek was *synthesis*, a
putting-together — which is what a Spanish lesson's last section is called.
