# मुझे चाय चाहिए — the wanter is not the subject

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[HI-CONCEPT-C35-PASAND-01, HI-LEX-C95-NEED-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say *I like tea* in Hindi. Look at the first word you said, and keep
it in mind.

## You'll want to know: मुझे … चाहिए
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[HI-LEX-C95-NEED-02]; assesses=[] -->

**मुझे चाय चाहिए।** — I want tea.

Not **मैं**. **मुझे** — *to me*.

## The two words
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[HI-LEX-C95-NEED-02, HI-CONCEPT-C35-PASAND-01] -->

This is the liking sentence again, with one word swapped:

| Hindi | word for word |
|---|---|
| मुझे चाय पसंद है | to me, tea is pleasing |
| मुझे चाय चाहिए | to me, tea is wanted |

**The person is in the dative both times**, and the thing is what the sentence
is really about. Hindi treats wanting and liking as things that **happen to**
you rather than things you **do**, and puts the person on the receiving end of
both.

That is not a Hindi peculiarity. The proxy language reaches the same shape in
*me gusta*, and Italian in *mi piace* — three languages in two unrelated
families building the same idea the same way. What makes Hindi's version worth
a lesson is that **चाहिए** extends it to **wanting**, where those languages
switch back to an ordinary verb.

## Grammar Lens: what agrees with what
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[HI-LEX-C95-NEED-02, HI-SCRIPT-RECOG-133] -->

**चाहिए** does not change, so there is nothing to agree. The sentence has no
word that bends, which is unusual enough to be a useful landmark: **if you hear
चाहिए, stop looking for agreement.**

And **मुझे** carries **झ**, the breathy partner drawn a few chapters back. It
was in your mouth from the liking chapter onwards and only recently on your
hand.
