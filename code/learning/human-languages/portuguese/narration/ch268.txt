Portuguese, chapter 268: Would, Could, Should --- The Condicional, and Six Lines.
4 lessons. You can do the first 3 of them in the car; after that you will want to have stopped.


Lesson 1 of 4.
poderia, gostaria, deveria — the would of Portuguese.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say "I would like". (Gostaria.) Say "I ought to rest", the careful way. (Deveria descansar.)
[pause 1 second]
Two forms of one tense. This lesson gives it its name and its recipe.

Grammar Lens: the whole infinitive, plus -ia.
This is the condicional, Portuguese's "would". Keep the whole infinitive and add -ia for "I":
falar, falaria — I would speak comer, comeria — I would eat partir, partiria — I would leave.
The other persons change only the ending: falaria, falarias, falaria, falaríamos, falariam. Those are the imperfeito's -ia endings, but glued to the whole infinitive: comia, I was eating; comeria, I would eat.
Three common verbs squeeze their infinitive first: fazer gives faria, dizer diria, trazer traria. Poder, gostar and dever follow the plain recipe: poderia, gostaria, deveria.
Three jobs, all of them about softening. A polite request: Poderia ajudar-me? — Could you help me? A wish: Gostaria de viajar. And advice: No teu lugar, estudaria. — In your place, I'd study.
In everyday European speech the imperfeito often stands in for it, as queria did at the ticket window: podia ajudar-me?, gostava de viajar. Both are right. Notice also the me after the verb in ajudar-me; Brazil puts it first.

The word, taken apart: "I had to speak".
Latin had no would-tense. Spoken Latin made one by gluing a past of habēre, "to have", onto the infinitive: roughly "I had to speak", which slid into "I would speak". Portuguese and Spanish chose the imperfect of that verb, habēbat, the same past that became havia; worn down on the end of a verb, it is the -ia of falaria. Italian glued on the perfect instead, which is why its endings do not match. Neither EtymDB nor Lewis & Short covers a change this late; this is the usual explanation in histories of Romance, given as that. The name condicional is a learned borrowing from Medieval Latin conditiōnālis (EtymDB 2.1).

Lesson 2 of 4.
seria — it would be.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Build "I would speak". (Falaria.) Ask politely for help with poder. (Poderia ajudar-me?)

You'll want to know: seria.
seria — "it would be", "he or she would be".
ser refused the imperfeito's recipe and gave you era. Its condicional does not refuse: the whole infinitive plus -ia, seria, serias, seria, seríamos, seriam. Seria is the one you will use most, because it judges a situation rather than a person:
Seria difícil. — It would be difficult. Seria mais rápido de comboio. — It would be quicker by train.
De comboio, "by train", and de autocarro, "by bus", are how Portugal says how you travel.

Grammar Lens: seria bom.
Seria bom + infinitive — "it would be good to, it would be nice to":
Seria bom viajar juntos. — It would be nice to travel together.
That is a wish with the person left out, softer than gostaria and less private than espero. And it sits naturally after a depende, as the verdict on each branch:
Depende. De comboio seria mais rápido, mas de autocarro seria mais barato.

The word, taken apart.
ser is inherited from Latin sedēre, "to sit" (EtymDB 2.1), as chapter sixteen showed, and -ia is the worn Latin habēbat, "he had", of the last lesson. So seria began as something like "had to be". Neither source records forms this late, so that step is the usual explanation rather than a cited one.

Lesson 3 of 4.
Review — if this, then that would.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say "it would be difficult". (Seria difícil.) Then "I could". (Poderia.)

What you've built.
Two chapters, and a whole way of talking about what has not happened yet. A real branch: se chove, não saio. A branch not yet named: depende, or depende de ti. And the softened result, in the condicional: poderia, deveria, seria.
— Vamos ao parque amanhã? — Depende. Seria bom, mas se chover, poderíamos ir ao museu.
[pause 2 seconds]
Vamos is "shall we go", from ir, and poderíamos is "we could", the nós form of poderia. And listen to se chover: one occasion still to come, so the special form you met last chapter.

Lesson 4 of 4.
Six lines about a journey — six connected lines to one reader about a journey — a scene in the imperfeito, events in order, a reason, and a last line that looks forward.

Before we start: this one needs your hands, so it is not a driving lesson. You will want a pen and something to write on and your eyes or your hands for the steps that ask you to read, point, write or gesture. You can listen to everything else now. I will say so again when we reach it, but leave this section until you have stopped: Wrap-up recall.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
The writing chapters asked for four lines that held together. This one asks for six, and they tell a story.
[pause 1 second]
Pick one person who will read it: um amigo, uma amiga, someone in your family.

You'll want to know.
[pause 2 seconds]
A told story is not a list of events. It has a scene, in the imperfeito: era, havia, fazia frio, estava contente. It has events, in the perfeito, put in order with primeiro, depois and no fim. It has at least one reason, pointing back with porque or forward with por isso, and perhaps a turn with mas.
[pause 2 seconds]
And a good one ends by looking forward: espero …, or seria bom …, or a real condition with se.
[pause 2 seconds]
This book's B1 writing paper asks for a connected text of 130 to 170 words. Six lines is the first step towards it, not the whole of it.

Writing — connected composition.
[pause 2 seconds]
On paper, write six lines to your reader about a journey, real or invented, one line for each of these:
the scene: when it was, and the weather;
where you were, who was with you, and how long you stayed, with durante;
the first thing that happened, with primeiro;
the next thing, with depois, and a reason, with porque or por isso;
how it ended and how you felt, with no fim and estava;
one line forward, with espero, seria bom or se.
[pause 3 seconds]
Use only words you already write. The whole exercise is choosing the right past for each line, and joining them.

[once you have stopped driving — this part needs your eyes or your hands; if you are driving, skip ahead to the next part]
Wrap-up recall.
[pause 2 seconds]
Now read your lines as you read the journey in chapter two hundred and sixty. Mark every verb C for cena, the scene, or E for evento. Lines one and five should be mostly C; three and four mostly E. A line about the weather marked E is the commonest slip: change choveu back to chovia.
[pause 2 seconds]
Then two spelling checks. The nós form of the perfeito carries an accent in Portugal, chegámos, esperámos, and so does the imperfeito's, estávamos, tínhamos. And every til stays on: não, estação.
[pause 2 seconds]
Last, read line six alone. If it has a se, is the verb after it in the plain present, or in the special form for one occasion still to come? If it has seria, is it your verdict on something that has not happened yet? Then it looks forward, and the story is finished.
