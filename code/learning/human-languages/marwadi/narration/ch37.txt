Marwadi (Marwari), chapter 37: Eleven to Fifteen.
12 lessons. The first lesson already needs your eyes or your hands, so save this one for when you have stopped.


Lesson 1 of 12.
इ — the fourth standing vowel.

Before we start: this one needs your hands, so it is not a driving lesson. You will want a pen and something to write on, your eyes, for letter shapes on the page and your eyes, because the lesson points at something written down. You can listen to everything else now — leave the section called Script — the mark, standing on its own until you have stopped, and I will say so again when we reach it.

Warm-up.
[pause 14 seconds]
Say the line that closes a bargain — paisā le lo — and write the ि mark on any consonant you like.

Script — the mark, standing on its own.
इ — i — the vowel with no consonant under it.
Since chapter 7 the hand has made ि, the hook that leans in front of the consonant it is read after. A vowel that opens a word has nothing to lean on, so Devanagari gives it a letter that stands on the line by itself.
आ was isolated in chapter 2, ई in chapter 6, ए in chapter 32 for the first number. इ is the fourth, and eleven is what needs it.

Guided Practice.
Trace इ three times. Write ि beside it and say which of the two can begin a word on its own. Then look at ई and mark the one stroke that separates the two standing letters.

Wrap-up Recall.
This is the first of the four signs the teens will ask for.

You'll want to know.
igyārā — eleven.
The count so far stopped at das, ten, and jumped to bīs, twenty. This is the first of the nine words that fill the gap.
Say it in three beats: i-gyā-rā. The middle beat carries a gy cluster that has to stay one sound, not two — the tongue is already at the palate for the y while the g is still closing.

Lesson 3 of 12.
इग्यारा (igyārā) — eleven on the page.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, because the lesson points at something written down. You can listen to all of it now and come back to the parts that need looking at once you have stopped.

Warm-up.
[pause 12 seconds]
[your turn — recall: say igyārā aloud before you look at it — R1]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

You'll want to know.
इ + ग्या + रा becomes इग्यारा — igyārā — eleven.
Three pieces, and only the first is new. ग्या is ग with its upright stem cut away and या joined on behind it — the same virama ् you have been writing since chapter 4, doing the job it exists for: two consonants, one breath, no vowel between them.
रा is chapter 1's र with the ा you have written more often than any other mark in this book.

Guided Practice.
Copy इग्यारा (igyārā) once while looking. Then write the three pieces apart — इ, ग्या, रा — and point at the stem that ग gave up.

You'll want to know.
bārā — twelve.
Two is do. Twelve is bārā, and there is no do in it — the b is what is left of an older word for two that Marwari kept only inside the teens. English does the same thing with two and twelve, where the tw- survives and the rest does not.
The ending -ārā is the one igyārā had. From here to nineteen you will hear it again and again: the teens are a set that rhymes.

You'll want to know.
बा + रा becomes बारा — bārā — twelve.
Two pieces, no new sign, and both halves take the same ा. ब came in with bīs; र has been in the hand since the first greeting.
Written beside दो, the point the ear made is visible: twelve and two share one letter and nothing else.

You'll want to know.
terā — thirteen.
Three is tīn. Thirteen is terā: the t stays, the long ī flattens to e, and the teens ending arrives. Two beats, both short of effort — te-rā.
You have now heard the pattern three times, and it is the pattern rather than the words that is worth holding: a consonant off the small number, then -ārā or -erā.

You'll want to know.
caudā — fourteen.
Four is chār. Fourteen keeps the ch and then does something none of the others do: it ends -dā, not -rā.
That is worth marking rather than smoothing over. Three teens in a row have rhymed and the fourth breaks the rhyme, so this is the one in the set most likely to be misremembered as chaurā. It is not.
The au is the diphthong of mausam, the weather word from chapter 16 — the mouth already knows it.
