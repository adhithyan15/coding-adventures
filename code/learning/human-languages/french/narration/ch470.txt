French, chapter 470: Quelle frayeur !.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
la frayeur — fright, scare.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the French for to annoy, then the French for to irritate.

You'll want to know: la frayeur.
la frayeur — "fright, scare".
Quelle frayeur! J'ai cru que j'avais perdu mon passeport. What a fright! I thought I had lost my passport.
Stronger than la peur, and over quickly.

The word, taken apart.
la frayeur is inherited from Old French freor (EtymDB), from Latin fragor "a crash, a din", built on frangere "to break" (Lewis & Short); its form was later drawn towards effrayer.

Lesson 2 of 5.
effrayer — to frighten, to scare.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the French for to irritate, then the French for fright.

You'll want to know: effrayer.
effrayer — "to frighten, to scare".
L'orage a effrayé le chien toute la nuit. The storm frightened the dog all night.
Être effrayé — to be frightened. Effrayant — frightening.

The word, taken apart.
effrayer comes from Old French esfreer "to disturb, to take the peace away" (EtymDB), built on es- "out" and freer "to make peace", which is usually traced to Frankish fridu "peace".

Lesson 3 of 5.
sursauter — to jump, to start (with surprise).

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the French for fright, then the French for to frighten.

You'll want to know: sursauter.
sursauter — "to jump, to start (with surprise)".
J'ai sursauté quand la porte a claqué. I jumped when the door slammed.
A sudden jump of the body, from surprise or fear.

The word, taken apart.
sursauter is built from sur and sauter "to jump", which is inherited from Latin saltāre "to dance, to leap" (Lewis & Short).

Lesson 4 of 5.
le soulagement — relief.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the French for to frighten, then the French for to jump.

You'll want to know: le soulagement.
le soulagement — "relief".
Quel soulagement! Ma fille est bien arrivée. What a relief! My daughter has arrived safely.
The verb is soulager "to relieve"; je suis soulagé — I am relieved.

The word, taken apart.
le soulagement is built on soulager "to relieve", which comes from Latin sublevāre "to lift up, to lighten" (Lewis & Short), from levāre "to raise".

The word, taken apart.
rassurer comes from Old French rasseürer (EtymDB), built from re- and asseürer "to make safe", which goes back to Latin sēcūrus "free from care" (Lewis & Short).
