Latin, chapter 464: Steelyard, Pair of scales, Scale pan, To weigh out, To weigh carefully.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
statēra, statērae — a steelyard, a balance with one arm.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a wicker basket, then the Latin for a bread basket.

You'll want to know: statēra, statērae.
statēra, statērae — "a steelyard, a balance with one arm".
Lanius carnem in statērā pōnit. The butcher puts the meat on the steelyard.
First declension, feminine: statēra, statērae f. A beam hung from a hook, with a weight slid along one arm.

The word, taken apart.
statēra is usually traced to Greek statēr, a weight and a coin, built on histēmi 'to make stand, to weigh' (EtymDB). Lewis & Short compare Greek stathmē 'rule, balance' instead, so the exact source is not settled.

Lesson 2 of 5.
trutina, trutinae — a pair of scales, a balance.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a bread basket, then the Latin for a steelyard.

You'll want to know: trutina, trutinae.
trutina, trutinae — "a pair of scales, a balance".
Mercātor aurum in trutinā dīligenter pōnit. The merchant carefully puts the gold on the scales.
First declension, feminine: trutina, trutinae f. Also figurative: weighing a thing in the mind.

The word, taken apart.
trutina was borrowed from Greek trytanē 'balance, tongue of a balance' (Lewis & Short). EtymDB lists it without a parent.

Lesson 3 of 5.
lanx, lancis — a scale pan; a dish, a platter.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a steelyard, then the Latin for a pair of scales.

You'll want to know: lanx, lancis.
lanx, lancis — "a scale pan; a dish, a platter".
In ūnā lance merx est, in alterā pondus. In one scale pan there are the goods, in the other the weight.
Third declension, feminine: lanx, lancis f. English balance comes, through French, from bilanx 'having two pans'.

The word, taken apart.
lanx is inherited; Lewis & Short and EtymDB compare Greek lekos 'dish, pan' as a cousin. English balance comes, through French, from Late Latin bilanx 'with two scale pans'.

Lesson 4 of 5.
appendō, appendere — to weigh out; to hang on.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a pair of scales, then the Latin for a scale pan.

You'll want to know: appendō, appendere.
appendō, appendere — "to weigh out; to hang on".
Pistor pānem appendit et pretium dīcit. The baker weighs out the bread and says the price.
appendō, appendere, appendī, appēnsum. Money was once weighed, so appendere can also mean to pay. English append and appendix come from it.

The word, taken apart.
appendō is built from ad- 'to' and pendō 'to hang, to weigh' (EtymDB; Lewis & Short). English append and appendix come from it.

Lesson 5 of 5.
pēnsō, pēnsāre — to weigh carefully, to weigh out; to ponder.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a scale pan, then the Latin for to weigh out.

You'll want to know: pēnsō, pēnsāre.
pēnsō, pēnsāre — "to weigh carefully, to weigh out; to ponder".
Mercātor prūdēns mercem pēnsat. A careful merchant weighs the goods.
pēnsō, pēnsāre, pēnsāvī, pēnsātum, a frequentative of pendō. English pensive comes from it through French penser 'to think'.

The word, taken apart.
pēnsō is a frequentative of pendō 'to weigh', made from its participle pēnsus (Lewis & Short). English pensive comes from it through French penser 'to think, to weigh in the mind'.
