Latin, chapter 307: Venēnum.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
venēnum, venēnī — poison; a drug.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a dagger, then the Latin for to kill.

You'll want to know: venēnum, venēnī.
venēnum, venēnī — "poison; a drug".
Medicus venēnum in pōculō invēnit. The doctor found poison in the cup.
Neuter. First any strong drug or charm, then poison. English venom comes from it through French.

The word, taken apart.
venēnum is inherited; EtymDB traces it to Proto-Italic wenesnom, from a root meaning "to desire", so first a love potion. English venom comes from it through French.

Lesson 2 of 5.
venēficus, venēficī — a poisoner; a sorcerer.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to kill, then the Latin for poison.

You'll want to know: venēficus, venēficī.
venēficus, venēficī — "a poisoner; a sorcerer".
Venēficus herbās perīculōsās in hortō alēbat. The poisoner grew dangerous herbs in his garden.
Masculine; the feminine venēfica is the witch of Roman stories.

The word, taken apart.
venēficus joins venēnum, "poison", and faciō, "to make" (EtymDB, Lewis & Short): a maker of poisons and charms.

Lesson 3 of 5.
īnsidiae, īnsidiārum — an ambush; a plot.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for poison, then the Latin for a poisoner.

You'll want to know: īnsidiae, īnsidiārum.
īnsidiae, īnsidiārum — "an ambush; a plot".
Hostēs in silvā īnsidiās parāvērunt. The enemy prepared an ambush in the forest.
Feminine, plural only: men sitting hidden, waiting. English insidious.

The word, taken apart.
īnsidiae is built on īnsideō, "to sit in, to occupy" (Lewis & Short), from in and sedeō, "to sit": a lying in wait. English insidious comes from it.

Lesson 4 of 5.
coniūrātiō, coniūrātiōnis — a conspiracy.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a poisoner, then the Latin for an ambush.

You'll want to know: coniūrātiō, coniūrātiōnis.
coniūrātiō, coniūrātiōnis — "a conspiracy".
Cōnsul coniūrātiōnem in senātū patefēcit. The consul exposed the conspiracy in the senate.
Feminine, 3rd declension. Cicero brought one to light in 63 BC.

The word, taken apart.
coniūrātiō is built on coniūrō, "to swear together", from con- and iūrō, "to swear" (Lewis & Short): a band bound by a shared oath.

The word, taken apart.
