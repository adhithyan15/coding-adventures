# auf — "on, upon," and it is English *up*

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GE-LEX-WIEDER-01, GE-LEX-SEHEN-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Two pieces down, one to go. The formal goodbye opens with a
three-letter word that turns out to be one you say constantly in English.

## You'll want to know: auf
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[GE-LEX-AUF-01]; assesses=[] -->

> **auf** — "on, upon."

## Sounds you'll need
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[GE-SOUND-AUF-01]; assesses=[GE-LEX-AUF-01] -->

- `au-diphthong` — German **au** is the *ow* of English *house*, never the *aw*
  of *caught*. So *auf* rhymes with English **owf**, not with *oaf*.
- `f-final` — the **f** is crisp and unvoiced at the end.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[GE-ETYMON-AUF-UP-02]; assesses=[GE-LEX-AUF-01] -->

**auf** is English **up**. The vowel drifted — German widened it to *au*, English
kept it short — but it is one word, and the meanings still overlap almost
exactly:

| German | English |
|---|---|
| *auf* | up, upon, on |
| *aufstehen* | to stand **up** |
| *aufmachen* | to make **up**, to open |

English **upon** is literally *up* + *on*, which is the same doubling German
gets from *auf* alone. That is why *auf* translates sometimes as "up" and
sometimes as "on": English split the job into two words and German never did.
