Spanish, chapter 462: La reunión.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
reducir — to bring it down.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say la ducha and el coste. A shower is about to turn out to be a relative of the verb coming next.

You'll want to know: reducir.
reducir — to reduce, to cut back.
Reducir costes — to cut costs. Reducir la velocidad — to slow down, the wording on a road sign. Han reducido la plantilla — they've cut the staff.
La reducción is the noun, and reducido on a price tag means marked down.

Grammar Lens: the -zc- verbs.
Reducir belongs to a group whose first person gains a c:
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
verb: reducir. I …: reduzco.
verb: conducir. I …: conduzco.
verb: conocer. I …: conozco.
Everything else is regular — reduces, reduce, reducimos. Only the form ending in -o takes the extra consonant, and it does so because a Latin sound sat there and survived in exactly that one slot.
If you have ever heard somebody say conozco and wondered where the z came from, this is the same group, and there are perhaps forty of them.

The word, taken apart.
Latin reducere: re-, back, plus ducere, to lead. To reduce something is to lead it back — to a smaller size, to where it was.
Ducere is everywhere once you see it. La ducha is the same root through French douche and Italian doccia: a pipe that leads water. English has conduct, produce, educate (to lead out), duke — a leader — and aqueduct, which leads water exactly as a shower does.
Spanish keeps conducir, to drive, and el conductor. A driver leads the car; a shower leads the water; reducing leads the number back down.

Lesson 2 of 5.
decidirse — to make your mind up.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say decidir and acercar. You already know what putting -se on the second one did. Do it to the first.

You'll want to know: decidirse.
decidirse — to make up your mind.
No me decido — I can't decide. Decídete — make your mind up. Se decidió por el azul — she went for the blue one.
The -se version takes por for the thing chosen: me decidí por el piso pequeño.

Grammar Lens: what the -se adds here.
You have met the -se switch three times, and this is a fourth use of it — not a new meaning, but a new shade:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
without -se: acercar — bring it closer. with -se: acercarse — come closer.
without -se: decidir — settle a matter. with -se: decidirse — settle yourself.
He decidido vender el coche states the decision. Me he decidido a vender el coche says you struggled and got there. The reflexive adds the effort, the sense of having had to bring yourself to it.
That is why no me decido is the natural way to say you are dithering, and no decido would sound like a job description.

The word, taken apart.
Latin decidere: de-, off, plus caedere, to cut. To decide is to cut off — the other options, the deliberating, the retreat.
That picture explains the English family exactly. A decision cuts things off. Something decisive cuts cleanly. And caedere is also in concise (cut short), precise (cut in front, trimmed to the edge), and — through the same cutting — homicide and suicide.
Spanish has decidir, la decisión, conciso and preciso off the same root. When you make your mind up, a Latin speaker heard you reaching for a blade.

Lesson 3 of 5.
el jefe — the one at the head.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say la cabeza and el trabajo. Put the first at the top of the second and you have named somebody.

You'll want to know: el jefe.
el jefe — the boss, the head of something.
Hablar con el jefe — to speak to the boss. La jefa de personal — the head of HR. El jefe de estación — the stationmaster.
The feminine is la jefa, formed normally, and both are ordinary words at work — not slang and not deferential.

Grammar Lens: the j that used to be something else.
Jefe came from French chef, and the ch became a j. Spanish did that to a whole shelf of borrowings:
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
French: chef. Spanish: el jefe.
French: jardin. Spanish: el jardín.
French: jamais. Spanish: —.
The j is the jota, the sound at the back of the throat, and it is a latecomer: medieval Spanish did not have it. Words spelled with j or with g before e and i all shifted to it at roughly the same time, a few hundred years ago, which is why jefe, mujer and gente now share a sound that none of them started with.

The word, taken apart.
French chef is Latin caput, head — and caput is the same word inside la cabeza.
So el jefe and la cabeza are one Latin noun arriving twice: once worn down through Spanish speech into the body part, once borrowed from French as the person in charge. A boss is literally a head, exactly as in English head of department.
The family is large: el capítulo (a chapter, a little head), la capital (the head city), acabar — to finish, to bring to a head — and English captain, chief, chef, capital and cape. A chef is the head of a kitchen, and chief and jefe are the same word in two languages.

Lesson 4 of 5.
(reducir, decidirse, el jefe) — from cold.

Warm-up.
[pause 3 seconds]
Close the lessons before this one. Say all three: to cut back, to make your mind up, the boss.

Grammar Lens: one word, two roads, four hundred years apart.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
the road: worn down through Spanish speech. the word: la cabeza.
the road: borrowed from French, much later. the word: el jefe.
Both are Latin caput, head. The first went through centuries of mouths and came out as a body part; the second was picked up whole from a neighbour and came out as a person.
You have watched this twice before — rueda against rotativo, consejo against consultar — but those pairs both came from Latin. This one arrived through a third language, which is why it looks like neither.

Grammar Lens: two verbs, two pictures of cutting and leading.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
verb: reducir. the picture inside it: re-ducere, to lead back.
verb: decidirse. the picture inside it: de-caedere, to cut off.
Deciding cuts the other options away; reducing leads the number back down. Both are physical, and both are still doing the work: la ducha leads water, preciso is cut to the edge.
And decidirse is the fourth -se pair you have met, after mudarse, acercarse and arreglarse. Here the -se adds effort — no me decido is dithering, where no decido would be a job description.

Lesson 5 of 5.
(el correo del jefe) — read it, then do it yourself.

Warm-up.

Say el jefe and reducir. The message below is from one about the other.

Guided Practice.
An internal message, and one reply. Read both, then answer.
1 — de la jefa.
Buenos días. Tenemos que reducir el gasto de material este trimestre. Antes del viernes, decidme qué se puede dejar de comprar en vuestra sección.

Lo miro hoy. Todavía no me decido entre quitar el papel de color o bajar los pedidos de tinta, así que te digo mañana.

Why it's said this way.
No me decido entre is the phrase to keep whole. With entre you name the options you are stuck between; with por you name the one you finally take. No me decido entre A y B, then me decidí por A.
Decidme is vosotros, plural and informal — a boss writing to her own team in Spain. A Latin American writer would say díganme, and a formal Spanish notice to the public would too. The verb ending places the writer, the reader and the register in one word.
And tenemos que reducir: nosotros, although the decision is hers. Spanish management writing does this constantly, and it is doing the same softening as the se on a shop notice — spreading the action out so nobody is singled out as its author.

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Now your turn, out loud and then in writing. Four lines: as a boss, say spending has to be cut and ask for suggestions by Friday; then as the reply, say you are torn between two options and will answer tomorrow.
Then check yourself: did you use entre for the options and por for the choice?
