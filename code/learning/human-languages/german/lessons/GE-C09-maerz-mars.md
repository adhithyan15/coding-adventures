# März and Dienstag

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GE-LEX-MAERZ-01, GE-LEX-DIENSTAG-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Two German words you now own. They come from the same god, and
neither of them looks like the other.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[GE-ETYMON-MAERZ-MARS-01]; assesses=[GE-ETYMON-DIENSTAG-TIW-01] -->

**Mars** entered German **twice**, and by two different routes.

| the route | what German got | result |
|---|---|---|
| his **name**, borrowed whole | Latin *Martius* | *März* |
| his **job**, given to Tiw | Germanic *Tiwaz* | *Dienstag* |

When the Romans met the Germanic gods, they matched them up by what they did:
the war-god here answers the war-god there. Tuesday was Mars's day, so it became
Tiw's day, and it still is — in German *Dienstag*, in English **Tuesday**.

The month got no such treatment. Nobody translated *Martius*; German took it as
it stood.

## Why it's said this way
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GE-ETYMON-MAERZ-MARS-01, GE-GRAMMAR-NATIVE-VS-LOAN-01] -->

The difference is worth holding onto, because it is the whole chapter in one
example.

The **week** was a thing Germanic speakers already had, so when the Roman scheme
arrived they mapped it onto their own gods and kept their own words. The
**calendar of twelve months** was not; it came in as an administrative object,
name-plates and all, and German took the name-plates.

Same god, same century, two outcomes — decided by whether German already had
something to put in the slot.
