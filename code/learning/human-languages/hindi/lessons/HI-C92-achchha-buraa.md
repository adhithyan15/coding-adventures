# अच्छा / बुरा — well and badly, from words you already own

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[HI-LEX-C41-ADJ-03, HI-LEX-C83-CHAR-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] You have **अच्छा** for *good* and **बुरा** for *bad*. English needs
two different words to say *well* and *badly*. Guess what Hindi needs.

## You'll want to know: the manner use
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[HI-LEX-C92-DEG-02]; assesses=[] -->

**अच्छा** is also *well*. **बुरा** is also *badly*.

**वह अच्छा बोलता है।** — He speaks well.
**वह बुरा बोलता है।** — He speaks badly.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[HI-LEX-C92-DEG-02, HI-SCRIPT-RECOG-130] -->

English keeps two shelves of words — *good / well*, *bad / badly* — and puts
**-ly** on the second shelf. Hindi keeps one. The same word describes a thing or
describes an action, and its position in the sentence says which:

| where it stands | what it describes |
|---|---|
| beside a noun | the thing — a **good** book |
| in front of a verb | the action — speaks **well** |

So *he is good* and *he speaks well* use the same word in Hindi, and an English
speaker's instinct to reach for a second form is the thing to unlearn.

**अच्छा** is also where you can now read the letter **छ**, which arrived a
moment ago in the alphabet. Find it in the middle of the word.

## Grammar Lens: it still agrees
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[HI-LEX-C92-DEG-02, HI-CONCEPT-C05-BOLTAHUN-01] -->

| speaker | sentence |
|---|---|
| a man | वह अच्छा बोलता है। |
| a woman | वह अच्छी बोलती है। |

Even doing the work of an English adverb, it is still an adjective, so it still
bends. English adverbs never do, which is the second half of the same instinct
to unlearn.

The ordinary spoken way to say *well* is also **अच्छी तरह**, "in a good manner"
— useful to recognise, and the bare adjective is enough to produce.
