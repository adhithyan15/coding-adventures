# los símbolos — the third kind of short form, and the one with no point

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-ORTH-C426-03, ES-ORTH-C426-05] -->

[PAUSE 2s] An abbreviation ends in a point and a sigla does not. Say both rules.
This lesson adds a third kind, and it sides with the sigla.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-ORTH-C426-06]; assesses=[] -->

**un símbolo** — a fixed sign for a unit, a currency or a direction. It is
**international, unchanging, and takes no point**.

| symbol | meaning |
|---|---|
| € | the euro |
| km | kilometre |
| kg | kilogram |
| h | hour |

*km* is never *km.* and never *kms*. A symbol has no plural and no point,
because it is not a shortened Spanish word — it is the same sign in every
language.

You have already read one in use: **km/h**, which you learned to say aloud as
*kilómetros por hora*.

## Grammar Lens: the compass, and where the euro goes
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-ORTH-C426-06, ES-ORTH-C424-08] -->

The four points of the compass have symbols that match their Spanish names, and
one does not match the English:

| symbol | Spanish | English |
|---|---|---|
| N | norte | north |
| S | sur | south |
| E | este | east |
| **O** | **oeste** | west (**W**) |

A Spanish map prints **O** where an English map prints **W**. On a reading paper
that is a trap worth having seen once.

And the currency: Spanish writes the amount first and the symbol after, with a
space — **20 €**, not *€20*. English does the reverse.
