# el polideportivo — one roof, many sports

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-DEPORTE, ES-LEX-GIMNASIO] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say **el deporte** and **el gimnasio**. A gimnasio holds one room of
machines. Now picture a building that holds a pool, a court and a running track at once.

## You'll want to know: el polideportivo
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-C438-CUERPO-01]; assesses=[ES-LEX-DEPORTE] -->

**el polideportivo** — the municipal sports centre.

Every town of any size has one, and its sign is the one you read to find out
what the pool costs and when the court is free. It is a **deporte** word, so it
is masculine: *el polideportivo*, *los polideportivos*.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C438-CUERPO-01] -->

Three pieces, and the middle one is already yours.

| piece | where it comes from |
|---|---|
| **poli-** | Greek *polýs*, many |
| **deportiv-** | from **deporte**, sport |
| **-o** | masculine ending |

**Many-sport-place.** Spanish did not borrow this word from anywhere; it built
it, in the twentieth century, out of a Greek prefix and a Latin stem, because
town councils needed a name for a thing that did not exist before.

That Greek **poli-** is the same one inside *polígono* and *poliglota*. It is
not the *poli-* of *policía*, which is a different Greek word entirely — *pólis*,
the city. Two prefixes that look identical and mean nothing like each other.
