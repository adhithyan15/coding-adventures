# ich las — the reading, and the having read

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GE-GRAMMAR-PRAETERITUM-STARK-01, GE-LEX-LESEN-07] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say "I went" and "I saw". (*Ich ging. Ich sah.*)

[PAUSE 1s] *Lesen* is strong too. Its past is **ich las**.

## Grammar Lens: one past, two English meanings
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[GE-GRAMMAR-ERZAEHL-PRAETERITUM-01]; assesses=[GE-ETYMON-LESEN-08, GE-GRAMMAR-PRAETERITUM-SCHRIFTSPRACHE-01] -->

English has two pasts for one verb: "I read", finished, and "I was
reading", still going on. German has no "-ing" past. **Ich las** is both:

> *Ich las die Nachricht.* — I read the message.
> *Ich las, und es regnete.* — I was reading, and it was raining.

The sentence around the verb tells you which. A message gets read and is
done. A rainy afternoon of reading is a scene: it was going on while
something else happened.

That is how a German story sets its scene. *Es war Samstag. Es regnete.
Ich las.* Three Präteritum verbs, and none of them is finished yet. The
weather verb takes the plain *-te*, with one extra *e* to make it
sayable: *es regnet*, **es regnete**. *Warten* does the same:
*ich wartete*, I waited, I was waiting.

## Why it's said this way: während holds the scene open
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[GE-GRAMMAR-WAEHREND-HINTERGRUND-02]; assesses=[GE-LEX-C251-LINK251-05, GE-GRAMMAR-VERB-LETZT-01, GE-LEX-C147-VERB147-05] -->

When German wants to say plainly that one thing was still going on, it
reaches for **während**, "while". It works like *weil*: the verb goes to
the end of its clause.

> *Während ich las, klingelte das Telefon.* — While I was reading, the
> phone rang.

The reading is the scene; the phone is the event that cut into it.

In conversation the same afternoon comes out in the Perfekt: *ich habe
gelesen*. The book and the story keep *ich las*. You may also hear *ich war
am Lesen*, "I was at the reading", in everyday speech, especially in the
west; it is colloquial, and a written story keeps the plain *ich las*.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GE-ETYMON-LESEN-08] -->

**lesen** ← Old High German *lesan* ← Proto-Germanic *\*lesaną*, "to
gather" (EtymDB 2.1), and it still means "to pick" in *Weinlese*, the
grape harvest. *Las* is its past by
the vowel change of the last lesson, already *las* in Old High German by the
usual account.
