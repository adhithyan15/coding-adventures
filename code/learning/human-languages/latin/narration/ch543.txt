Latin, chapter 543: Song.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
cantus, cantūs — singing, song; music.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to stay in the country, then the Latin for lovely.

You'll want to know: cantus, cantūs.
cantus, cantūs — "singing, song; music".
Cantum avium māne audīmus. In the morning we hear the song of the birds.
Masculine, fourth declension, from canō. Singing, and playing too. English chant comes from the verb cantō.

The word, taken apart.
cantus is the action noun of canō "to sing", inherited from a Proto-Indo-European root meaning "to sing" (EtymDB). English chant belongs to the same family.

Lesson 2 of 5.
cantiō, cantiōnis — a song; a spell.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for lovely, then the Latin for singing.

You'll want to know: cantiō, cantiōnis.
cantiō, cantiōnis — "a song; a spell".
Ancillae in culīnā cantiōnem canunt. The maids sing a song in the kitchen.
Feminine, third declension, from canō. Also a magic spell sung over someone. French chanson and Italian canzone come from it.

The word, taken apart.
cantiō is the action noun of canō "to sing" (Lewis & Short; EtymDB). French chanson and Italian canzone come from it.

Lesson 3 of 5.
cantilēna, cantilēnae — an old song, the same old tune.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for singing, then the Latin for a song.

You'll want to know: cantilēna, cantilēnae.
cantilēna, cantilēnae — "an old song, the same old tune".
Avia eandem cantilēnam semper canit. Grandmother always sings the same old song.
Feminine, first declension. Cantilēnam eandem canis — you sing the same old tune: you keep repeating yourself.

The word, taken apart.
cantilēna is built on cantillō "to hum", a diminutive of cantō "to sing" (Lewis & Short).

Puellae in templō concinunt. The girls sing together in the temple.
concinō, concinere, concinuī: con- + canō.

canōrus, canōra, canōrum — "melodious, tuneful".
Vōx puellae canōra est. The girl's voice is melodious.
From canor — melody, and so from canō.

The word, taken apart.
canōrus is built on canor "melody", from canō "to sing" (Lewis & Short; EtymDB).
