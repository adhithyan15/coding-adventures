# దయచేసి టీ (dayacēsi ṭī) — "tea, please," a word that sailed rather than walked

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[TE-LEX-C38-HRUDAYAM-01, TE-LEX-C08-DAYACHESI-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Two ways to say heart, done. Chapter 8's **దయచేసి** — please —
is about to earn its keep again, in front of the drink you'd politely ask
for first.

## You'll want to know: టీ
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[TE-LEX-C39-TII-01]; assesses=[TE-LEX-C08-DAYACHESI-01, TE-PRAGMATICS-C08-DAYACHESI-03] -->

> **టీ** — *ṭī* — **tea**

> **దయచేసి టీ.** — *dayacēsi ṭī.* — "Tea, please."

Day to day, Telugu politeness usually rides on the **-అండి** verb ending
Chapter 8 already named as the everyday carrier — but a bare request for a
drink, with no verb at all, is exactly where **దయచేసి** itself does the
work instead.

## The word, taken apart - the word that sailed, not the word that walked
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[TE-ETYMON-C39-TII-02]; assesses=[TE-SCRIPT-C08-DAYACHESI-04] -->

**టీ** is a direct loan from English **tea**. English's own word traces to
**Hokkien** (Southern Min Chinese) **tê**, carried west by Dutch and English
sea trade through **Malay** *teh* and into Europe — the **sea** route.

That is the opposite road from the word most of Telugu's neighbours use.
Hindi, Kannada, and Marathi all say some form of **chai**, which travelled
**overland**: Mandarin **chá**, into Persian **چای** (*chāy*), and from
Persian into North and South India alike. One drink, two Chinese dialects,
two trade routes, two families of words — and Telugu's coastal contact with
English trade left it holding the sea-word, **ṭī**, where its landlocked
neighbours picked up the land-word instead.
