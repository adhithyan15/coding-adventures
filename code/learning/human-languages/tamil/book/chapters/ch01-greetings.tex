\chapter{Greetings}
\label{ch:greetings}
\hlchaptermodality{1}

The Tamil greetings and the words that oil a first conversation, and how Tamil
sits inside the Dravidian family. This chapter is \textbf{sound first}: you can
say everything in it without reading a letter. The Tamil is printed alongside
so the shapes grow familiar. After the first greeting, one optional two-minute
step lets your finger follow and your pencil copy just its first visible shape.
Formal letter construction still starts in Chapter~4 and adds one lesson at a
time, always spelling words you already know by ear.

% canonical-insertion: TA-C01-vanakkam
\section{\ta{வணக்கம்} \emph{(vaṇakkam)} --- ``greetings,'' a small act of respect}
\label{lesson:vanakkam}
\label{lesson:TA-C01-vanakkam}

\begin{sounds}
Say it \emph{va-\d{n}ak-kam}. The \d{n} is a \textbf{retroflex} \emph{n} ---
curl the tongue back to the roof of the mouth, further back than an English
\emph{n}. The \emph{kk} is \textbf{held}, a beat longer than a single \emph{k},
and that length is doing real work: Tamil distinguishes words by it.
\end{sounds}

\begin{cousinweb}
\ta{வணக்கம்} (\emph{vaṇakkam}) comes from the verb \ta{வணங்கு}
(\emph{vaṇaṅku}), ``to bow, to pay homage.'' Turned into a noun of the action,
it means ``a bowing, an act of reverence'' --- the idea of \emph{namaste},
but from a \textbf{native Tamil} root, not a Sanskrit one. That is Tamil's
whole personality: an old language that keeps its home-grown words where its
neighbours borrowed.
\end{cousinweb}

\begin{cognates}
Watch ``hello'' split the family --- four borrowed Sanskrit, Tamil alone kept
its own:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
Language & ``Hello'' & Source \\
\midrule
\textbf{Tamil} & \ta{வணக்கம்} \emph{vaṇakkam} & native Dravidian \\
Kannada & \emph{namaskāra} & Sanskrit \\
Telugu & \emph{namaskāram} & Sanskrit \\
Malayalam & \emph{namaskāram} & Sanskrit \\
Hindi & \emph{namaste} & Sanskrit \\
English & \emph{hello} & Germanic \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\end{cognates}

\begin{culture}
Said with the palms pressed together and a small bow --- the gesture \emph{is}
the word. It serves as both \textbf{hello and goodbye}, any time, to almost
anyone, respectful without being stiff.
\end{culture}

\subsection*{Let your finger meet one shape}

Keep \ta{வணக்கம்} visible. Find only its first shape, \ta{வ}. Follow that
printed shape once with your finger while you say the whole greeting. Do not
write it yet, and do not try to remember or decode the whole word. This first
pass only lets your eye, voice, and hand notice the same greeting together.
Tracing a typeface here does not claim a handwriting stroke order.

% canonical-insertion: TA-W00-va-guided-copy
\section[Copy one visible shape]{\ta{வ} --- copy one visible shape with help}
\label{lesson:TA-W00-va-guided-copy}

Point to \ta{வணக்கம்} and say \emph{vaṇakkam} once. Keep the model on the page,
then copy \ta{வ} \textbf{once} beside it. Look back as often as you need. Your
copy can be large and slow; it does not need to look like a font.

This is shape familiarity, not a stroke-order test. Do not hide the model, copy
the rest of \ta{வணக்கம்}, or add a second new shape today. Compare your one copy
with \ta{வ}; repair one curve if you want, then stop. Say \emph{vaṇakkam} once
more: one known greeting, one tiny hand movement.

\section[\ta{நன்றி}]{\ta{நன்றி} \emph{(naṉṟi)} --- ``thank you''}
\label{lesson:nandri}

\begin{sounds}
Say it \emph{na\d{n}-\d{r}i}. Tamil hears \textbf{three} different \emph{n}
sounds and this word uses two of them: a \textbf{dental} \emph{n} (tongue on the
teeth) to open, then an \textbf{alveolar} one (on the ridge just behind them).
The \d{r} is its own sound again, not the English \emph{r}. English ears
flatten all of this to ``nandri,'' which is close enough to be understood.
\end{sounds}

\begin{cousinweb}
\ta{நன்றி} (\emph{naṉṟi}) grows from the root \ta{நல்} (\emph{nal}), ``good'' ---
so it literally means \textbf{``goodness.''} To thank, in Tamil, is to name the
good someone did you.
\end{cousinweb}

\begin{grammarlens}
\textbf{Tamil hears three of every \emph{n}, \emph{l}, and \emph{r}.} You have
now met \ta{ந} (dental \emph{n}), \ta{ன} (alveolar \emph{ṉ}), and \ta{ண}
(retroflex \emph{ṇ}) --- three letters for three tongue positions (on the
teeth / on the ridge / curled back). Tamil does the same for \emph{l}
(\ta{ல ள ழ}) and \emph{r} (\ta{ர ற}). English fuses each set into one letter;
Tamil keeps them apart, and the differences \emph{change meaning}. Learn them
word by word, not as a chart.
\end{grammarlens}

\begin{cognates}
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
Language & ``Thanks'' & Note \\
\midrule
\textbf{Tamil} & \ta{நன்றி} \emph{naṉṟi} & native (\emph{nal}, ``good'') \\
Malayalam & \emph{nandi} & the \emph{same} Dravidian word \\
Kannada & \emph{dhanyavāda} & Sanskrit \\
Telugu & \emph{dhanyavādamulu} & Sanskrit \\
Hindi & \emph{dhanyavād} / \emph{shukriyā} & Sanskrit / Perso-Arabic \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Malayalam, Tamil's closest sister, kept the native word; Kannada and Telugu
took the Sanskrit one --- the family splits as it did over ``hello.''
\end{cognates}

\section{\ta{ஆம்} \emph{(ām)} --- ``yes''}
\label{lesson:aam}

\begin{sounds}
Say it \emph{\=am} --- a \textbf{long} \emph{a}, held about twice as long as
the short one, closing on a plain \emph{m}. Vowel length is meaningful in Tamil,
so the length is not decoration.
\end{sounds}

\begin{cousinweb}
\ta{ஆம்} (\emph{ām}) is the plain ``yes.'' Doubled for emphasis it becomes
\ta{ஆமாம்} (\emph{āmām}), ``yes, indeed.''
\end{cousinweb}

\begin{grammarlens}
Tamil often says ``yes'' by \textbf{echoing the verb}: asked \emph{vandāyā?}
(``did you come?''), a natural yes is \emph{vandēṉ} (``I came''). Bank \emph{ām}
as the simple word, but expect a ``yes'' to come back as a whole verb.
\end{grammarlens}

\section{\ta{இல்லை} \emph{(illai)} --- ``no,'' and how Tamil says a thing is \emph{not}}
\label{lesson:illai}

\begin{sounds}
Say it \emph{il-lai}, with the \emph{l} \textbf{held} across the join --- the
same doubling you met in \emph{va\d{n}ak-kam}. The ending is the diphthong
\emph{ai}, as in English ``eye,'' not two separate vowels.
\end{sounds}

\begin{cousinweb}
\ta{இல்லை} (\emph{illai}) is built on \ta{இல்} (\emph{il}), ``not-being,
absence'' --- so it is not really the adjective ``no'' but the statement
\textbf{``[it] is not, [there] is not.''} Its positive twin is \ta{உண்டு}
(\emph{uṇḍu}), ``there is.''
\end{cousinweb}

\begin{grammarlens}
\textbf{Tamil negates by \emph{being}, not with a ``not''-word.} Where English
inserts \emph{not}/\emph{no}, Tamil swaps in the negative verb of existence:
\emph{puttakam \textbf{uṇḍu}} (``there \emph{is} a book'') $\to$ \emph{puttakam
\textbf{illai}} (``there \emph{is not} a book''). So \emph{illai} is both the
everyday ``no'' and the word that makes a whole sentence negative --- a deeply
Dravidian habit, unlike English or Hindi.
\end{grammarlens}

\section{\ta{சரி} \emph{(sari)} --- ``okay,'' the word that oils every conversation}
\label{lesson:sari}

\begin{sounds}
Say it \emph{sari}, two light syllables, stress even. The opening sound sits
between English \emph{s} and \emph{ch}, and Tamil treats that whole range as one
sound chosen by \textbf{position} in the word rather than by a separate spelling
--- the same economy that lets one \emph{k}-sound also be heard as \emph{g} or a
soft \emph{h}.
\end{sounds}

\begin{cousinweb}
\ta{சரி} (\emph{sari}) is native Tamil for ``correct, even, level,'' and so
``okay, agreed.'' It is the verbal nod of the language: propose a plan, hear a
warm \emph{sari}; doubled, \emph{sari sari} = ``alright, alright.''
\end{cousinweb}

\begin{grammarlens}
\textbf{One letter, several sounds.} For its stop-consonants Tamil writes
\emph{one} letter per mouth-position and lets rules of position fill in the
voicing (\emph{k}/\emph{g}, \emph{s}/\emph{ch}). The payoff is a strikingly
\emph{small} alphabet; the price is that the exact sound isn't always
predictable from the letter alone --- you pick it up by ear, word by word.
\end{grammarlens}

\section{Recap, and how Tamil says goodbye}
\label{lesson:ta-greetings-practice}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
& & Meaning \\
\midrule
\ta{வணக்கம்} & \emph{vaṇakkam} & hello / greetings \\
\ta{நன்றி} & \emph{naṉṟi} & thank you \\
\ta{ஆம்} & \emph{ām} & yes \\
\ta{இல்லை} & \emph{illai} & no / there isn't \\
\ta{சரி} & \emph{sari} & okay / correct \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{culture}
\textbf{Why Tamil won't just say ``I'm leaving.''} The everyday goodbye is
\ta{போய் வருகிறேன்} (\emph{pōy varugiṟēṉ}) --- literally ``I'll \emph{go and
come [back]},'' and the reply is \ta{போய் வா} (\emph{pōy vā}), ``go, and come
back.'' A bare ``I go'' sounds like a final parting, so you always promise a
return. A whole worldview folded into a farewell. (Or simply say \emph{vaṇakkam}
again --- it parts as well as it meets.)
\end{culture}
