## Added — Italian proves its three missing writing stages

Three writing lessons, chapters 260-262 (sequences 15620/15630/15640), all on
the A1 node `SPINE-SAY-WHY`, where `perché` already lives. None introduces a
knowledge atom, an idiom, a sense or a culture claim; every Italian word they
print is taught earlier in reading order.

- **Chapter 260, `IT-W260-my-own-line` — controlled composition.** One line
  about yourself, chosen from a closed supply with no model to copy. The
  Italian-specific check arrives with it: the accent on **è** is part of the
  letter. Without it the word is **e**, *and*, so *il mio nome e Marco* says
  "my name and Marco".
- **Chapter 261, `IT-W261-with-a-clock` — timed assessment production.** This
  book's own A1 writing paper, described with the numbers in
  `task-shapes/a1.json`: 20 minutes, a practical form of six to eight fields,
  then a 30-40 word message to a named reader (il nonno). The lesson says those
  numbers are project-defined and does not attribute them to CILS. It clocks
  only the form part: six fields against a three-minute timer, then at most one
  minute checking, so the directed time (240 s) stays inside the five-minute
  lesson cap. The age field asks for *ho … anni*, in figures past twenty,
  because the book has spelled numbers only that far. It then gives
  the CILS rule that `assessment-spec.md` records from its cited Siena, Pavia and
  Buenos Aires sources: five parts, including the structures-of-communication
  part, each scored out of 12, with 7 needed in every part. It says plainly that
  CILS's own writing minutes and word counts are not restated, because they
  could not be checked against the exam body when this was written. The spelling
  pass names the two checks the spec singles out: held double consonants
  (*nono* / *nonno*) and the accent on a final stressed vowel.
- **Chapter 262, `IT-W262-four-lines` — connected composition.** Four lines
  to one reader, joined with **e**, **ma** and **perché**. The words after
  **perché** keep their ordinary order. Because **perché** is both *why* and
  *because*, on the page only the end mark separates the two, so the wrap-up
  checks for a full stop rather than a question mark, and for the rising accent
  (é) rather than the falling one on **è**. The accents are described as a
  hand sees them moving right: é rises, è falls.

With these lessons Italian's cumulative writing stages are proved at A1 and,
inherited upward, at every level through C2. No attainment, mock, calibration or
human-validation claim is made.
