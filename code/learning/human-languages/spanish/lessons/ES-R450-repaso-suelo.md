## Grammar Lens: three words, one broken vowel
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C450-SUELO-02, ES-LEX-C450-SUELO-03, ES-LEX-C450-SUELO-05] -->

Say these aloud and listen for where the stress sits:

| Latin or French | Spanish |
|---|---|
| herba | la h**ie**rba |
| petra | la p**ie**dra |
| entraîner | entren**a**miento |

The first two broke their stressed **e** into **ie**. The third did not,
because the stress in *entrenamiento* never lands on that **e** at all — it
lands three syllables later. Same vowel, same language, different outcome, and
the only variable is stress.

This is why *piedra* has a diphthong and *petrificar* does not, and why
*rueda* has one and *rodamos* does not. One condition explains all four.

## Grammar Lens: two pairs that are not synonyms
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C450-SUELO-01, ES-LEX-C450-SUELO-02, ES-LEX-C450-SUELO-04] -->

| the pair | what separates them |
|---|---|
| patio / jardín | a patio is defined by what surrounds it |
| hierba / césped | hierba is the plant, césped is the tended surface |

Both pairs fail in the same direction for an English speaker: "yard" and
"grass" are wide words that cover each pair whole. Spanish is narrower here
than English, which is the harder direction, because nothing in your own
language warns you that a choice is waiting.
