German, chapter 30: Head and Hand.
4 lessons. All 4 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 4.
Der Kopf — “the head”.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Learn the first body part with its article and its characteristic final sound.

You'll want to know: der Kopf.
der Kopf — the head.
It is masculine and capitalised because every German noun begins with a capital letter.

Sounds you'll need: Make the -pf sequence.
At the end of Kopf, close for p and release directly into f. English has no identical native ending, so practise it slowly:
Ko-p-f becomes Kopf.

Grammar Lens: Kopf + Schmerzen.
A useful phrase is:
Ich habe Kopfschmerzen. — I have a headache.
German compounds simply place nouns together: Kopf (head) + Schmerzen (pains).

The word, taken apart: The old cup.
Kopf originally meant a cup or bowl. It is related to English cup; both were early borrowings of Late Latin cuppa. A container word became slang for the skull and eventually took over the everyday job.
German’s older inherited head-word did not disappear completely. The next micro-lesson follows it into compounds and compares the same container metaphor in French.

[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: “Ich habe Kopfschmerzen.”]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: “Kopf — cup or bowl”]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[repeat that twice]
“Der Kopf — masculine, capitalised.”

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Say “the head.” (Der Kopf.) Give its gender. (Masculine.) Why the capital? (It is a noun.) What did Kopf first mean? (A cup or bowl.) Name the related English word. (Cup.) Next: find the inherited word Haupt.


Lesson 2 of 4.
Kopf / Haupt — two head-words.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Kopf began as “cup.” German’s inherited word for “head” is Haupt, a cousin of English head and Latin caput.

The word, taken apart: inherited Haupt.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
language: Latin. inherited form: caput.
language: German. inherited form: Haupt.
language: English. inherited form: head.
The initial k becomes h relationship reflects Grimm’s law, the same systematic Germanic sound shift seen in Latin pater beside English father. The later consonants have their own history; use the initial pair as the clear signal.
Haupt survives in compounds and formal vocabulary:
Hauptstadt — capital, literally “head city”;
Hauptbahnhof — main station;
Hauptsache — main thing.

Why it's said this way: Two container metaphors.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
language: German. everyday word: Kopf. older picture: cuppa, cup.
language: French. everyday word: tête. older picture: testa, pot.
Both languages independently promoted a vessel-word to “head,” while keeping the older head-root in words for chiefs, capitals, or main things. The vocabulary sources are both Latin loans, but the metaphor was applied separately: heads look like bowls in many languages.

Lesson 3 of 4.
die Hand — "the hand".

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
After the tangle of Kopf and Haupt, here is the easiest word in the chapter — and a useful lesson about when languages don't match.

You'll want to know: die Hand.
die Hand — the hand. Feminine, and spelled exactly like English.

Sounds you'll need: Final devoicing.
One pronunciation note: the final d is said as a t. German devoices a consonant at the end of a word, so Hand ends like English hunt — but with the open ah of Tag, not hunt's vowel. Add an ending and the d comes back: die Hände ("the hands").

The word, taken apart: It is the English word.
Hand is Germanic handuz, and English inherited it directly. Same word, same meaning, two thousand years apart. German and English are close relatives, and body parts are where you feel it most:
[a table of 5 rows, read aloud]
Hand means hand.
Arm means arm.
Finger means finger.
Fuß means foot.
Herz means heart.

Why it's said this way: Where the family tree stops.
Now the interesting part. Every Romance language builds "hand" on Latin manus:
[a table of 4 rows, read aloud]
French, la main.
Italian, la mano.
Portuguese, a mão.
Spanish, la mano.
And Germanic — German, English — has handuz, which is not related to manus at all. The two families simply have different words here.
That is worth noticing, because these connections keep turning up, and it can start to feel as though everything must connect. It doesn't. Indo-European languages share a great deal, and then diverge, and "hand" is one of the places where they diverged early and completely.
The Latin word did reach German — but only as borrowed vocabulary: Manuskript, Maniküre, manuell. Learned imports, sitting beside the native word without displacing it.

Lesson 4 of 4.
Wie geht es dir? — and the whole ladder of answers.

Warm-up.
[pause 3 seconds]
Ask a friend how they are. Then ask a stranger. One word moves, and the capital letter moves with it — say which.

Guided Practice: four rungs, top to bottom.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: the question, informally — Wie geht es dir?]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: the good answer — gut]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: the middle, sing-song — so lala]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: the shrug — es geht]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: the question, formally — Wie geht es Ihnen?]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Why it's said this way.
es geht says it goes and nothing more, and the nothing more is the point. German does not treat how are you as a greeting to be waved past, so an answer that adds no adjective is heard as an answer, not as politeness. It means something between I'm managing and don't ask — and the person who hears it may well follow up.
That is the same fact the register pair makes from the other side. dir becomes Ihnen is one word, and it does not soften or intensify the question; it changes who you are allowed to be to the person. The metaphor underneath — how does it go with you — is untouched by either.

Guided Practice: and the first thing that can be wrong.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: the head — Kopf, and hold that final pf as one sound]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: what you have when it hurts — Kopfschmerzen]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
A compound built the ordinary German way: the part of you, then the pains, run together into a single word. Say the seam out loud once, then stop hearing it.

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Which answer invites a follow-up question — gut, so lala, or es geht? (Es geht.) What two words are inside Kopfschmerzen? (Kopf and Schmerzen.)
