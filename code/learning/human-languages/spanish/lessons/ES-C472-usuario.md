# el usuario — the user

## You'll want to know: el usuario
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-C472-ENCUESTA-01]; assesses=[ES-LEX-USAR] -->

**el usuario** — the user.

> Preguntaron a los **usuarios** qué necesitaban.

**La usuaria** for a woman. This is the word a library, a transport system or
a health service uses for the people it serves, and you will meet it on
notices far more often than in speech.

## Grammar Lens: the word a service reaches for
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C472-ENCUESTA-01, ES-LEX-C353-WHERE-24] -->

Spanish picks a different noun depending on what the relationship is, and the
choice tells you what kind of place you are in.

| the place | what it calls you |
|---|---|
| a shop | *el cliente* — you pay |
| a service | **el usuario** — you use |

A *biblioteca* has *usuarios*, not *clientes*, because nothing is being sold.
A *cliente* implies a transaction; a *usuario* implies **access**.

So when a notice addresses *los usuarios*, it is telling you something beyond
the word: this is a thing you are entitled to use, and the notice expects you
to behave accordingly.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C472-ENCUESTA-01, ES-LEX-USAR] -->

**usar** plus **-ario**, an ending that names the person connected with the
thing. Latin **usus**, a use, from **uti**, to use.

The same *usus* is inside English **use**, **usual** — what is used — and
**utensil**, a thing for using. And *-ario* is the ending in *el
bibliotecario*, the librarian, and *el funcionario*, the official.

So *usuario* is transparent once you see it: **the one who uses**. You could
have built it from *usar* the way you built *principiante* from *principio*.
