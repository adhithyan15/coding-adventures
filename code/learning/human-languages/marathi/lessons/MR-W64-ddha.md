# ढ — the fifth of the curled row

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[MR-SCRIPT-DDA-01, MR-SCRIPT-TTHA-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Chapter seven gave you **ट**, **ठ**, **ड** and **ण**. Four of a row of
five. Say the two of those four that belong to the *d* half of the row.

## Script
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[MR-SCRIPT-DDHA-01]; assesses=[MR-SCRIPT-DDA-01, MR-SCRIPT-TTHA-01] -->

> ढ

**ढ** is the aspirated partner of **ड** — the same step you have taken three
times already, at **क**/**ख**, at **ट**/**ठ** and at **त**/**थ**. Curl the tongue
to the roof of the mouth for a **ड**, then let the breath out after it.

Set the two side by side and the shape follows the sound: **ड ढ**. The second
carries an extra curl at its foot, so the letter that has more breath in it also
has more ink.

**ढग** — *a cloud*. Two letters, and you can read both.

## Grammar Lens: the romanization lies about this one
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[MR-SCRIPT-DDHA-01, MR-SCRIPT-DHA-01] -->

| letter | where the tongue is |
|---|---|
| ध | against the teeth |
| ढ | curled to the roof |

Both of these are written **dh** in most romanizations, and that is the whole of
the problem. **They are different letters in different rows**, and the spelling
that is supposed to help you tell them apart makes them identical.

**On the page they are nothing alike**, which is the good news: a reader who
learns the shapes is never in doubt, and a reader who leans on the romanization
is in doubt every time. This is the argument for the script the track has been
making since chapter one, in one pair of letters.

## Writing: guided copy
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[MR-SCRIPT-DDHA-01, MR-SCRIPT-DDA-01] -->
<!-- hl-writing-stage: guided-copy -->

Copy **ढ** twice with the model visible. Then write **ड** beside it and check
that only one of the two carries the extra curl at the foot.
