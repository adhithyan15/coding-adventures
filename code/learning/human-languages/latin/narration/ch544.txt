Latin, chapter 544: Strings.
6 lessons. All 6 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 6.
cithara, citharae — a cithara, concert lyre.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to sing together, then the Latin for melodious.

You'll want to know: cithara, citharae.
cithara, citharae — "a cithara, concert lyre".
Iuvenis citharam pulsat et canit. The young man plays the lyre and sings.
Feminine, first declension. A large lyre for public performance.

The word, taken apart.
cithara was borrowed from Greek kithara (Lewis & Short; EtymDB). English guitar and zither come from the same Greek word by other routes.

Lesson 2 of 6.
citharoedus, citharoedī — a singer to the lyre.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for melodious, then the Latin for a cithara.

You'll want to know: citharoedus, citharoedī.
citharoedus, citharoedī — "a singer to the lyre".
Citharoedus in theātrō canit. The lyre-singer sings in the theatre.
Masculine, second declension. A star performer who played the cithara and sang.

The word, taken apart.
citharoedus was borrowed from Greek kitharodos, built on kithara "lyre" and aoidos "singer" (EtymDB; Lewis & Short).

[pause 2 seconds]

You'll want to know: chorda, chordae.
chorda, chordae — "a string (of an instrument)".
Ūna chorda lyrae rupta est. One string of the lyre is broken.
Feminine, first declension, from Greek: first gut, then a string. English chord and cord.

The word, taken apart.
chorda was borrowed from Greek chorde "gut, string" (Lewis & Short; EtymDB). English cord and chord come from it.

Lesson 5 of 6.
psallō, psallere — to play the cithara, sing to strings.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a string, then the Latin for a lyre-player.

You'll want to know: psallō, psallere.
psallō, psallere — "to play the cithara, sing to strings".
Puella psallit et saltat. The girl plays the lyre and dances.
psallō, psallere, psallī. From Greek. English psalm comes from the same Greek verb.

The word, taken apart.
psallō was borrowed from Greek psallo "to pluck the strings" (Lewis & Short; EtymDB). English psalm comes from the same Greek verb.
