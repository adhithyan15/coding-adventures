Latin, chapter 58: Reading — Words, Lines, and a First Scene.
3 lessons. All 3 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 3.
(reading) — six words, and length made visible.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Every word below is old. The new work is meeting it first with your eyes.

Reading.
salvē avē valē grātiās ita nōn.
[pause 3 seconds]
Read down once without stopping.
[pause 3 seconds]
Again, and let each macron hold its vowel for the length you already know from hearing it.

Grammar Lens: a mark Romans did not need.
[pause 2 seconds]
The line over a vowel is a macron. Ancient Romans normally did not write one. This book does because vowel length can distinguish words and because a beginning reader deserves to see what a careful speaker says.
[pause 2 seconds]
So the mark is not decoration and it is not an accent. In salvē and valē, it asks you to hold the final vowel. In grātiās, it does the same job twice.

[repeat that twice]

Wrap-up Recall.

[pause 2 seconds]

Lesson 2 of 3.
(reading) — six lines, and the person at the end.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
The last lesson gave you isolated words. These are whole lines.
[pause 1 second]
You have already said every one of them.

Reading.
Salvē! Quid agis? Valeō. Grātiās tibi agō. Mihi nōmen est Mārcus. Valē!
[pause 3 seconds]
Read down once without stopping.
[pause 3 seconds]
Again, and this time watch the last letter of agis, valeō and agō.

Grammar Lens: the person rides on the verb.
[pause 2 seconds]
Latin does not need to write ego, “I,” before valeō or agō. The -ō already says who acts. In agis, the -s points to “you.”
[pause 2 seconds]
That makes a Latin line short without making it incomplete. A reader looks to the verb's right edge for a person English usually puts at the left.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: the six lines in order, once, without stopping]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[repeat that twice]
[question — say your answer, then pause 13 seconds]
Which line gives a name?

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 2 seconds]
Where does a Latin reader find the person when there is no written pronoun? (On the verb ending.)


Lesson 3 of 3.
(reading) — the first connected scene.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Six words, then six lines. Now the lines become one meeting.
[pause 1 second]
Forty-two words, and every one has already appeared in an earlier lesson.

Reading.
Salvē! Mihi nōmen est Mārcus. Quid tibi nōmen est? Mihi nōmen est Iūlia. Quid agis? Valeō, grātiās tibi agō. Et tū? Valeō. Haec est soror mea. Illa est māter mea. Haec aqua est. Ille pānis est. Ante merīdiem est. Post merīdiem, valē!
[pause 3 seconds]
Read the whole scene once without stopping. Let the endings tell you who acts, and let the cases keep each name and relationship in place.
[pause 3 seconds]
Read it again. This time follow the three pointers: haec, illa, ille.

Grammar Lens: Latin holds together without articles.
[pause 2 seconds]
English would keep supplying “the” and written pronouns. Latin does not. Haec aqua est needs no article; valeō needs no ego. Gender, case and verb ending carry the links instead.
[pause 2 seconds]
That is why the scene can look spare while saying everything it needs. Reading Latin is not filling in missing English words. It is learning to trust the information Latin chose to write.
[pause 2 seconds]
The final pair does the same work with time. Ante and post put the scene on opposite sides of merīdiēs without a longer explanation.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: the scene aloud, once, without stopping]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[repeat that twice]
[question — say your answer, then pause 15 seconds]
Who is nearby, and who is farther away?

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 2 seconds]
What holds the Latin scene together without articles and repeated pronouns? (Gender, case, and verb endings.)
