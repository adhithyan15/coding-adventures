Latin, chapter 19: How Are You?.
4 lessons. All 4 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 4.
quid agis? — how are you?

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Roman comedy gives you a short wellbeing question: quid agis?

Why it's said this way: one attested question.
Quid agis? literally means “What are you doing?” In the comedies of Plautus and Terence, it also works like “How are you?” or “How is it going?”
Say it in two pieces: quid … agis. Keep Latin qu as kw.
That is the whole step. A second attested question comes next, after this one is comfortable.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: quid agis?]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — recall: its literal meaning — “what are you doing?”]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — recall: its conversational use — “how are you?”]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Ask “How are you?” with today’s Roman question. (Quid agis?) What does it say literally? (What are you doing?)


Lesson 2 of 4.
ut valēs? — how do you fare?

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
You know quid agis? Add one alternative built from the farewell valē: ut valēs?

Why it's said this way: the valēre family.
Ut valēs? literally asks “How do you fare?” Its verb is valēre, “to be strong or well,” the same family as valē, “be well.” Plautus and Terence use this question in ordinary dialogue.
You may also meet modern quōmodo tē habēs? in spoken-Latin courses. It is grammatical, but it is not found in surviving classical texts. Keep that useful provenance distinction attached to the phrase.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: ut valēs?]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — recall: valē — “be well”]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — choose: Which is classically attested: ut valēs? or quōmodo tē habēs? (Ut valēs?)]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Ask “How do you fare?” (Ut valēs?) Which familiar farewell shares its verb? (Valē.) Name the modern but unattested alternative. (Quōmodo tē habēs?)


Lesson 3 of 4.
valeō and valē — answer and farewell from one verb.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
You can ask ut valēs?, "how do you fare?" Its most direct answer is already hiding inside the first farewell you learned.

You'll want to know: valeō — I fare well.
The attested reply is valeō: "I fare well" or "I am strong." Plautus's characters can intensify it as valeō rēctē ("I am properly well") or valeō et salvōs sum rēctē ("I am well and safe").
The dictionary verb is valēre, "to be strong, be well." Watch one stem move through the exchange:
ut valēs? — how do you fare?
valeō — I fare well.
valē! — be well!
That last imperative became the Chapter 1 goodbye. Latin's farewell is therefore a wish for the other person to remain well.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: "ut valēs?" — how do you fare?]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: "valeō" — I fare well]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: "valē" — be well; goodbye]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
What verb unites ut valēs?, valeō, and valē? (Valēre, "to be strong or well.") Which form answers the question? (Valeō.) What does the farewell valē literally command? (Be well.)


Lesson 4 of 4.
Practice — open, check, answer, close.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Nothing new. You already own every word below; this lesson only puts them in order, so that for the first time you can carry a whole Latin exchange from the first breath to the last.

The exchange — five lines, all of them already yours.
A: Salvē! Quid agis? Be well! What are you doing? — "hello, how's it going?"
B: Valeō, grātiās tibi agō. Ut valēs? I fare well, I give thanks to you. How do you fare?
A: Valeō. — I fare well.
B: Valē! — Be well!, the parting wish.
A: Valē!
Hear what holds it together. One verb, valēre ("to be strong, to be well"), supplies the second question (valēs), both answers (valeō), and the farewell (valē). Latin does not need a separate word for "I": the -ō of valeō and agō already is it. And the exchange opens and closes on the same wish — salvē "be well," valē "be well" — because a Roman greeting is a wish for the other person's health, coming and going.

[your turn — run: both voices, aloud, once straight through]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — choose: you meet a whole room, not one person becomes salvēte]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: the ending that already means "I" in valeō and agō — -ō]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
