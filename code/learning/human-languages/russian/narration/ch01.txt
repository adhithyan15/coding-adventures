Lesson 1 of 17.
Privét — "hi," the everyday hello.

Before we start: you can do this one in the car, but part of it needs your eyes. For that part you will want your eyes, for letter shapes on the page. You can listen to everything else now. I will say so again when we reach it, but leave this section until you have stopped: The letters in this word.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Your first Russian word and Cyrillic letters. Several shapes are false friends that look Latin but sound different; meet them head-on.

The letters in this word.
[once you have stopped driving — this part needs your eyes or your hands; if you are driving, skip ahead to the next part]
(If you already read Cyrillic, skim this.) Russian is written left to right, like English. Six letters spell privét; meet three of them now:
п = "p" — think Greek pi (Π), not Latin p.
р = "r" — a rolled r. FALSE FRIEND: it looks like Latin p but says r (it's Greek rho).
в = "v" — FALSE FRIEND: looks like Latin B but says v.
The other three, and the whole written word, arrive over this chapter. Say privét with the stress on the second syllable; Russian never writes its stress marks, so you learn the stress with the word.
Two false friends in one short word — р=r and в=v — are the two you'll trip on most. Fix them now and half of Cyrillic stops fooling you.

The word, taken apart.
Privét is built from pri- ("at, to, up against") + the old root -vet ("to speak, to counsel"). The picture is a word brought to you — a greeting handed over.
That root -vet is quietly everywhere in Russian, and it hides a word you already know in English:
sovét = "a speaking-together" becomes council, advice. Borrowed into English as Soviet — the Soviet Union was literally the Council Union.
otvét = "a speaking-back" becomes an answer.
obét = "a vow."
So privét is a cousin of Soviet — the same "speak" root, one a friendly hello, the other the most famous Russian word in English.

The word, taken apart — across the family.
Russian privét, Spanish hola, French salut, German hallo, and English hi fill the casual slot. Russian draws the line sharply: use privét with friends and the next lesson's formal greeting with everyone else.

Why it's said this way.
Privét is informal — warm, but only for people you'd address casually. To a stranger, an elder, a shopkeeper, or your boss, it can sound too familiar; there you reach for zdrávstvuyte. Russian draws the formal/informal line more strictly than English does, and greetings are where you first feel it.

Lesson 2 of 17.
Zdrávstvuyte — "be healthy," the formal hello.

Before we start: you can do this one in the car, but part of it needs your eyes. For that part you will want your eyes, for letter shapes on the page. You can listen to everything else now. I will say so again when we reach it, but leave this section until you have stopped: The letters in this word.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
You can say privét to a friend. Now the word for everyone else — longer, older, and worth the effort.

You'll want to know first.
the privét lesson — this is its formal opposite, and it reuses в=v and р=r from there.

The letters in this word.
[once you have stopped driving — this part needs your eyes or your hands; if you are driving, skip ahead to the next part]
(Skim if you read Cyrillic.) It's a long word, and you already know two of its letters, р and в. Meet three more now:
з = "z" — looks like the digit 3.
с = "s" — FALSE FRIEND: looks like Latin C but says s (Greek sigma).
у = "oo" (as in boot) — FALSE FRIEND: looks like Latin y but says oo.
Its other letters arrive with this chapter's next words; the whole written word waits until every one of them is yours.
A pronunciation mercy: the first v is silent here — natives say "zdrás-tvuy-tye," dropping the v in -vstv-. Nobody articulates all those consonants.
zdrávstvuyte ("ZDRAST-vooy-tye").

The word, taken apart.
Zdrávstvuyte is a wish for health. Its core is zdoróvye ("health") / zdoróv ("healthy") — so the greeting literally means "be healthy!" You are, politely, wishing wellness on the person in front of you.
-te on the end is the plural/polite ending — the same -te that makes a command respectful. Drop it and you get the informal singular zdrávstvuy.
An honest note on cousins: Russian zdoróvye ("health") is not related to English health — they come from different roots (English health/whole/hale is Germanic). The idea is the shared one: many languages greet by wishing health (Latin salve = "be well," the ancestor of Spanish hola's cousin salud) or peace (Arabic salām, Hebrew shalom). Russian picked health.

Grammar Lens — Russian's formal you is plural.
The -te ending here is your first taste of a rule that runs through Russian: politeness = plural. To address one person formally, Russian uses the plural "you" (vy) and plural verb endings — exactly like French vous or the usted idea in Spanish. So zdrávstvuy-te is "be healthy" said to a vy — one respected person, addressed as if plural.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Two greetings so far, one casual and one formal. The next two words are the shortest in the chapter and the most used in the language.

The letters in this word.
[once you have stopped driving — this part needs your eyes or your hands; if you are driving, skip ahead to the next part]
(Skim if you read Cyrillic.) Just two, both new and both heard in zdrávstvuyte: д (d, from Greek delta Δ) + а (a, as in father) — the one vowel that looks and sounds like Latin a.
да = da — short, clear, stressed. The easiest word in the language to read.

The word, taken apart.
да (da) is an old Slavic да (da) that has always meant "yes." But it moonlights: Russians sprinkle да (da) through speech as a soft "and / so / well…" or a tag —.
nu da = "well, yeah."
«да (da)?» on the end of a sentence = "…right?" (like French n'est-ce pas?).
So the first word you can say is also one of the most common little glue-words in the language.

The word, taken apart — across the family: the shapes of "yes".
Every language improvised its own "yes," and you can hear where each one came from:
Russian да (da) — an old Slavic affirmative.
Spanish sí — Latin sic, "thus, so."
French oui — Latin hoc ille, "that's it."
German ja — old Germanic ja.
Latin ita or sic — "thus, so."
Notice how many languages say "yes" with a word that means "so / thus" (sí, sic, ita). Russian's да (da) is its own thing — but its side-job as a "so/and" word rhymes with that pattern.

Lesson 4 of 17.
нет (nyet) — "no," literally "there-is-not".

Before we start: you can do this one in the car, but part of it needs your eyes. For that part you will want your eyes, for letter shapes on the page. You can listen to everything else now. I will say so again when we reach them, but leave these sections until you have stopped: The letters in this word and Wrap-up Recall.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say da. One syllable, no trap in it. Its opposite has a trap.

You'll want to know first.
da — its opposite, learned together as a pair.

The letters in this word.
[once you have stopped driving — this part needs your eyes or your hands; if you are driving, skip ahead to the next part]
(Skim if you read Cyrillic.) Three letters: н, е, т (e, t).
н = "n" — FALSE FRIEND: it looks exactly like Latin H but says n.
е = "ye" (as in yes) — looks like Latin e, but carries a y-glide; it is the ye of privét.
т = "t" — like Latin T.
нет = nyet ("nyeht") — one syllable, stressed, with a soft y onset.
That н=n is the last of the big four false friends (н=n; the other three say v, r and s). Learn those four and Cyrillic stops disguising itself as Latin.

The word, taken apart.
нет (nyet) is a compression of a whole clause. It comes from не + (ye)st' — "not is", i.e. "there is not."
не (nye) — the negator, "not."
yest' — "is / there is" (its ancestor is the same verb as English is).
So Russian's "no" is really "not-is" — a denial of existence, worn down to a single syllable. (Its longer form nyétu, "there isn't any," still shows the seam.)
And that little не is one of the deepest words in the whole Indo-European family — the ancient negation ne. You already own a dozen of its English children:
не corresponds to English no, not, none, never, nay, neither corresponds to Latin nōn corresponds to Sanskrit na corresponds to French non.
When you say нет (nyet), you are pronouncing a cousin of no that has meant "not" for six thousand years.

The word, taken apart — across the family: one ancient "no".
Unlike "yes" — where every language improvised — the world's "no" is astonishingly one word, stretched across the whole family. Russian нет (nyet) carries не; English says no and not; Latin said nōn, French says non, Sanskrit said na. All of them are the same ancient ne, and нет (nyet) is Russia's share of it.

Lesson 5 of 17.
спасибо (spasíbo) — "God save you," now just "thanks".

Before we start: you can do this one in the car, but part of it needs your eyes. For that part you will want your eyes, for letter shapes on the page. You can listen to everything else now. I will say so again when we reach them, but leave these sections until you have stopped: The letters in this word and Wrap-up Recall.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
You can greet someone. Now thank them — and this word has a whole sentence folded inside it.

You'll want to know first.
privét — to contrast б (b) with the B-shaped letter you met there, which says v.

The letters in this word.
[once you have stopped driving — this part needs your eyes or your hands; if you are driving, skip ahead to the next part]
(Skim if you read Cyrillic.) Six letters: с, п, а, с, и, б, о.
с = "s" (the false friend), п = "p", а = "a" — all met already.
и = "ee," like a backwards Latin N; о = "o".
б = "b" — meet the partner that ends the confusion: б is b, while the very similar-looking letter in privét is v. One has an open belly (б = b); the other stacks two bowls and says v.
A sound note: the first о is unstressed, so it reduces toward "a" — natives say "spa-SEE-ba." Unstressed Russian о almost always softens to an a-ish sound. Stress lands on -си-.
спасибо = spasíbo ("spa-SEE-ba").

The word, taken apart.
спасибо (spasíbo) is a frozen prayer. It is a worn-down contraction of spasí Bog — "God save [you]":
спаси — "save!", the command form of spasti ("to save, rescue").
Bog — "God."
Over centuries spasí Bog fused and shed its final consonant into today's спасибо (spasíbo). You are, etymologically, blessing whoever helped you.
Courtesy words are often fossilised blessings — Spanish adiós is a Dios, English goodbye is God be with ye. спасибо (spasíbo) belongs to that family: a little "God save you," said so often the prayer wore invisible.

The word, taken apart — across the family: thanks, and where it comes from.
Russian спасибо (spasíbo) — "God save you."
Spanish gracias — Latin gratia, "grace."
French merci — Latin merces, "reward, mercy."
German danke — "think of, be mindful."
English thank you — "think": I'll hold you in thought.
Every culture built "thanks" from a different metaphor — grace, reward, mindful thought — and Russian built it from a blessing.

Why it's said this way.
Because it began as spasí Bog, older or very devout speakers sometimes still feel the "God" in it; a few religious communities prefer blagodaryú ("I give a blessing," a calque of Greek eucharistō). But in everyday modern Russian, спасибо (spasíbo) is simply the word for thanks, its prayer long dissolved.

Before we start: you can do this one in the car, but part of it needs your eyes. For that part you will want your eyes, for letter shapes on the page. You can listen to everything else now. [pause 2 seconds]
Say spasíbo. The word that answers it, and asks for things, is the longest you have met — and shorter than it looks when spoken.

You'll want to know first.
spasíbo — this is its natural partner: spasíbo becomes пожалуйста (pozhálusta) is Russia's "thank you" becomes "you're welcome."

The letters in this word.
[once you have stopped driving — this part needs your eyes or your hands; if you are driving, skip ahead to the next part]
(Skim if you read Cyrillic.) Everything here is familiar except one new letter: пожалуйста (pozhálusta).
ж = "zh" — the sound in measure. It looks like a little beetle with wings; no Latin letter carries this sound.
The rest you know: п(p) о(o) а(a) л(l) у(oo) й(y) с(s) т(t).
A big pronunciation mercy: nobody says all four middle syllables. The written пожалуйста (pozhálusta) is spoken "pa-ZHAL-sta," swallowing the -уй- entirely, and the first о reduces to a.
пожалуйста = pozhálusta, said "pa-ZHAL-sta".

The word, taken apart.
пожалуйста (pozhálusta) is пожалуй + a softening tag -ста. пожалуй is the command form of the old verb zhálovat', "to grant, to bestow a favour" — from the root жал-, the same root as zhal' ("a pity") and zhalet' ("to feel for someone").
So under the politeness sits "grant [me this], do me the kindness." English please is the same idea worn down — short for if it please you.
It also does a job English splits off: the reply to thanks. Where English needs a separate "you're welcome," Russian answers spasíbo with пожалуйста (pozhálusta) — "[it was] a favour freely given."

The word, taken apart — across the family: "please" is a favour, everywhere.
Russian пожалуйста (pozhálusta) — "grant [me] a favour."
French s'il vous plaît — "if it pleases you."
Spanish por favor — "for a favour."
English please — "[if it] please [you]."
German bitte — "I request," and also "you're welcome."
German bitte pulls the same double shift — one word for both "please" and "you're welcome."

Why it's said this way.
Because it is really a request for a kindness, пожалуйста (pozhálusta) also softens commands: add it to an instruction and a bark becomes a request. And as the answer to spasíbo, it closes the ritual of thanks the way "you're welcome" does.

Lesson 8 of 17.
в and р — writing the two great false friends.

Before we start: this one needs your hands, so it is not a driving lesson. You will want a pen and something to write on. You can listen to everything else now. I will say so again when we reach it, but leave this section until you have stopped: Guided Practice.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
You read these two in привет (privét). Now you'll write them. в and р are the letters that fool every beginner — they wear Latin faces (B and P) but say v and r. Forming them by hand is the fastest way to stop your eye from lying to you.

Why it's said this way — why they fool you.
Both came from Greek, not Latin, so the shape and the sound drifted apart:
в is Greek beta — beta made the "v" sound in later Greek, and Cyrillic kept it. It looks like Latin B; it says v.
р is Greek rho — always an "r." It looks like Latin P; it says a rolled r.

Writing — break it apart, then write it.
в ("v") — a vertical, then two stacked bowls:
the vertical — a straight downstroke, top to bottom.
the upper bowl — from the top of the vertical, bulge out right and back in.
the lower bowl — from the middle, a second belly out to the base.
Two bellies stacked on a spine — that's what tells в apart from Latin B (one belly) and from Russian б (which you'll meet next).
р ("r") — a vertical that drops below the line, then a top loop:
the vertical — a downstroke that sinks below the baseline (like the tail of Latin p).
the loop — a single closed bump at the top, on the right.
So р is drawn like a Latin lowercase p, tail and all — but it is an r.
Their capitals, В and Р, are the same shapes drawn to full height, with Р sitting on the line. They look even more like Latin B and P, and they still say v and r.

Writing — break it apart, then write it.
с ("s") — a single open curve:
the curve — one stroke: start top-right, sweep left and down like a C, open on the right.
That's the whole letter — a lone crescent. It is the shape of Latin C, but it comes from Greek sigma (the "s" sound). Think of it as the "s" hiding inside the English word centre said the soft way — c that sounds like s.
н ("n") — two verticals joined by a middle bar (a Latin H shape):
the left vertical — a downstroke.
the cross bar — a short horizontal joining the two uprights in the middle (not slanted like a Latin N).
the right vertical — a second downstroke, parallel to the first.
So н is drawn exactly like a capital H — two posts and a rung — but it is an n. (Russian's letter for the H-sound doesn't exist; the "h" of foreign words is usually spelled with г or х.)
The capital of с is С, the same crescent drawn to full height, and it still says s.

Script — б ("b") — and how it differs from в.
[once you have stopped driving — this part needs your eyes or your hands; if you are driving, skip ahead to the next part]
б and в are cousins (both from Greek beta, which split into a "b" letter and a "v" letter). They share a vertical spine, so keep them apart by their tops:
в (v) = spine + two stacked bowls (both bellies face right).
б (b) = spine + one lower belly + a flag/hat curving off the top.
Break б apart — a vertical, a lower belly, a top flag:
the vertical — a downstroke.
the belly — one bowl bulging right from the lower half.
the top flag — a short stroke off the top, curving right (the "hat" that в doesn't have).
That top flag is the whole difference: б has a hat and one belly; в has no hat and two bellies.

Script — д ("d") — the delta on little feet.
[once you have stopped driving — this part needs your eyes or your hands; if you are driving, skip ahead to the next part]
д comes from Greek delta (Δ), the triangle — and you can still feel the triangle in it. Break it apart — a body, then two little feet:
the body — a rounded/looped shape sitting on the baseline (think a small delta or a curled g-like body).
the feet — two little legs dropping below the baseline, one at each corner of the base.
Those two descending feet are д's signature — no Latin letter has them. Say "delta" as you draw it and the shape sticks.
Their capitals: Б keeps the belly but swaps the curling flag for a straight bar across the top; Д is the delta on its feet, drawn to full height.

п and и — filling in привет (privét).

Before we start: this one needs your hands, so it is not a driving lesson. You will want a pen and something to write on and your eyes, for letter shapes on the page. Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
You can already hand-write the в and the р of привет (privét). Now take two more of its letters: п ("p") and и ("ee"). One is an honest Greek shape; the other is a quiet false friend — it looks like a Latin N.

Script — п ("p") — the Greek gate.
[once you have stopped driving — this part needs your eyes or your hands; if you are driving, skip ahead to the next part]
п is Greek pi (Π), the letter mathematicians write for 3.14…. It's an honest letter: it looks like a little gateway and says "p."
Break it apart — two verticals joined by a top bar:
the left vertical — a downstroke.
the top bar — a short horizontal across the top.
the right vertical — a second downstroke, parallel to the first.
A doorway shape: two posts and a lintel. (Don't confuse it with н — that one's bar sits in the middle, this one's sits on top.)

Script — и ("ee") — the backwards N.
[once you have stopped driving — this part needs your eyes or your hands; if you are driving, skip ahead to the next part]
и says "ee" (as in meet) — a vowel. But watch out: it looks almost exactly like a Latin N with the diagonal reversed. That's the quiet trap — your eye reads "N," but it's a vowel.
Break it apart — two verticals joined by a rising diagonal:
the left vertical — a downstroke.
the diagonal — from the bottom of the left vertical up to the top of the right (rising left-to-right — the mirror of Latin N, whose diagonal falls).
the right vertical — a downstroke.
So the tell between и and Latin N is which way the diagonal leans: и rises, N falls. (Later you'll meet й — an и with a little breve on top, "ee-y.")
Their capitals, П and И, are the same shapes drawn to full height. So is Н, the capital of н, with its bar still in the middle.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
The finish line. е and т are two of Cyrillic's most honest letters — they look like their Latin twins and sound close to them. Learn these and you can hand-write the whole of привет (privét) from memory.

Script — е ("ye") — the honest vowel.
[once you have stopped driving — this part needs your eyes or your hands; if you are driving, skip ahead to the next part]
е looks like a Latin e and sounds close to one — with a small twist: at the start of a syllable it carries a y-glide, "ye" (as in yes). So привет (privét) ends …v-ye-t. (Letters that add a y-onset like this are called iotated — е, ё, ю, я are the family; you'll meet the others in Chapter 2.)
Break it apart — a curve, then a middle bar (exactly like writing a Latin e):
the curve — start top-right, sweep left, curl down and round.
the middle bar — the short horizontal that closes the e.
No trap here: what you see is what you get.

Script — т ("t") — Greek tau, honest too.
[once you have stopped driving — this part needs your eyes or your hands; if you are driving, skip ahead to the next part]
т is Greek tau, and it looks and sounds like Latin T: a crossbar on a stem. (In handwriting Russians often make lowercase т look like an m with a bar over it, but the printed citation form is the clean T-shape you see here.)
Break it apart — a central downstroke, then a top bar:
the downstroke — a vertical stem.
the top bar — a horizontal across the top, centred on the stem.

Writing — now write the whole word.
You now own every letter of привет (privét):
п (p), р (r), и (ee), в (v), е (ye), т (t) = privét.
Two of them are false friends (р=r, в=v), one is a quiet one (и=ee), and three are honest (п, е, т (e, t)). Write it slowly, naming each letter's sound.
