Hindi, chapter 93: Telling Somebody To Do Something.
6 lessons. You can do the first 4 of them in the car; after that you will want to have stopped.


Lesson 1 of 6.
बोलो (bolo) — a command at the level you already know how to choose.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say tumhārā. You learned that Hindi has three levels of you and that the possessive changes with each. Say the verb bolnā. This chapter does the same thing to the verb.

You'll want to know: बोलो.
बोलो (bolo) — "speak," said to somebody you would call तुम.
Take the verb, drop the -ना, add -o:
bolnā becomes bol becomes bolo.
The same operation on any verb: karnā becomes karo, sunnā becomes suno, calnā becomes calo.

Grammar Lens: the command carries the level, and nothing else has to.
This is the part that makes the imperative economical. In English a command is one word for everybody — speak — and politeness has to be added around it: speak, please speak, would you mind speaking.
In Hindi the verb ending itself carries the level:
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
level: तुम. the form: बोलो (bolo). to whom: a friend, a sibling.
level: आप. the form: boliye. to whom: a stranger, an elder.
level: तू. the form: bol. to whom: intimate, or rude.
You already know how to choose between those three, because you chose between tumhārā, āpkā and terā the same way. The pronoun does not even have to be said: बोलो (bolo) on its own tells the listener which one you meant.

Lesson 2 of 6.
बोलिए (boliye) — the ending you have been saying for eighty chapters.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say māf kījiye. You learned it as one lump meaning "sorry." Say it again and listen to the end of the second word.

You'll want to know: बोलिए.
बोलिए (boliye) — "please speak," said to somebody you would call आप.
The ending is -iye, on the same stem as before:
bolnā becomes bol becomes boliye.
And now the lump comes apart. कीजिए is karnā, "to do," with this same ending: kar + -iye. Māf kījiye is literally "please do forgiveness." You have been giving a respectful command since the ninth chapter without knowing it was one.

Grammar Lens: politeness is built in, not added on.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
English. command: speak. how politeness is carried: by adding please.
Hindi तुम. command: बोलो (bolo). how politeness is carried: by the ending.
Hindi आप. command: बोलिए (boliye). how politeness is carried: by the ending.
बोलिए (boliye) already contains the respect, so it needs no kṛpayā. Adding one is not wrong — it is warmer, the way would you mind is warmer than please — but the bare form is complete and polite on its own.
A stem ending in a vowel lengthens slightly before -iye: karnā gives kījiye, not kariye, and denā gives dījiye. Those two are the ones you will meet most, and you already had the first.

[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: what māf kījiye literally asks for (please do forgiveness)]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: whether boliye needs kṛpayā in front of it (no)]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
What ending makes the respectful command? (-iye.) What verb is hiding inside kījiye? (Karnā, to do.) Which of bolo and boliye would you use with a stranger? (Boliye.)


Lesson 3 of 6.
तुम हो (tum ho) — the copula that was hiding inside one question.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say tumhārā. You can name the tum level and command at it. Say "you are" at that level, and notice you have never been given the word.

You'll want to know: तुम हो.
तुम हो (tum ho) — "you are," at the tum level.
हो is the copula that goes with तुम, and nothing else takes it.

Grammar Lens: the copula, completed.
You have had the others for a long time. Here they are together, which is the first time the set has been complete:
[a table of 4 rows, read aloud]
subject: मैं. copula: हूँ.
subject: तुम. copula: हो.
subject: आप. copula: हैं.
subject: वह. copula: है.
Four forms and no irregularity beyond having to learn them. हो is the one that was missing, and it was missing in an awkward way: the track has been saying it inside the age question all along, as a lump, without the form ever standing on its own.
Now it can. Tum acchhe ho, tum śarmīle ho — any of the character adjectives you have, at the familiar level.

Grammar Lens: matching the level all the way through.
A sentence has to keep one level from end to end:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
level: तुम. copula: हो. command: बोलो (bolo).
level: आप. copula: हैं. command: बोलिए (boliye).
Mixing them — āp ho, or tum boliye — is the error a listener notices at once, in the way an English speaker notices you was.

Lesson 4 of 6.
मत (mat) — the word Hindi keeps for saying "don't".

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say bolo and boliye. Now say the opposite of each — and notice that the negative you already know is not the one Hindi uses here.

You'll want to know: मत.
मत (mat) — "don't," and it goes in front of the verb:
मत बोलिए — mat boliye — please don't speak. मत बोलो — mat bolo — don't speak.
Hindi has a negative for statements, which you already have, and a separate one reserved for commands. Using the statement negative to tell somebody not to do something is the mistake an English speaker makes, because English uses not for both.

Grammar Lens: the sign you will actually meet it on.
A negative command is what a public notice is. This is the shape:
[a table of 1 row, read aloud]
यहाँ मत बैठिए (yahā̃ mat baiṭhiye) means please do not sit here.
Three pieces, and you have all three: yahā̃ here, mat don't, baiṭhiye the respectful command from baiṭhnā, built with the ending from the last lesson.
Notice the politeness level. A public sign addresses a stranger, so it uses the आप form — which is why a reading paper's notices are full of -iye and why that ending was worth taking apart.

[your turn — say: where mat stands relative to the verb (in front)]

Lesson 5 of 6.
tīn star, ek kriyā — three levels running through everything.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, because the lesson points at something written down. You can listen to all of it now and come back to the parts that need looking at once you have stopped.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say the command for "speak" at both levels you can now use, and name who each one is for.

Grammar Lens: the same choice, made four times.
The level is not a fact about the verb. It is a fact about who you are talking to, and every part of the sentence agrees to it:
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
level: तू. possessive: तेरा. copula: है.
level: तुम. possessive: तुम्हारा. copula: हो.
level: आप. possessive: आपका. copula: हैं.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
level: तू. command: बोल. negative command: मत (mat) बोल.
level: तुम. command: बोलो (bolo). negative command: मत (mat) बोलो (bolo).
level: आप. command: बोलिए (boliye). negative command: मत (mat) बोलिए (boliye).
Read each row straight across. Choosing a person picks the whole row, and every cell in it follows. That is the saving: one decision, not four.
The तू column is there for recognition. A learner should read it and not produce it — it is intimate between close friends and insulting to anyone else, and the risk of getting it wrong is much larger than the benefit of using it.

Grammar Lens: how each command is built.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
level: तू. from bolnā: बोल. ending: the bare stem.
level: तुम. from bolnā: बोलो (bolo). ending: -o.
level: आप. from bolnā: बोलिए (boliye). ending: -iye.
Three endings, one stem. And as the table above showed, मत (mat) is the same word at every level — the negative does not change with politeness, only the verb does.

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
What does choosing a level of you decide? (A whole column — possessive, copula and command together.) Which column should a learner read but not use? (तू.) Does the negative change with the level? (No — mat is the same at all three.)


Lesson 6 of 6.
यहाँ मत बैठिए (yahā̃ mat baiṭhiye) — a notice is the whole chapter in three words.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say the negative command word and the respectful ending. A public sign is made of nothing else.

Grammar Lens: why a notice is always at the aap level.
A sign does not know who is reading it, so it addresses a stranger — and a stranger takes आप. That single fact explains the shape of almost every notice a reading paper prints:
[a table of 1 row, read aloud]
on the sign: यहाँ मत बैठिए (yahā̃ mat baiṭhiye). at the tum level it would be: यहाँ मत बैठो.
The second is what a friend says to you. The first is what a wall says to everybody, which is why -iye is worth more to a reader than -o.

Reading.
Read these aloud. Every word has been taught:
यहाँ मत बैठिए (yahā̃ mat baiṭhiye)। यहाँ मत बोलिए (boliye)। कृपया सुनिए।.
Three notices, and the pattern is identical in all three: the respectful ending, with mat in front when the notice forbids and nothing in front when it asks.
