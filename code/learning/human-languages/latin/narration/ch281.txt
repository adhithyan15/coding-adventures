Latin, chapter 281: The Scene and the Event.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
vīdī, vīdit — the past of what happened.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say "I recently read a book". (Nūper librum lēgī.) Now "before, I lived in Rome". (Anteā Rōmae habitāvī.) And ask "what happened?" (Quid accidit?)
[pause 1 second]
All three are the perfect, Latin's past for a thing that happened and was over. Here are its six persons.

Grammar Lens: one stem, six endings.
Every verb has a perfect stem: for videō, vīd-, with a long ī. Onto it go six endings, the same for every verb:
vīdī — I saw. vīdistī — you saw. vīdit — he saw, she saw. vīdimus — we saw. vīdistis — you all saw. vīdērunt — they saw.
Stories lean on two of them: -it for one person, -ērunt for several.
[pause 2 seconds]
The stem is learned with each verb. veniō has vēn-: vēnit, vēnērunt. sum has fu-: fuī, I was; fuit, he was.
[pause 1 second]
Vīdī is also "I have seen"; the sentence decides.

You'll want to know: the -āvī verbs.
Most -āre verbs add -āv-, like the habitāvī you own:
ambulāre — ambulāvī, I walked; ambulāvit, he walked clāmāre — clāmāvit, he shouted nārrāre — nārrāvit, she told; nārrāvērunt, they told.
The stems learned one by one are the others: vīdī, vēnī, fuī, lēgī, and bibī, from bibō, with nothing added at all.

The word, taken apart: fuī.
fuī looks nothing like sum because it is built on another root, the one the chapter on sum named: Proto-Indo-European bʰuH-, "to become", which EtymDB 2.1 lists among the parents of sum and which also gives English be.
vīdī keeps the root of videō with a longer vowel (Lewis & Short give vīdi as its perfect). That vowel is how the ear tells videt, "he sees", from vīdit, "he saw".

[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: ambulāvī, I walked; ambulāvērunt, they walked]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
"He saw"? (Vīdit.) "They came"? (Vēnērunt.) "I walked"? (Ambulāvī.) Which ending says "they"? (-ērunt.) And which root is behind fuī? (bʰuH-, "to become", the root of English be.)


Lesson 2 of 5.
eram, erat — the past that stays open.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say "I am" and "they are". (Sum. Sunt.) Now "I was", in the perfect. (Fuī.)
[pause 1 second]
Fuī closes a door: I was, and that is over. Latin has a second "was" that holds it open.

Grammar Lens: six forms, and the endings you know.
Ōlim Rōma parva erat. — Long ago Rome was small.
erat is "was", the imperfect of sum. All six persons:
eram — I was. erās — you were. erat — he, she, it was. erāmus — we were. erātis — you all were. erant — they were.
[pause 2 seconds]
The endings -m, -s, -t, -mus, -tis, -nt are those of sum, es, est, sumus, estis, sunt, on the stem era-. Before -m, -t and -nt the a is short.
[pause 1 second]
Erat is a state that went on, with no edge in view.
Fessus eram. — I was tired. Caelum obscūrum erat. — The sky was dark.

Why it's said this way: fuit or erat?
[pause 2 seconds]
Both are "was"; the choice is the frame. Laetus eram paints a mood that lasted through the story. Laetus fuī sums it up from outside: I was happy, and that time is over. A story sets its scene with erat.

The word, taken apart: eram.
eram is usually explained as an older esam, built on the same root as est: between two vowels an old Latin s turned into r, and es-a-m came out as eram. Neither EtymDB nor Lewis & Short records a form that old, so this is the account histories of Latin give, offered here as one. The s survives where no vowel followed it: es, est, estis.

Guided Practice.

[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: Fessus eram. — then a woman says it: Fessa eram.]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: Caelum obscūrum erat. — then In vīllā erāmus.]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: the closed door, then the open one — fuit, then erat]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
"They were"? (Erant.) "We were"? (Erāmus.) Whose endings does eram borrow? (The present sum, es, est.) Which "was" paints a scene, fuit or erat? (Erat.) And what did the r of eram used to be? (An s, between two vowels.)


Lesson 3 of 5.
ambulābam — I was walking.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say "I was tired", the open past. (Fessus eram.) Now "I walked", the closed one. (Ambulāvī.)
[pause 1 second]
Eram belongs to one verb. Every other verb makes its open past with two letters slipped in before the ending.

Grammar Lens: -ba- before the ending.
Take the present stem, add -ba-, then the endings eram taught you:
ambulābam — I was walking. ambulābās — you were walking. ambulābat — he was walking. ambulābāmus — we were walking. ambulābātis — you all were walking. ambulābant — they were walking.
[pause 2 seconds]
The vowel before -ba- comes from the infinitive. An -āre verb keeps its ā, and an -ēre verb its ē: habēre, habēbam, I had, I used to have; sedēre, sedēbat, he was sitting.
[pause 1 second]
The other verbs put -ēbam on the stem: legere, legēbam, I was reading; volō gives volēbat, he wanted. And the verbs with -iō keep their i: venīre, veniēbam; dormīre, dormiēbat, he was sleeping.
[pause 1 second]
What it says is the same for all of them: an action under way, or a habit. Ambulābam is "I was walking", and also "I used to walk".

The word, taken apart: the -b- of -bam.
Histories of Latin often trace the -b- of -bam to the root behind fuī, Proto-Indo-European bʰuH-, "to become". That is a reconstruction, and neither EtymDB nor Lewis & Short takes it up, so it is offered as the usual account, not as a fact.
What is certain is where the ending went. Spanish kept it: cantābat, "he was singing", is Spanish cantaba. French wore the b away, so the same form there is chantait.

[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: Mercātōrēs clāmābant. — The merchants were shouting]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: legēbam, I was reading; veniēbam, I was coming]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Lesson 4 of 5.
pluēbat … vēnit — the scene and the event.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say "it rains", with no subject. (Pluit.) Now put it in the open past, the way legere gives legēbat. (Pluēbat, it was raining.)

Grammar Lens: which past for which job.
Pluēbat. Subitō amīcus vēnit. — It was raining. Suddenly a friend came.
[pause 2 seconds]
The rain was already going on, and it went on afterwards: that is the imperfect, pluēbat. The friend's arrival happened once, at one moment, and was done: that is the perfect, vēnit.
[pause 2 seconds]
So a story runs on two tracks. The imperfect paints the scene: weather, places, what people were doing. The perfect moves the events along.
In forō ambulābam; subitō amīcum vīdī. — I was walking in the forum; suddenly I saw a friend.
[pause 1 second]
A word like subitō, "suddenly", usually brings a perfect after it: suddenness is an event.

Why it's said this way: habitābam or habitāvī?
[pause 2 seconds]
Sometimes both are right. Rōmae habitāvī counts it up from outside: I lived in Rome, a closed stretch. Rōmae habitābam puts you inside it: I was living in Rome then.
[pause 2 seconds]
One small trap: pluit in the perfect is spelled pluit too, "it rained", the way accidit serves two tenses. Only the story around it decides.

The word, taken apart: perfect and imperfect.
The names are Latin. perfectum is the participle of a verb that Lewis & Short gloss "to carry through, to finish", and EtymDB 2.1 builds that verb from per-, "through", and faciō, "to make, to do". The perfect is the past that was carried through. imperfectum puts in-, "not", in front: unfinished, still going, which is what pluēbat is.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: Pluēbat. Subitō amīcus vēnit.]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: In forō ambulābam; subitō amīcum vīdī.]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: a scene — Caelum obscūrum erat. — and an event — Amīcus vēnit.]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: inside it, then outside it — Rōmae habitābam, then Rōmae habitāvī]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[question — say your answer, then pause 12 seconds]
In 'Pluebat. Subito amicus venit.', which verb is the event?

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Which past paints the scene? (The imperfect: pluēbat.) Which moves the story along? (The perfect: vēnit.) After subitō, which one is likely? (The perfect: suddenness is an event.) And what does perfectum mean, taken apart? (Carried through.)


Lesson 5 of 5.
Review — the scene, and what happened in it.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say "they saw". (Vīdērunt.) Then "we were", the open past. (Erāmus.)

What you've built.
Four lessons, and both of Latin's story pasts are in your hands. The perfect, a stem and six endings: vīdī, vīdit, vīdērunt, with -āvī for most -āre verbs. The imperfect: eram, erat, erant for sum, and -ba- before the ending for every other verb: ambulābam, habēbam, legēbam, veniēbam.
[pause 2 seconds]
And the division of labour between them: the imperfect holds the scene still, the perfect drops the events into it.
Pluēbat, et in caupōnā sedēbam. Subitō amīcus intrāvit. — It was raining, and I was sitting in the inn. Suddenly a friend came in.
[pause 1 second]
Two verbs of scene, pluēbat and sedēbam; one event, intrāvit, and subitō in front of it.
