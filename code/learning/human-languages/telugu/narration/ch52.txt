Telugu, chapter 52: Welcoming a Guest.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
తలుపు (talupu) — a door.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the last run: what did daṇḍaṁ mean, and what did gauravaṁ mean?

You'll want to know: తలుపు.
తలుపు (talupu) — "a door".
తలుపు (talupu) is the leaf: the panel that swings and shuts. The opening it fills is a different word — వాకిలి in everyday speech, ద్వారం in the formal Sanskrit register. Telugu keeps the hole and the thing that fills it apart, which English manages only when it is being careful.
తలుపు (talupu) is inherited. That ద్వారం sits beside it doing no daily work is the usual arrangement here: the old word carries the load, the borrowed one keeps the ceremony.

What you've built.
The first of five things standing between the street and a guest.

Lesson 2 of 5.
కుర్చీ (kurcī) — a chair.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: what did talupu mean?

You'll want to know: కుర్చీ.
కుర్చీ (kurcī) — "a chair".
Arabic kursī is a seat, a throne — the word standing in the Qur'ān's Throne Verse. Persian took it, Urdu carried it across the Deccan, and Telugu has కుర్చీ (kurcī).
The word came because the thing came. A house welcoming a guest before the chair arrived offered a పీట, a low wooden plank set on the floor, and many still do. Borrowed furniture brings borrowed nouns with it.
One trap: కుర్చీ (kurcī) and కూర్చో, "sit", look like a pair and are not. The verb is inherited, the noun is Arabic, and the resemblance is an accident. Sound alone never proves a relation.

What you've built.
Two: a door and a chair.

Lesson 3 of 5.
ముగ్గు (muggu) — the rice-flour design drawn at the doorstep.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: what did kurcī mean?

You'll want to know: ముగ్గు.
ముగ్గు (muggu) — "the rice-flour design drawn at the doorstep".
ముగ్గు (muggu) is the design put on the ground outside the door, in rice flour, before the sun is up. It is walked over all day and drawn again the next morning.
The flour is the point. It is laid where ants and birds will find it, so the welcome begins by feeding something smaller than a guest. Tamil calls the same act கோலம், Kannada ರಂಗೋಲಿ. The word is local; the practice is not.

What you've built.
Three: the door, the chair, and the mark on the ground in front of both.

Lesson 4 of 5.
పువ్వు (puvvu) — a flower.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: what did muggu mean?

You'll want to know: పువ్వు.
పువ్వు (puvvu) — "a flower".
Proto-Dravidian pū, and the family kept it: Tamil பூ, Malayalam പൂ, Kannada ಹೂವು.
There is that p- to h- once more on the Kannada side — the third time in this book, after పాలు beside ಹಾಲು and పండు beside ಹಣ್ಣು. A sound law you have watched work three times has stopped being a claim; it is a pattern you can predict with, and predicting is the only test a sound law ever gets.
The plural stem is పూల-, which is about to matter.

What you've built.
Four.

Lesson 5 of 5.
పూలదండ (pūladaṇḍa) — a flower garland.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: what did puvvu mean?

You'll want to know: పూలదండ.
పూలదండ (pūladaṇḍa) — "a flower garland".
పూల, flowers, and దండ — the same దండ that gave you దండం, the salutation, one chapter back. Sanskrit daṇḍa is a staff, and by extension a row. A పూలదండ (pūladaṇḍa) is a row of flowers, strung.
So the greeting and the garland are one word used twice. You say దండం with your hands; you put పూలదండ (pūladaṇḍa) round a neck. Both of them come from a straight line.
And that closes the welcome: the ముగ్గు (muggu) on the ground, the తలుపు (talupu) opened, the కుర్చీ (kurcī) offered, and a పూలదండ (pūladaṇḍa) of పువ్వులు for the అతిథి who sent no word ahead.

What you've built.
Five: తలుపు (talupu), కుర్చీ (kurcī), ముగ్గు (muggu), పువ్వు (puvvu), పూలదండ (pūladaṇḍa). A guest can now be met at the door and seated.
