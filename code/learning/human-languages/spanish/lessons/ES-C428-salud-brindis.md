# ¡Salud! — a noun doing a job

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C334-BODY-31, ES-ORTH-C428-03] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say *la salud*, health. Say *¡Felicidades!* You have one noun that
describes and one that acts. This lesson turns the first into the second.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-ORTH-C428-04]; assesses=[] -->

**¡Salud!** — "cheers," raising a glass.

It is the same word you already have, with no article and an exclamation around
it. Stripped of *la*, it stops describing a condition and becomes a **wish**:
*health to you*.

That is what almost every toast in Europe is. English *cheers* wishes
cheerfulness; Irish *sláinte* is literally *health*; and *¡Salud!* is the Spanish
member of the same family. Raising a glass is wishing somebody well, and most
languages say so plainly.

## Grammar Lens: the same word, two occasions
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-ORTH-C428-04, ES-LEX-C334-BODY-31] -->

**¡Salud!** does a second job that catches English speakers out:

| when | what it means |
|---|---|
| raising a glass | cheers |
| after somebody sneezes | bless you |

English uses two unrelated expressions for those. Spanish uses one word for
both, and the reason is the same in each case: **you are wishing the person
health**, whether because they are about to drink or because they have
sneezed.

Some speakers say *¡Jesús!* after a sneeze instead, and in a formal toast you
may hear the fuller *¡A tu salud!* — "to your health." The bare word covers
both occasions and is never wrong.
