5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
inquilīnus, inquilīnī — a tenant, a lodger.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to delay, then the Latin for to stop for a while.

You'll want to know: inquilīnus, inquilīnī.
inquilīnus, inquilīnī — "a tenant, a lodger".
Inquilīnus dominō mercēdem dat. The tenant pays the landlord his rent.
Second declension, masculine: inquilīnus, inquilīnī m. Someone living in a house that is not his own; most people in a big city rented.

The word, taken apart.
inquilīnus is built on incolō 'to live in', from in- and colō 'to dwell, to till' (Lewis & Short, from an older incolīnus). EtymDB lists it without a parent.

Lesson 2 of 5.
oppidānus, oppidānī — a townsman, an inhabitant of a town.

You'll want to know: oppidānus, oppidānī.
oppidānus, oppidānī — "a townsman, an inhabitant of a town".
Oppidānī in forō mercātōrēs exspectant. The townspeople wait for the merchants in the forum.
Second declension, masculine: oppidānus, oppidānī m., mostly plural. Also an adjective: of a country town.

The word, taken apart.
oppidānus is built on oppidum 'town' with the suffix -ānus 'belonging to' (EtymDB; Lewis & Short).

Lesson 3 of 5.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a tenant, then the Latin for a townsman.

You'll want to know: incola, incolae.
incola, incolae — "a resident, an inhabitant".
Incolae vīcī nōs benignē accipiunt. The people who live in the village receive us kindly.
First declension, masculine or feminine: incola, incolae. Like nauta and agricola, a first-declension noun for a person who is often a man.

The word, taken apart.
incola is built on incolō 'to live in' (EtymDB; Lewis & Short), which comes from in- and colō 'to dwell'.

Lesson 5 of 5.
habitātor, habitātōris — an occupant, a dweller.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a resident, then the Latin for to live in.

You'll want to know: habitātor, habitātōris.
habitātor, habitātōris — "an occupant, a dweller".
Domus vetus est, sed habitātōrēs eam amant. The house is old, but the people who live in it love it.
Third declension, masculine: habitātor, habitātōris m., from habitō 'to live'. English habitat comes from the same verb.

The word, taken apart.
habitātor is built on habitō 'to live, to dwell' with the suffix -tor 'one who' (Lewis & Short). habitō itself is a frequentative of habeō 'to have, to hold'.
