Spanish, chapter 440: La Fiesta Y El Año.
7 lessons. All 7 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 7.
la boda — the vows, taken as one thing.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say la fiesta, la iglesia and la familia. One day puts all three in the same place. Name it.

You'll want to know: la boda.
la boda — the wedding.
Vamos a una boda el sábado — we are going to a wedding on Saturday. Spanish uses the singular for the event and the plural las bodas for an anniversary of it: las bodas de plata are a silver wedding, twenty-five years on.
The b is soft between vowels — la BO-da, with the lips barely closing.

The word, taken apart.
boda looks like one solid piece and is not. It is Latin vōta, the vows — the plural of vōtum, a vow.
A wedding was named for what is said at it, and the name is plural because more than one vow is spoken. Then Spanish stopped hearing the plural and treated the whole word as a single feminine noun, which is why you can now say una boda, one-of-the-vows, without it sounding odd to anybody.
English kept the same root in vote, votive and devotion, all of them about something pledged.

Why it's said this way.
This is not a one-off. Latin had a third gender, neuter, whose plural ended in -a, and Spanish lost that gender entirely. The plurals were left standing with a feminine-looking ending and nothing to belong to, so the language took them as feminine singulars.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
Latin, a neuter plural: vōta, the vows. Spanish, a feminine singular: la boda.
Latin, a neuter plural: folia, the leaves. Spanish, a feminine singular: la hoja.
Latin, a neuter plural: ligna, the firewoods. Spanish, a feminine singular: la leña.
Every one of those is a countable plural read as a single mass or event, and that is the shape to look for: a Spanish feminine noun naming one thing made of many. It is a third way for a word to hide its pieces — not a false ending, not a false prefix, but a number the language forgot.
cerrado in the last chapter came apart the moment you looked. boda will not, and no amount of looking at modern Spanish would tell you why.

Lesson 2 of 7.
el cumpleaños — the years you have completed.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say ¿Cuántos años tienes? Say el aniversario. Say la boda. One day a year answers the first question, and it is not called an aniversario.

You'll want to know: el cumpleaños.
el cumpleaños — the birthday.
It ends in -s and it is singular: el cumpleaños, los cumpleaños. The word does not change; only the article tells you how many.
¡Feliz cumpleaños! — happy birthday. Mañana es mi cumpleaños — tomorrow is my birthday.

The word, taken apart.
Two words fused into one: cumple plus años.
cumplir is to complete, to fulfil, to carry something through to its end. Cumplo treinta — I complete thirty. So a cumple-años is the day the years get completed, and the -s you can hear is the plural of años trapped inside a word that is now singular.
That is why el cumpleaños looks plural and is not. It is not an exception to be memorised; it is a sentence that hardened.

Why it's said this way.
Spanish has two words for the same square on the calendar, and they disagree about what is happening.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
the word: el aniversario. what it says is happening: the year turns.
the word: el cumpleaños. what it says is happening: you complete years.
Aniversario is annus plus vertere, the year coming round again — the calendar does the work, and you stand still. Cumpleaños puts the work on you: the years are something you finish, one at a time, like a task.
Spanish keeps both and uses them for different things. A wedding gets an aniversario because a date recurs; a person gets a cumpleaños because a person is doing the ageing. That is not a rule anyone wrote down — it is the metaphor inside each word still deciding where it is welcome.

Lesson 3 of 7.
celebrar — to make a crowd of it.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say el cumpleaños, la fiesta and la música. You have the day, the party and the sound. Name the verb that puts them together.

You'll want to know: celebrar.
celebrar — to celebrate.
Celebramos su cumpleaños en casa — we are celebrating her birthday at home. Celebrarse is the form a notice uses: la reunión se celebra el martes — the meeting is held on Tuesday.
That second use is worth having early. On an official notice, celebrarse does not mean anybody is enjoying themselves; it means the event takes place.

The word, taken apart.
Latin celebrāre, from celeber — crowded, much-visited, thronged.
A celeber locus was a place people went to in numbers. To celebrate something was therefore to make a crowd of it, and only later to enjoy it. The idea underneath is attendance, not happiness.
English took the same root twice: celebrate, and celebrity — a person who has a crowd.

Why it's said this way.
That original meaning is exactly why a town-hall notice can say se celebra for an event nobody would call festive.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
se celebra la fiesta means the party takes place.
se celebra la reunión means the meeting takes place.
Both are celebrar, because both are a gathering. English would need two verbs here, and a reader who knows only the happy sense will misread the second one as a joke.
When you meet celebrarse on a notice, read it as is held. The crowd is what the word is really about, and a meeting has one.

Lesson 4 of 7.
disfrutar — to take the fruit of it.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say la fruta, descansar and celebrar. Two of those three share a prefix shape. Hold on to that.

You'll want to know: disfrutar.
disfrutar — to enjoy.
Disfruté mucho de la boda — I really enjoyed the wedding. ¡Que disfrutes! — enjoy yourself, said as you hand somebody a ticket or wave them off.
It usually takes de: disfrutar de algo. Dropping the de is common in speech and still reads as informal in writing.

The word, taken apart.
dis- plus frut-, and the second half is la fruta you already have. Both come from Latin frūctus, which meant produce, yield, the thing a plant gives you.
To disfrutar something is to take the fruit of it — to get the yield. English has the same picture in to enjoy the fruits of your labour, which most speakers hear as a metaphor and which Spanish has compressed into one verb.

Why it's said this way.
Now the trap, and it corrects the test you learned two chapters ago.
descansar is des- plus cansar, and the prefix undoes: it takes the tiredness away. It would be natural to read dis- in disfrutar the same way and land on to un-fruit, which would be the opposite of what the word means.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
word: descansar. what the prefix does: reverses — removes the tiredness.
word: disfrutar. what the prefix does: intensifies — takes the yield fully.
The piece-test still works: take dis- off and frutar stands, so the join is real. What the test does not tell you is what the join means. A prefix that usually reverses does not always reverse, and the only way to know is the word's sense, not its shape.
That is the honest limit of taking words apart. It shows you the seams; it does not read them for you.

Lesson 5 of 7.
el humor — the fluid you are in.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say reír, alegre and disfrutar. All three describe a person having a good time. Now name the thing that person is in.

You'll want to know: el humor.
el humor — mood, and also humour.
Estoy de buen humor — I am in a good mood. Está de mal humor — he is in a bad mood. Tiene mucho sentido del humor — she has a great sense of humour.
The h is silent here as everywhere in Spanish: el u-MOR. And note the preposition — Spanish says you are de a mood, of it, not in it.

The word, taken apart.
Latin ūmor meant one thing only: moisture, a fluid. English keeps that original sense in humid.
Medieval medicine held that a body ran on four fluids, and that whichever one you had most of set your temperament. Your humour was literally which liquid you were full of. The theory is long dead, and the word survived it — so when a Spanish speaker says estar de buen humor, the sentence still says to be of a good fluid, and nobody notices.

Why it's said this way.
One Spanish word covers what English splits into mood and humour, and the split happened in English rather than the join happening in Spanish.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
Spanish: estar de buen humor. English needs: to be in a good mood.
Spanish: sentido del humor. English needs: sense of humour.
English narrowed humour onto the funny sense and borrowed mood from its own older stock to carry the rest. Spanish never divided the work, so el humor still holds both — the state you are in, and the thing that makes other people laugh.
That is worth noticing whenever an English pair maps to one Spanish word. The usual cause is not that Spanish is vaguer; it is that English acquired a second word from somewhere and gave it half the job.

Lesson 6 of 7.
(boda, cumpleaños, celebrar, disfrutar, humor) — from cold.

Warm-up.
[pause 3 seconds]
Close the lessons before this one. Say all five: the wedding, the birthday, to celebrate, to enjoy, the mood.

Grammar Lens: three ways a word hides its pieces.
You now have a small collection of them, and they fail differently.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
the word: boda. what is hidden: a plural the language forgot.
the word: cumpleaños. what is hidden: a whole phrase, fused.
the word: disfrutar. what is hidden: a real join whose meaning misleads.
Boda cannot be recovered from modern Spanish at all — only Latin says why it is one word. Cumpleaños can: say it slowly and cumple and años fall apart in your mouth. Disfrutar comes apart cleanly and then lies about what the prefix is doing.
The piece-test tells you whether there is a join. It never tells you what the join means, and disfrutar is the word that proves it.

What you've built.
You can now be invited to something, say you went, and say how it was. That is the whole social round-trip, and it is what a message from a friend actually contains.
You also have the pair that will keep paying: celebrarse on a notice means is held, not is enjoyed, and el humor is one word where English keeps two.

Why it's said this way.
The two uses look identical and are told apart by one thing: whether the verb is reflexive.
Celebramos mi cumpleaños — we celebrate, somebody is doing the enjoying. Se celebrará la junta — it gets held, and no one is named, because a notice does not care who convenes a meeting. The reflexive drains the person out of the sentence, and what is left is the bare fact that the thing takes place.
So the answer to which of the two would you enjoy is not a matter of tone. It is in the grammar: the message has a we, and the notice does not.
Now answer the message the way a friend would: say yes, say what mood you are in, and say you will enjoy it.
