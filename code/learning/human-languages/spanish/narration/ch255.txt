Spanish, chapter 255: Vi, Di --- The Shortest Pasts.
2 lessons. The first lesson already needs your eyes or your hands, so save this one for when you have stopped.


Lesson 1 of 2.
vi — the shortest past in the language.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, because the lesson points at something written down. You can listen to all of it now and come back to the parts that need looking at once you have stopped.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
The -er past, first person: comí, with an accent, because the stress lands at the end.
[pause 1 second]
Now do the same to ver, whose stem is a single letter.

Grammar Lens: nothing left to accent.
ver becomes vi, viste, vio.
[pause 2 seconds]
Look at vi and vio: no accent, where comí and comió both had one.
[pause 2 seconds]
And the reason is not an exception. A written accent exists to say the beat is here, not where you would expect. A word of one syllable has nowhere else the beat could be, so there is nothing to correct. Spanish does not spend a mark on a choice that does not exist.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: comí, then vi, and hear which one needs the mark]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: vi, viste, vio]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[repeat that twice]
[question — say your answer, then pause 10 seconds]
I saw.
[question — say your answer, then pause 11 seconds]
He saw.
[question — say your answer, then pause 15 seconds]
Why does vi take no accent when comi does?

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 2 seconds]
Why does vi carry no accent? (One syllable — the beat has nowhere else to fall.)
[pause 2 seconds]
And what is "I saw"? (Vi.) Next: a second verb that does the same thing, and it is one you have never met.


Lesson 2 of 2.
di — the verb that changed families in the past.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Vi, viste, vio — bare, because one syllable has nowhere else to put the beat.
[pause 1 second]
A second verb does the same, and it is one you have never met.

Grammar Lens: dar, which behaves like an -er verb.
Dar means to give. It is an -ar verb — and in the preterite it flatly refuses to behave like one:
dar becomes di, diste, dio, dimos, disteis, dieron.
[pause 2 seconds]
Those are the -er endings, on an -ar verb, with the same one-syllable forms going bare. Ver does the same in the plural: vimos, visteis, vieron.
[pause 2 seconds]
Two verbs, one shared oddity, and now the last two named irregulars of the preterite are yours.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: di, diste, dio]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: vi, viste, vio, then di, diste, dio]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[repeat that twice]
[question — say your answer, then pause 11 seconds]
He gave.
[question — say your answer, then pause 13 seconds]
Dar is an -ar verb. Which endings does it take in the past?
[question — say your answer, then pause 15 seconds]
Which two preterite forms of dar carry no written accent, and why?

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 2 seconds]
Which endings does dar take in the past? (The -er ones, though it is an -ar verb.)
[pause 2 seconds]
And what does dar mean? (To give.)
