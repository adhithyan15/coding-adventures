Spanish, chapter 430: The Wardrobe, And The Word For Having No Work.
6 lessons. All 6 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 6.
estar en paro — the halt, rather than the missing job.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
You can say el trabajo. Try to say that somebody has none, and notice English reaches for a word built on employ that Spanish does not use in conversation.

You'll want to know: estar en paro.
estar en paro — to be unemployed, to be out of work.
Mi hermano está en paro. — "My brother is out of work."
El paro on its own is both the state and the benefit paid during it.

The word, taken apart.
El paro is built straight off parar, "to stop" — so it is a stoppage. Spanish names the situation after the halt, where English names it after the missing employment.
The verb has a startling history. Latin parare meant to make ready — the root inside preparar and reparar. The sense travelled in steps: to get ready became to stand ready, and to stand ready became to stand still.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
word: preparar. where on the journey: to make ready beforehand.
word: parar. where on the journey: to stand still.
word: el paro. where on the journey: the standing still itself.
So estar en paro is, underneath, being in the halt.

Grammar Lens: which verb, and which country.
It takes estar rather than ser, because it names a situation rather than a person, and situations change.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
where: Spain. the everyday word: el paro.
where: much of Latin America. the everyday word: el desempleo.
Desempleo is the formal word everywhere, and the one a newspaper prints. A person in Spain talking about their own life says paro.
The pair a form will ask you to choose between is short: tengo trabajo against estoy en paro.

Lesson 2 of 6.
los vaqueros — trousers named after the man who wore them.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
You can say el pantalón for trousers in general. Name the one kind of trousers almost everybody in the room is wearing, and see whether you have a word for it.

You'll want to know: los vaqueros.
los vaqueros — jeans.
Always plural, the way pantalones is, because the garment has two legs.

The word, taken apart.
La vaca is a cow, from Latin vacca. A vaquero is the man who looks after them — a cowherd, and in the Americas a cowboy.
Jeans arrived in Spain as the trousers those men wore, and Spanish named them after the wearer rather than the cloth. English went the other way: jeans comes from Genoa, the Italian port whose hard cotton the trousers were cut from, and denim from de Nîmes, the French town. Three languages, three different things noticed about the same garment.
Some regions say los tejanos instead — "the Texans" — which is the same thought arriving from a different direction.

Grammar Lens: garments that come in pairs.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
garment: los vaqueros. usual number: plural.
garment: los pantalones. usual number: plural.
garment: la camisa. usual number: singular.
Anything with two legs tends to arrive plural. Spanish tolerates el pantalón in the singular, and does not do the same for vaqueros — you will hear the plural every time.
The v and the b sound alike in Spanish, so vaquero opens with the same soft sound as bueno. Spelling keeps the Latin v; the mouth stopped distinguishing them centuries ago.

Lesson 3 of 6.
el jersey — an island, worn.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
You can say la camisa. Name what you put over it when the weather turns, and see whether the word is there.

You'll want to know: el jersey.
el jersey — the jumper, the pullover, the sweater.
Said the Spanish way: jer-SÉI, with the j as the throaty jota you already have from jamón.

The word, taken apart.
Jersey is an island in the Channel, between England and France. Its knitters were famous, and the heavy woollen garment they made took the island's name in English. Spanish borrowed the English word entire, island and all.
That makes it one of a small set of Spanish clothing words that name a place rather than a shape or a material:
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
garment: el jersey. the place: a Channel island.
garment: los tejanos. the place: Texas.
garment: la chaqueta. the place: a French peasant named Jacques.
Latin America mostly says el suéter instead — also English, borrowed a different way, and spelled to fit Spanish.

Grammar Lens: what a borrowed word has to learn.
A word taken from another language still has to obey the accent rules of this one. Jersey is stressed on its last syllable, and a word ending in -y takes no written accent, so the singular is bare.
The plural is where it shows:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
number: singular. form: el jersey.
number: plural. form: los jerséis.
The y turns into an i, the ending becomes -s, and now an accent is needed to hold the stress where it was. Spanish did not leave the word foreign; it made it follow the rules.

Lesson 4 of 6.
el bolso — a hide, a purse, and one vowel that changes the object.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
You have clothes and now a jumper. Name the thing carried beside the clothes rather than worn, and see whether you have it.

You'll want to know: el bolso.
el bolso — the handbag.
Not a shopping bag and not a pocket. A bolso is the bag carried on the arm or shoulder, and a shop will file it under accessories beside the shoes.

The word, taken apart.
Greek byrsa meant a hide, a piece of worked leather. Latin took it as bursa, and because a leather pouch is what money was carried in, the word came to mean purse.
From that one pouch:
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
word: el bolso. what it kept: the bag on your arm.
word: el bolsillo. what it kept: the little one sewn into your clothes.
word: la bolsa. what it kept: the bag, and the stock exchange.
English got bursar, reimburse and purse from the same leather.
The stock-exchange sense is the strangest and the best attested: traders in fifteenth-century Bruges met at the house of a family called Van der Buerse, and the name of the meeting place became the name of the market everywhere.

Grammar Lens: the vowel that picks the object.
Bolso and bolsa are one letter apart and name different things:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
word: el bolso. what it is: a handbag.
word: la bolsa. what it is: a bag, a carrier bag, the stock exchange.
Ask for una bolsa in a shop and you get something to carry the shopping home in. Ask for un bolso and you are shown the leather goods.
This is a pattern worth knowing rather than a single oddity: Spanish sometimes uses the -o / -a pair to separate two related objects rather than two genders of one. You have already met el pantalón keeping its own shape for the same kind of reason.

garment: el pantalón. already yours: yes.
garment: la falda. already yours: yes.
garment: la camisa. already yours: yes.
garment: el zapato. already yours: yes.
garment: los vaqueros. already yours: new.
garment: el jersey. already yours: new.
garment: el bolso. already yours: new.
Four were taught across earlier chapters and three arrived here. The list an exam works from is now covered end to end, which is what the three were for.

Grammar Lens: each one's own trap.

word: estar en paro. what to watch: takes estar, and paro means the halt.
word: los vaqueros. what to watch: plural every time.
word: el jersey. what to watch: plural jerséis, with a written accent.
word: el bolso. what to watch: la bolsa is a different object.

Grammar Lens: where each word came from.
[a table of 4 rows, read aloud]
word: el paro. named after: the stopping.
word: los vaqueros. named after: the cowherd.
word: el jersey. named after: an island.
word: el bolso. named after: a leather hide.
Three of the four are named after something other than themselves — a person, a place, a material. That is a habit worth noticing, because it is how a reader guesses at a word they have not met.

Lesson 6 of 6.
Synthesis — a shop window and a sentence about yourself.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Name the three garments this chapter added, before they appear in a price list.

Reading: el escaparate.
ROPA — REBAJAS.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
artículo: vaqueros. precio: 25 €.
artículo: jerséis. precio: 18 €.
artículo: bolsos. precio: 30 €.
Every line is readable, and one of them is teaching you something: the middle row is a plural, and the accent on jerséis is exactly where the singular did not need one.

How to answer.
Say what is cheapest, what is dearest, and what you would buy:
Los jerséis son los más baratos. Quiero unos vaqueros.
[pause 4 seconds]
Note unos vaqueros rather than un vaquero. A pair of trousers is a plural thing, so the article is plural too.

The exchange.
The other half of the chapter, in the two lines a form or an interview wants:
— ¿Trabajas? — No, estoy en paro.
Estoy, not soy. Being out of work is a situation, and Spanish keeps situations away from ser. The same answer in the positive is sí, tengo trabajo — the noun you have had since the professions chapter, finally with a negative to stand against.
