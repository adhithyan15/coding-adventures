# faceva freddo — the weather, made in the past

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[IT-LEX-ERA-01, IT-LEX-C-ERA-02] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say "the sun was out" the way last lesson built it. (*C'era il
sole.*) Then "I was tired". (*Ero stanco*, or *ero stanca*.)

## You'll want to know: faceva freddo
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[IT-LEX-FACEVA-01]; assesses=[IT-LEX-C73-AGG2-05, IT-LEX-C73-AGG2-04] -->

English says the weather **was** cold. Italian says it **made** cold:

> **Faceva freddo.** — It was cold.
> **Faceva caldo.** — It was hot.

*Faceva* is the imperfetto of a verb meaning "to make, to do", and for heat
and cold that is the verb Italian reaches for. It needs no subject: nobody is
doing the making.

Keep **era** for things. *Il caffè era freddo* — the coffee was cold — is about
the coffee. *Faceva freddo* is about the whole day.

And rain takes this chapter's plain recipe: *piovere* gives **pioveva**,
"it was raining". Put three of them together and you have a whole morning
before anything has happened in it:

> *Faceva freddo e pioveva. Non c'era il sole.*

## The phrase, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[] -->

**faceva** ← Latin *faciēbat*, "he was making", the imperfect of *facere*,
"to make, to do" — inherited, with the *-ēba-* worn to *-eva*, the same *b*
to *v* as *habēbat* and **aveva**. That *facere* is the root of English
**fact**, **factory** and **effect**. (Lewis & Short, under *facio*.)
