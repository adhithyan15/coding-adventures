\chapter{Greetings}
\label{ch:greetings}

The Gujarati greetings --- and, taught through them, how to read a script that is
Devanagari's sister, but bareheaded. Each lesson introduces the letters its word
needs. \emph{Practice lessons: \texttt{lessons/GU-C01-*.md}.}

\section{\gu{નમસ્તે} \emph{(namaste)} --- ``hello,'' and a script with no ceiling}
\label{lesson:namaste}

\begin{sounds}
\emph{(If you already read Gujarati, skim this.)} Gujarati runs \textbf{left to
right}; every consonant carries a built-in ``a'': \gu{ન} na, \gu{મ} ma, \gu{સ}
sa, \gu{ત} ta. A \emph{virama} joins consonants: \gu{સ} $+$ \gu{્} $+$ \gu{ત}
$\to$ \gu{સ્ત}. Above all: Gujarati has \textbf{no top line}. Devanagari, Bengali,
and Gurmukhi hang letters from a bar (the \emph{shirorekh\=a}); Gujarati cut it
away --- ``the headless script.''
\end{sounds}

\begin{cousinweb}
\gu{નમસ્તે} (\emph{namaste}) is Sanskrit \emph{namas} (``a bow'') $+$ \emph{te}
(``to you'') --- ``I bow to you,'' the very word Hindi and Sanskrit use. Gujarati
is \textbf{Indo-Aryan}, a granddaughter of Sanskrit, so it inherited the greeting
whole. (The everyday Hindu hello is often \gu{કેમ છો}? \emph{kem chho?}, ``how are
you?'' --- Chapter 3.)
\end{cousinweb}

\section{\gu{આભાર} \emph{(\=abh\=ar)} --- ``thank you,'' an obligation gladly owed}
\label{lesson:aabhaar}

\begin{cousinweb}
\gu{આભાર} (\emph{\=abh\=ar}, ``thanks'') is from Sanskrit \emph{\=abh\=ara} (``a
burden, an obligation''), \emph{\=a-} (``toward'') $+$ the root \emph{bh\d{r}}
(``to bear, carry''). To thank is to acknowledge you now \emph{bear an
obligation} --- the idea behind English \textbf{oblige} and Portuguese
\emph{obrigado}. That root \emph{bh\d{r}} is PIE *b\textsuperscript{h}er-, the
ancestor of English \textbf{bear} (to carry) and Latin \emph{ferre}.
\end{cousinweb}

\section{\gu{હા} / \gu{ના} \emph{(h\=a / n\=a)} --- ``yes'' and ``no''}
\label{lesson:haa-naa}

\begin{cousinweb}
\gu{હા} (\emph{h\=a}, ``yes'') and \gu{ના} (\emph{n\=a}, ``no''). The ``no'' rides
the ancient negative \emph{na-} --- the same PIE *ne behind English \textbf{no},
\textbf{not}, \textbf{none}, Latin \emph{n\=on}, Hindi \emph{nah\=\i\d{m}}. One
syllable spoken, in some form, from Iceland to Gujarat for five thousand years.
\end{cousinweb}

\section{\gu{સારું} \emph{(s\=arũ)} --- ``good,'' ``okay''}
\label{lesson:saarun}

\begin{cousinweb}
\gu{સારું} (\emph{s\=arũ}, ``good, fine, okay'') is from Sanskrit \emph{s\=ara}
(``essence, the best part'') --- the same \emph{s\=ara} behind Bengali's
\emph{s\=aramilla}. As ``okay/agreed'' it oils every conversation.
\end{cousinweb}

\begin{grammarlens}
\textbf{Three genders, on the adjective.} That -\gu{ું} (\emph{-ũ}) ending is
\textbf{neuter}. Gujarati, like Marathi, keeps three genders (Hindi kept two):
\emph{s\=aro} (m.) / \emph{s\=ar\=\i} (f.) / \emph{s\=arũ} (n.). Standing alone,
``okay'' is the neuter \emph{s\=arũ}.
\end{grammarlens}

\section{\gu{આવજો} \emph{(\=avjo)} --- ``goodbye'' (come again)}
\label{lesson:aavjo}

\begin{cousinweb}
\gu{આવજો} (\emph{\=avjo}) is the polite imperative of \gu{આવવું} (\emph{\=avvũ},
``to come'') --- literally ``\textbf{[please] come [again]}.'' A Gujarati never
signs off with ``I'm leaving''; the parting word is \emph{come}. It is the
subcontinent's ``promise of return'' (Tamil \emph{p\=oy varugi\d{r}\=e\d{n}},
Bengali \emph{\=ashi}, Hindi \emph{phir milenge}), here made a single warm word on
the verb \emph{to come}. (Familiarly: \gu{આવજે} \emph{\=avje}.)
\end{cousinweb}

\section{Recap}
\label{lesson:practice}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
Gujarati & & Meaning \\
\midrule
\gu{નમસ્તે} & \emph{namaste} & hello / goodbye \\
\gu{આભાર} & \emph{\=abh\=ar} & thank you \\
\gu{હા} / \gu{ના} & \emph{h\=a / n\=a} & yes / no \\
\gu{સારું} & \emph{s\=arũ} & good / okay \\
\gu{આવજો} & \emph{\=avjo} & goodbye (``come again'') \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Two things set Gujarati apart already: the \textbf{missing top line} (the
``headless'' script) and its \textbf{three genders} (\emph{s\=aro/s\=ar\=\i/s\=arũ}).
Roots banked: \emph{\=abh\=ar} (\emph{bh\d{r}} ``to bear,'' cousin of English
\emph{bear}), \emph{n\=a} (PIE *ne, English \emph{no}), \emph{\=avjo} (``come
again,'' from \emph{\=avvũ}). Next chapter: introducing yourself.
