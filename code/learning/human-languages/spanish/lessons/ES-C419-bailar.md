# bailar — and the verb that needs no article

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-MUSICA, ES-GRAMMAR-DEFINITE-ARTICLES] -->

[PAUSE 2s] You have met *me gusta **la** música*, which keeps an article
English throws away. Hold that thought — this lesson is the case where the rule
switches off.

## You'll want to know first
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[] -->

- [música](./ES-C413-musica.md) — the noun this verb belongs with, and the
  article rule this lesson contrasts against.

## Sounds you'll need
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[] -->

- `diphthong-ai` — **ai** is one syllable, *bai-*, close to English *bye*. →
  [reference](../pronunciation-reference.md)
- `b-soft` — the **b** is softer than an English *b*; the lips barely close.
- `r-tap` — the final **r** is a single flick of the tongue, not a growl.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-BAILAR]; assesses=[] -->

**bailar** — "to dance." An *-ar* infinitive, the most ordinary shape a Spanish
verb can have.

Root: Late Latin **ballare**, "to dance," from Greek *ballízein*. This root
travelled further into English than almost any other on this page:

- a **ball** — the *dancing* kind. A ballroom is a room for *ballare*.
- **ball**et — through Italian *balletto*, "a little dance."
- **ball**ad — Provençal *balada*, originally a **song you danced to**. That is
  why a ballad tells a story in verses: the verses were danced.

And now the trap worth knowing, because it looks like the strongest member of
the family and is not:

> A **ball** you *throw* is not related. That one is Germanic, a cousin of
> *balloon* and *bowl*. English has two unrelated words spelled *ball*, and only
> the one you dance at comes from *ballare*.

## Grammar Lens: where the article rule switches off
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-GUSTAR-07, ES-GRAMMAR-GUSTAR-INVERSION-09, ES-LEX-MUSICA] -->

Put the two halves of this chapter side by side:

> *Me gusta **la** música.* — I like music. **Article.**
> *Me gusta bailar.* — I like dancing. **No article.**

This is not an exception to memorise; it follows from what *gustar* does. It
needs a subject, and an infinitive is already one — *bailar* means "to dance,"
a whole action named. A **noun** needs its article to be spoken of in general; a
**verb** does not, because naming it is what the infinitive is for.

So: if what pleases you is a thing, it takes *el* or *la*. If what pleases you
is an action, the bare infinitive is the whole answer.

## Wrap-up Recall
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-BAILAR, ES-LEX-MUSICA, ES-GRAMMAR-DEFINITE-ARTICLES] -->

[PAUSE 3s] Why does *me gusta la música* take *la* while *me gusta bailar* takes
nothing? (A noun needs its article to mean the thing in general; an infinitive
already names the action.) Which English *ball* comes from this root — the dance
or the thing you throw? (The dance.)
