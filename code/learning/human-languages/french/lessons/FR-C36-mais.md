# mais — "but," and the Latin word for *more*

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[FR-LEX-ET-01, FR-LEX-OU-CONJ-02, FR-GRAM-SVO-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] *Et* adds. *Ou* offers a choice. Neither can say that the second half
disagrees with the first.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[FR-LEX-MAIS-03]; assesses=[FR-LEX-ET-01, FR-LEX-OU-CONJ-02, FR-LEX-BIEN-02, FR-LEX-CAFE-01] -->

> **mais** — but.

> **Ça va bien, mais je suis fatigué.** — I'm well, but I'm tired.

Said *may*: the **ai** is the *e* sound you met in *lait*, and the **s** is
silent.

**mais** is Latin **magis**, which meant **more** — and that is worth a moment,
because the same word is still doing the older job next door. Spanish inherited
*magis* as **más** and it still means *more*; French inherited it and drifted it
into *but*.

The road between the two is short. *"He is clever, more he is lazy"* is not far
from *"he is clever, but he is lazy"* — a *more* used to add a second, competing
fact turns into a contrast if you use it that way for long enough. One Latin
adverb, two daughters, two different parts of speech.
