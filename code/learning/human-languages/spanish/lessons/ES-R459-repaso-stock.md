## Grammar Lens: two everyday verbs, hidden in office words
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C459-STOCK-02, ES-LEX-C459-STOCK-03] -->

| office word | the everyday verb inside it |
|---|---|
| el **invent**ario | **venir** — *in-venire*, to come upon |
| **habit**ual | **haber** — *habēre*, to hold |

Both verbs are among the first things anybody learns in Spanish, and both are
so worn down that nothing on the surface connects them to these words. An
inventory is **a list of what was found**; something habitual is **a thing
held on to**.

This is what happens when a language takes the same Latin word twice. The
spoken copy shrinks until it is almost nothing — *he*, *vengo* — while the
written copy keeps its whole Latin shape.

## Grammar Lens: the notice frames, collected
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C459-STOCK-01, ES-LEX-C459-STOCK-02] -->

| frame | example |
|---|---|
| *cerrado por* + bare noun | *cerrado por inventario* |
| *fuera de* + bare noun | *fuera de plazo* |
| *por* + bare noun, as a cause | *por obras* |

No article after any of them. Spanish signs run on these three shapes, and
once you can see the frame you can read a notice you have never met by filling
in one word.
