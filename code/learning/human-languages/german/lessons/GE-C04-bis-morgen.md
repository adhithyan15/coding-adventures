# bis morgen — "see you tomorrow"

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GE-LEX-MORGEN-TOMORROW-01, GE-LEX-BIS-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Third ingredient, and the most definite of the three: a goodbye with
an actual date on it.

## You'll want to know: bis morgen
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[GE-LEX-BIS-MORGEN-01]; assesses=[GE-LEX-BIS-01, GE-LEX-MORGEN-TOMORROW-01, GE-GRAMMAR-CAPITAL-SPLITS-SENSE-02] -->

> **bis morgen** — "until tomorrow" → "see you tomorrow."

Said *biss MOR-gen*. Small **m**: here *morgen* is the adverb "tomorrow," not
the noun *der Morgen*, "the morning."

This is the one you say leaving work, or leaving a class you both attend again
the next day. Unlike *bis bald* and *bis später*, it makes a claim that can be
checked.

## Grammar Lens: the bis… family, complete
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GE-LEX-BIS-MORGEN-01, GE-LEX-BIS-BALD-01, GE-LEX-BIS-SPAETER-01, GE-LEX-BIS-01] -->

One preposition, three time words, three goodbyes — and the same three in
Spanish and French, built the same way:

| German | Means | Spanish / French twin |
|---|---|---|
| *bis bald* | soon | *hasta pronto* / *à bientôt* |
| *bis später* | later | *hasta luego* / *à plus tard* |
| *bis morgen* | tomorrow | *hasta mañana* / *à demain* |

You now hold German's whole farewell set: the formal *auf Wiedersehen*, the
casual *tschüss*, and these three soft partings.
