\section*{The three facts}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Consonants carry a built-in ``a.''} \ta{க} = ka, \ta{ம} = ma,
    \ta{ர} = ra.
  \item \textbf{A vowel sign changes that built-in vowel.} \ta{ா} = ā, \ta{ி}
    = i, \ta{ீ} = ī, \ta{ு} = u, \ta{ூ} = ū, \ta{ெ} = e, \ta{ே} = ē, \ta{ை} =
    ai, \ta{ொ} = o, \ta{ோ} = ō. (E.g.\ \ta{க} ka $+$ \ta{ி} $\to$ \ta{கி} ki.)
  \item \textbf{A \emph{puḷḷi} (the dot, \ta{்}) removes the built-in vowel.}
    \ta{க்} = a bare \emph{k}. A vowel-less consonant then leans into the next.
\end{enumerate}

\section*{What makes Tamil distinctive}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Three \emph{n}'s, three \emph{l}'s, two \emph{r}'s.} Place of
    articulation is written: dental \ta{ந} / alveolar \ta{ன} / retroflex
    \ta{ண}; \ta{ல ள ழ}; \ta{ர ற}. English fuses each set; Tamil keeps them
    apart, and they change meaning.
  \item \textbf{One letter, several sounds.} Tamil marks no voicing or
    aspiration: \ta{க} can be \emph{k}, \emph{g}, or \emph{h}; \ta{ச} can be
    \emph{s} or \emph{ch} --- \textbf{position} decides. The alphabet is small;
    the sound is read from context.
  \item \textbf{No Sanskrit-only letters in the core set.} Classical Tamil gets
    by with 18 consonants (a handful of \emph{grantha} letters --- \ta{ஜ},
    \ta{ஷ}, \ta{ஸ}, \ta{ஹ} --- are added only to spell loanwords).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Tamil in the Dravidian family}

Tamil is the oldest-attested Dravidian language, and the one most protective of
its native word-stock. Its sisters --- Malayalam (closest), then Kannada and
Telugu --- share the grammar and many roots but borrowed far more freely from
Sanskrit. The lessons trace, word by word, which Tamil words are home-grown and
which its neighbours replaced.
