Hindi, chapter 15: Water and Bread.
3 lessons. You can do the first 1 of them in the car; after that you will want to have stopped.


Lesson 1 of 3.
पानी, रोटी (pānī, roṭī) — water (the "drinkable thing") and bread.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Hindi's everyday word for water isn't the ancient Sanskrit word you might expect — it names water by what you do with it, not by what it is.

You'll want to know — पानी — "the drinkable thing".
पानी (pānī) = "water." Sanskrit actually has older, more "basic" words for water — आप् (āp) and जल (jala) among them — but Hindi's everyday word descends from neither. It comes from Sanskrit पानीय (pānīya), built on the verb पा (pā), "to drink," plus a suffix meaning roughly "fit for." So pānīya, and Hindi's pānī after it, literally means "the drinkable thing" — water defined by its use, not named as a substance in its own right.

You'll want to know — रोटी — bread, honestly uncertain in origin.
रोटी (roṭī) = "bread, flatbread" — the everyday word for the flatbreads (roti, chapati-style breads) central to North Indian meals. Be honest: unlike pānī, roṭī's deeper history is not cleanly traced back through Sanskrit the way many everyday Hindi words are. It comes through Prakrit रोट्ट (roṭṭa, "a kind of bread") from Sanskrit रोटिका (roṭikā); beyond that, one proposal (Turner's Indo-Aryan dictionary) tentatively links it to a root meaning "to strike / to press or crush" — perhaps evoking the pounding and flattening of dough — but this is genuinely uncertain, one guess among scholars rather than a settled chain. Worth knowing honestly rather than forcing a confident derivation that isn't there.

(No — it traces to Prakrit roṭṭa < Sanskrit roṭikā, but beyond that the deeper root is genuinely uncertain — one proposal links it to "striking/pressing" dough, not a settled chain.)

Before the new one: ◌ँ — what does it do?
[pause 2 seconds]
One character this time. Just one — and you have been saying it for pages without knowing which mark on the page it was.

Script you'll notice: ष.
ष — ssa.
It is a consonant, and in this script a consonant is never bare: it comes with an a already in it. So it is not ss, it is ssa.
You already say these, and every one of them has ष (ssa) somewhere inside it:
वसंत ग्रीष्म वर्षा शरद् हेमंत शिशिर — SIX traditional seasons, not four.

Writing: ष — copy what you see.
Put your pen on ष (ssa) and follow its line. Copy the shape you can see — slowly, and larger than it is printed.
This book does not yet tell you where to start the character or which way to travel. That is a real thing, taught with real variation from school to school, and it is not written down here until it can be written down with a source. Copying what is in front of you needs no such source, and it is how the shape gets into your hand in the meantime.

Lesson 3 of 3.
ढ (ḍha) — one lift, and the last one to come early.

Before we start: this one needs your hands, so it is not a driving lesson. You will want a pen and something to write on and your eyes, for letter shapes on the page. You can listen to everything else now — leave the sections called Script you'll notice: ढ, Script: the shortest build, and the last one early and Guided Practice until you have stopped, and I will say so again when we reach it.

Warm-up.
[pause 1 second]
Before the new one: घ — how many pen lifts did it take?
[pause 2 seconds]
This one takes fewer than any character in the book.

Script you'll notice: ढ.
ढ — ḍha, curled back and breathy.
What it is made of:
a descending stem joined to a broad outer bowl and closed inner loop.
the top shirorekhā.
Two parts, where most characters have three or four.

Script: the shortest build, and the last one early.
One pen lift. Everything below the headline is a single unbroken run — stem, shoulder, outer bowl and inner loop without the pen leaving the paper — and then the headline goes on top. Nothing else in this book is drawn in two strokes.
It is also the last letter you will meet before the words that use it. From here on, every remaining character will be one you have already been reading without being shown, and each of those lessons will say so.

Writing: ढ.
1. start at the stem's headline junction, descend, turn left through the upper shoulder, sweep counterclockwise around the broad outer bowl, and continue counterclockwise around the closed inner loop without lifting.
2. lift and draw the top shirorekhā left-to-right.
Pen lifts: 1. The pen comes up once.
Verified two-stroke teaching form fitted to the bundled printed outline; everyday handwriting may open the inner loop.
This is one attested teaching order and not a national standard — handwriting here is taught with school-to-school variation. Source: Opiaterein, ‘Deva-ढ (ḍha)-order.gif’, strokes 1–2, Wikimedia Commons, 10 May 2009.
