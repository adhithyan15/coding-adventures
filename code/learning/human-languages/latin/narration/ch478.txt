Lesson 1 of 6.
ōpiliō, ōpiliōnis — a shepherd (of sheep).

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to pasture, then the Latin for a pasture.

You'll want to know: ōpiliō, ōpiliōnis.
ōpiliō, ōpiliōnis — "a shepherd (of sheep)".
Ōpiliō ovēs in colle servat. The shepherd watches over the sheep on the hill.
Third declension, masculine: ōpiliō, ōpiliōnis m., a word of comedy and the farm writers. Pāstor is the general word.

The word, taken apart.
ōpiliō is usually traced to ovis 'sheep' (Lewis & Short, from an older ovilio), so a sheep-keeper; the second part is not settled. EtymDB lists it without a parent.

Lesson 2 of 6.
bubulcus, bubulcī — an ox-driver, a herdsman.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a pasture, then the Latin for a shepherd.

You'll want to know: bubulcus, bubulcī.
bubulcus, bubulcī — "an ox-driver, a herdsman".
Bubulcus cum bōbus ad agrum it. The ox-driver goes to the field with the oxen.
Second declension, masculine: bubulcus, bubulcī m. The man who drives the plough oxen.

The word, taken apart.
bubulcus is built on būbulus 'of cattle', from bōs, bovis 'ox' (EtymDB; Lewis & Short), from PIE gʷṓws 'cow'. English cow is a Germanic cousin.

Lesson 3 of 6.
caulae, caulārum — a sheepfold, a pen; an opening.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a shepherd, then the Latin for an ox-driver.

You'll want to know: caulae, caulārum.
caulae, caulārum — "a sheepfold, a pen; an opening".
Lupus prope caulās vagātur. A wolf prowls near the sheepfold.
First declension, feminine plural: caulae, caulārum f. First a hole or passage, then a fenced pen.

The word, taken apart.
caulae is usually traced to cavus 'hollow', through an older cavile (Lewis & Short, after an ancient grammarian); EtymDB gives no parent, so the line is hedged.

Lesson 4 of 6.
tondeō, tondēre — to shear, to clip; to shave.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for an ox-driver, then the Latin for a sheepfold.

You'll want to know: tondeō, tondēre.
tondeō, tondēre — "to shear, to clip; to shave".
Vēre agricolae ovēs tondent. In spring the farmers shear the sheep.
tondeō, tondēre, totondī, tōnsum, with a reduplicated perfect. Tōnsor 'barber' comes from it, and English tonsure.

The word, taken apart.
tondeō is inherited and is usually traced to PIE temh₂- 'to cut' (EtymDB; Lewis & Short compare Greek temnō 'to cut'). English tonsure comes from its participle tōnsus.

Lesson 5 of 6.
mulgeō, mulgēre — to milk.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a sheepfold, then the Latin for to shear.

You'll want to know: mulgeō, mulgēre.
mulgeō, mulgēre — "to milk".
Ancilla mane caprās mulget. In the morning the maid milks the goats.
mulgeō, mulgēre, mulsī, mulsum or mulctum. English milk is a Germanic cousin, and emulsion comes from ēmulgēre.

The word, taken apart.
mulgeō is inherited from PIE h₂melǵ- 'to milk' (EtymDB); German melken and English milk are Germanic cousins (Lewis & Short). English emulsion comes from the compound ēmulgēre.
