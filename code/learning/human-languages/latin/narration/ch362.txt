Latin, chapter 362: Proving Someone Wrong.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
refellō, refellere — to prove false, to refute.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to show, then the Latin for to strengthen.

You'll want to know: refellō, refellere.
refellō, refellere — "to prove false, to refute".
Facile erat eius verba refellere. It was easy to refute his words.
refellō, refellere, refellī, with no supine. Built on fallō, to deceive: to show up a deception.

The word, taken apart.
refellō is built from re- "back" and fallō "to deceive" (EtymDB; Lewis & Short): to turn a deception back on itself.

Lesson 2 of 5.
refūtō, refūtāre — to refute; to drive back.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to strengthen, then the Latin for to prove false.

You'll want to know: refūtō, refūtāre.
refūtō, refūtāre — "to refute; to drive back".
Hanc opīniōnem nōn difficile est refūtāre. This opinion is not hard to refute.
refūtō, refūtāre, refūtāvī, refūtātum. First to beat back an attack, then an argument. English refute.

The word, taken apart.
refūtō is built from re- "back" and an old verb fūtō, which EtymDB glosses "to beat"; Lewis & Short connect it instead with fundō "to pour", so its deeper origin is not settled. English refute comes from it.

redarguō, redarguere — "to disprove, to show to be wrong".
Testis falsum dīxit, sed iūdex eum redarguit. The witness told a lie, but the judge proved him wrong.
redarguō, redarguere, redarguī. Built on arguō: to prove the other side wrong, back to its face.

The word, taken apart.
redarguō is built from red- "back" and arguō "to make clear, to prove" (Lewis & Short): to prove a claim back against the one who made it.

Lesson 4 of 5.
convincō, convincere — to prove wrong, to convict; to prove.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to refute, then the Latin for to disprove.

You'll want to know: convincō, convincere.
convincō, convincere — "to prove wrong, to convict; to prove".
Eum mendāciī convīcērunt. They convicted him of lying.
convincō, convincere, convīcī, convictum. The charge goes in the genitive: mendāciī convincere, to convict of lying. In Latin it proves, it does not persuade. English convict and convince.

The word, taken apart.
convincō is built from con- and vincō "to conquer" (EtymDB; Lewis & Short): to beat an opponent completely in argument. English convict and convince both come from it.

Lesson 5 of 5.
coniectūra, coniectūrae — a guess, a conjecture; an inference.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to disprove, then the Latin for to prove wrong.

You'll want to know: coniectūra, coniectūrae.
coniectūra, coniectūrae — "a guess, a conjecture; an inference".
Hoc nōn sciō, sed coniectūrā tantum dīcō. I do not know this; I say it only by guesswork.
coniectūra, coniectūrae f. Built on coniciō, to throw together: pieces thrown together to make a guess. English conjecture.

The word, taken apart.
coniectūra is built on coniciō "to throw together, to infer" (EtymDB; Lewis & Short), from con- and iaciō "to throw". English conjecture comes from it.
