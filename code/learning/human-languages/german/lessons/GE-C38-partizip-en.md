# gegangen — the other half of the past

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GE-GRAMMAR-PARTIZIP-GE-T-01, GE-ETYMON-GE-PRAEFIX-01, GE-LEX-GELERNT-01, GE-GRAMMAR-PERFEKT-HABEN-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say **ich habe gelernt**. The participle is wrapped in **ge-** and
ends in **-t**, which the past chapter taught. Now say **ich bin gegangen** —
which you have also said, and which ends in something else entirely.

## Grammar Lens: the second family ends in -en
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[GE-GRAMMAR-PARTIZIP-GE-EN-01]; assesses=[GE-GRAMMAR-PARTIZIP-GE-T-01, GE-GRAMMAR-PERFEKT-SEIN-01, GE-GRAMMAR-STRONG-VOWEL-09] -->

> German builds participles two ways. The weak family wraps the stem in **ge-**
> and **-t**. The strong family wraps it in **ge-** and **-en**, and very often
> changes the vowel as well.

> **gehen → gegangen** · **sehen → gesehen** · **lesen → gelesen** ·
> **schreiben → geschrieben**

The **ge-** is the same unstressed wrapper in both families, so half of every
participle in the language is already yours. What the strong family adds is the
**-en** ending and a vowel that may move — and the moving vowel is the same
strong-verb behaviour that gave you *du liest* and *du siehst*.

## The word, taken apart: English kept both families too
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GE-GRAMMAR-PARTIZIP-GE-EN-01, GE-GRAMMAR-PARTIZIP-GE-T-01, GE-LEX-GELERNT-01] -->

English split its participles the same way, into the same two shapes, and every
English speaker sorts them without thinking:

| weak | strong |
|---|---|
| walk**ed**, learn**ed**, ask**ed** | go**ne**, see**n**, writt**en**, giv**en** |

That *-en* on *given* and *written* is German's *-en* on *gegeben* and
*geschrieben* — the same ending, in the same job, inherited rather than borrowed.
English then lost the *ge-* wrapper, which German kept, so the German participle
is the fuller survival of a shape both languages had.

## Wrap-up Recall
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GE-GRAMMAR-PARTIZIP-GE-EN-01, GE-GRAMMAR-PARTIZIP-GE-T-01, GE-ETYMON-GE-PRAEFIX-01, GE-GRAMMAR-PERFEKT-SEIN-01] -->

[PAUSE 3s] What are the two participle endings? (***-t*** for the weak family,
***-en*** for the strong.) What do both families share? (**The *ge-* wrapper**,
never stressed.) Which English participles end in *-en*? (***Gone, seen, written,
given*** — the same family, the same ending.)
