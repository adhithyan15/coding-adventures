Lesson 1 of 5.
pulverulentus, pulverulenta, pulverulentum — dusty, covered with dust.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a wheel rut, then the Latin for to spread.

You'll want to know: pulverulentus, pulverulenta, pulverulentum.
pulverulentus, pulverulenta, pulverulentum — "dusty, covered with dust".
Aestāte via pulverulenta est, et viātōrēs aquam quaerunt. In summer the road is dusty, and travellers look for water.
An adjective of three endings, from pulvis, pulveris 'dust' with the ending -ulentus 'full of'. English pulverize is a cousin.

The word, taken apart.
pulverulentus is built on pulvis, pulveris 'dust' with the suffix -ulentus 'full of' (EtymDB; Lewis & Short). English pulverize comes from the same noun.

Post pluviam via lutulenta est, et plaustra lentē eunt. After the rain the road is muddy, and the wagons go slowly.

Lesson 3 of 5.
vestīgium, vestīgiī — a footprint, a track; a trace.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for dusty, then the Latin for muddy.

You'll want to know: vestīgium, vestīgiī.
vestīgium, vestīgiī — "a footprint, a track; a trace".
In lutō vestīgia equōrum et mūlōrum sunt. In the mud there are the footprints of horses and mules.
Second declension, neuter: vestīgium, vestīgiī n. Eōdem vestīgiō, on the same spot. English vestige comes from it.

The word, taken apart.
The origin of vestīgium is not settled: Lewis & Short call the etymology doubtful, and EtymDB gives no parent. English vestige comes from it, and investigate from the related verb investīgāre.

Lesson 4 of 5.
lassus, lassa, lassum — weary, tired out.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for muddy, then the Latin for a footprint.

You'll want to know: lassus, lassa, lassum.
lassus, lassa, lassum — "weary, tired out".
Post longum iter viātor lassus in caupōnā sedet. After a long journey the weary traveller sits in the inn.
An adjective of three endings, close to fessus. English lassitude comes from the noun lassitūdō.

The word, taken apart.
lassus is inherited and is usually traced to PIE led- 'slow, weary' (EtymDB); Lewis & Short call its origin doubtful. English lassitude comes from the Latin noun lassitūdō.

Lesson 5 of 5.
dēfatīgō, dēfatīgāre — to wear out, to tire out.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a footprint, then the Latin for weary.

You'll want to know: dēfatīgō, dēfatīgāre.
dēfatīgō, dēfatīgāre — "to wear out, to tire out".
Via longa equōs dēfatīgat. The long road wears the horses out.
dēfatīgō, dēfatīgāre, dēfatīgāvī, dēfatīgātum. Often passive: dēfatīgātus 'worn out'. The simple verb fatīgō gives English fatigue.

The word, taken apart.
dēfatīgō is built from dē- 'down, completely' and fatīgō 'to tire, to weary' (EtymDB; Lewis & Short). English fatigue comes, through French, from fatīgō.
