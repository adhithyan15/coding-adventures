\chapter{Colours}
\label{ch:colours}

Four basic colours --- \emph{noir}, \emph{blanc}, \emph{rouge}, \emph{bleu} ---
and a surprise inside them: half of them are not Latin at all. Two walked down
from Rome; two walked in from the Germanic north, against the usual direction of
traffic. \emph{Practice lessons: \texttt{lessons/FR-C13-*.md}.}

\section{\emph{noir}, \emph{blanc} --- one inherited, one borrowed}
\label{lesson:noir-blanc}

Two colours, two completely different life stories. One walked straight down
from Latin. The other was borrowed from the Germanic neighbours --- and that
direction, Germanic \emph{into} Romance, is the opposite of what usually
happens.

\begin{cousinweb}
\textbf{noir} (``black'') $\leftarrow$ Latin \emph{niger}. No surprises:
\emph{niger} $\to$ \emph{negro} / \emph{nero} in the sister languages, and into
English in \textbf{denigrate} (``to blacken someone's name'').
\end{cousinweb}

\begin{cousinweb}
\textbf{blanc} (``white'') is the imported one. You'd expect Latin \emph{albus}
(``white''). It's not there. French says \emph{blanc}, from Frankish
\emph{*blank} --- a Germanic word meaning ``\textbf{shining, gleaming}.'' The
Franks who gave France its name gave it its word for white too.\\[3pt]
Why did \emph{albus} lose? Probably because \emph{blank} was \emph{vivid}: not
just ``white'' but ``bright, flashing.'' Colour words often get replaced by the
more dramatic option.
\end{cousinweb}

\emph{Albus} didn't die --- it stopped being a colour:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
Word & Meaning & The white inside \\
\midrule
\emph{aube} & dawn & the sky going \emph{white} \\
\emph{aub\'{e}pine} & hawthorn & the ``white thorn'' \\
\textbf{album} (Eng.) & album & a \emph{blank white} tablet \\
\textbf{albino} (Eng.) & albino & white-skinned \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

So Latin's white is still all around you --- just never where you'd look for it.

\begin{grammarlens}
\textbf{Say them.} \emph{noir}, \emph{blanc}. Then put them to work on a noun
you already have: \emph{le vin blanc}, \emph{le vin rouge}. And hold the pairing
in your head --- \emph{noir} from Latin, \emph{blanc} from the Franks. Say
\emph{l'aube} and hear the old Latin white still sitting inside it.
\end{grammarlens}

\section[\emph{rouge} and \emph{bleu}]{\emph{rouge}, \emph{bleu} --- an ancient word and a borrowed one}
\label{lesson:rouge-bleu}

The last section gave one inherited colour and one borrowed. This one repeats
the pattern --- and shows that French's borrowing from Germanic wasn't a
one-off.

\begin{cousinweb}
\textbf{rouge} (``red'') $\leftarrow$ Latin \emph{rubeus} (``reddish''). But
\emph{rubeus} itself descends from a root so old it predates Latin:
Proto-Indo-European \emph{*h$_1$rewd$^h$-}. Its children are everywhere.
\end{cousinweb}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}
\toprule
Language & Word \\
\midrule
Latin & \emph{ruber}, \emph{rubeus} $\to$ \emph{rouge}, \textbf{ruby}, \textbf{rubric} \\
English & \textbf{red}, \textbf{rust}, \textbf{ruddy} \\
German & \emph{rot} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

So \emph{rouge} and \textbf{red} are not lookalikes --- they are \emph{actual
cousins}, separated for maybe five thousand years. Red is one of humanity's
oldest named colours.

\begin{cousinweb}
\textbf{bleu} (``blue'') $\leftarrow$ Frankish \emph{*bl\=ao}. Another Germanic
word, borrowed exactly like \emph{blanc}. And then something neat happened:
English took \emph{bleu} from French --- so English \textbf{blue} is a Germanic
word that went to France, changed clothes, and came back. German kept the
original at home: \emph{blau}.
\end{cousinweb}

\begin{culture}
French also has \emph{azur} (from the same source as Spanish \emph{azul},
ultimately Arabic \emph{l\=azaward}, ``lapis lazuli'') --- the poetic blue of
the sky, and the \emph{C\^{o}te d'Azur}.
\end{culture}

The pattern of the chapter, laid out:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}
\toprule
Colour & Source \\
\midrule
\emph{noir} & Latin \emph{niger} \\
\emph{blanc} & Germanic \emph{*blank} \\
\emph{rouge} & Latin \emph{rubeus} (ancient PIE root) \\
\emph{bleu} & Germanic \emph{*bl\=ao} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Half of French's basic colour vocabulary walked in from the north.

\begin{culture}
Say them in the order of the flag: \emph{bleu, blanc, rouge}. All three are now
yours --- and two of the three are loanwords. The French tricolour is, quietly,
two-thirds Germanic.
\end{culture}

\begin{grammarlens}
\textbf{Practice.} Say \emph{rouge, bleu}. Say \emph{le vin rouge} --- the wine
you already know, now with its colour. Say the cousins out loud in a row:
\emph{rouge} $\cdot$ \textbf{red} $\cdot$ \emph{rot}. Next chapter: the verb
\emph{avoir}, and something French does with it that English would never do.
\end{grammarlens}
