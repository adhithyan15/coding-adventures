Portuguese, chapter 266: Mais Do Que --- More Than, and How Long.
3 lessons. All 3 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 3.
mais … do que — more than.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Answer tudo bem? with a shrug. (Mais ou menos.) Literally, "more or less".
[pause 1 second]
Those two words, mais and menos, are the whole of this lesson's new grammar.

You'll want to know: mais … do que.
To compare two things, put mais, "more", in front of the adjective and do que, "than", in front of the second thing:
O comboio é mais rápido do que o autocarro. — The train is faster than the bus. O avião é mais caro do que o comboio. — The plane is dearer than the train. És mais alto do que eu. — You are taller than me.
English often builds the comparison into the adjective, faster, taller; Portuguese builds it with mais. After do que a person stays in the plain form, eu, tu: do que eu. You will also hear plain que, mais rápido que o autocarro, in Portugal and in Brazil alike.
Its opposite is menos, "less": O autocarro é menos caro do que o comboio — the bus is less expensive than the train.

Grammar Lens: o mais, and two that refuse.
For the most of all, keep mais and give the noun its article:
o comboio mais rápido — the fastest train a cidade mais bonita — the most beautiful city.
The adjective still comes after the noun, and the article does the work of English -est. Who it is the most among comes with de: a cidade mais bonita de Portugal.
A few adjectives refuse mais, and two of them come first. Bom becomes melhor, "better", and mau becomes pior, "worse": o comboio é melhor do que o autocarro. You have heard both already, inside melhorar, "to get better", and piorar, "to get worse".

The word, taken apart.
mais is inherited from Latin magis, "more" (EtymDB 2.1), and so is the mas of chapter two hundred and sixty: "more" became "rather", and "rather" became "but". menos has no parent in EtymDB 2.1; it is usually traced to Latin minus, "less" (Lewis & Short), which English kept whole as minus. melhor is inherited from Latin melior, "better" (EtymDB 2.1).

Lesson 2 of 3.
há três anos que — how long, and still going.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say "the train is faster than the bus". (O comboio é mais rápido do que o autocarro.) Then "three years ago". (Há três anos.)
[pause 1 second]
An experience has a length, and Portuguese measures it with that same há.

You'll want to know: há três anos que.
Há três anos que estudo português. — I have been studying Portuguese for three years. Estudo português há três anos. — The same, the other way round.
Listen to the verb. English says have been studying; Portuguese says estudo, the plain present, because the studying is still going on.
Now set it beside the há you learned for "ago". With an event in the past, the same three words mean something else:
Fui a Lisboa há três anos. — I went to Lisbon three years ago.
So the verb decides: with the present, it is still going; with the perfeito, it happened that long ago.

Grammar Lens: desde, and durante.
desde, "since", which you have, names the starting point instead of the length, and it takes the present for the same reason:
Moro aqui desde janeiro. — I have lived here since January.
When the stretch has ended, the verb goes into the perfeito and the length can take durante, "for, during":
Morei em Lisboa durante dois anos. — I lived in Lisbon for two years. (and then I left).
One English word, for, splits in two. Há … que with the present: still going on. Durante with the perfeito: over. In Brazil you will also hear faz três anos que for the first.

The phrase, taken apart.
há is a form of haver, inherited from Latin habēre, "to have" (EtymDB 2.1), so há três anos que estudo is, word for word, "it has three years that I study". desde is a compound of des, itself Latin dē plus ex, and de (EtymDB 2.1): "from, out of, from". durante is inherited from Latin dūrāns, "lasting" (EtymDB 2.1), built on dūrus, "hard": what lasts is what holds hard.

[pause 3 seconds]

Lesson 3 of 3.
Review — describe an experience.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say "I have been studying Portuguese for three years". (Há três anos que estudo português.)

What you've built.
Two chapters gave you the four things that turn a fact into an experiência: how it felt, what you hope, how it compared, and how long it went on.
Há três anos que estudo português. Primeiro era difícil e eu estava nervoso, mas agora estou contente. Acho que o português é mais fácil do que a matemática. Espero falar bem.
[pause 2 seconds]
Count the moves: a length still running, há três anos que; a feeling in the past, estava nervoso, against one now, estou contente; a comparison, mais fácil do que a matemática, marked as an opinion; a hope, espero falar.

And when you tell the whole experience to someone, which verb counts it out? (Contar, from Latin computāre.)
