# wenn, falls — if

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GE-GRAMMAR-WENN-IMMER-02, GE-GRAMMAR-ALS-EINMAL-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Every time I was in Berlin, I visited my grandmother. Say it
with *wenn*. (*Wenn ich in Berlin war, besuchte ich meine Großmutter.*)

[PAUSE 1s] That *wenn* was "whenever". The same word also means "if".

## Grammar Lens: wenn for if
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[GE-GRAMMAR-WENN-FALLS-01]; assesses=[GE-GRAMMAR-VERB-LETZT-01, GE-GRAMMAR-IMPERATIV-DU-01, GE-LEX-ZEIT-01, GE-LEX-C144-VERB144-02, GE-LEX-C192-THING192-04] -->

German's everyday "if" is **wenn**, and its verb goes to the end as
before:

> *Wenn du Zeit* **hast**, *komm!* — If you have time, come!

English speakers hear "when" in it at first, and often that is fine: "if"
and "whenever" sit close together.

When you need it to be only "if", "in case it happens", German has
**falls**:

> *Falls der Zug Verspätung* **hat**, *rufe ich dich an.* — If the train is
> late, I'll call you.

*Falls* does not mean "when". It is a little more careful than *wenn*, and
it suits a plan with a fallback in it. Both send the verb to the end.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[GE-ETYMON-FALLS-FALL-02]; assesses=[GE-LEX-LINKS-01, GE-LEX-C140-VERB140-05] -->

**falls** is built on **der Fall**, "the case", which EtymDB 2.1 traces
through Middle High German *val* to Old High German *val*, "a fall"; it
belongs with the verb **fallen**. The *-s* is the old adverb ending of
*rechts* and *links*, so *falls* is "in case", by the usual account grown
out of phrases like *im Falle*, "in the case".

**wenn** is the old twin of *wann*, from chapter two hundred and
fifty-two.
