Latin, chapter 444: Dead calm, Calm weather, Peninsula, Of the shore, To sail out.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
malacia, malaciae — a dead calm (at sea).

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a strait, then the Latin for to sail past.

You'll want to know: malacia, malaciae.
malacia, malaciae — "a dead calm (at sea)".
Malacia est; nāvēs in marī stant et nautae ventum exspectant. There is a dead calm; the ships stand still on the sea and the sailors wait for wind.
First declension, feminine: malacia, malaciae f. No wind at all, the sailor's other trouble.

The word, taken apart.
malacia was borrowed from Greek malakiā 'softness, slackness' (EtymDB; Lewis & Short), from malakos 'soft': a sea gone slack.

Lesson 2 of 5.
tranquillitās, tranquillitātis — calm weather, a calm; stillness.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to sail past, then the Latin for a dead calm.

You'll want to know: tranquillitās, tranquillitātis.
tranquillitās, tranquillitātis — "calm weather, a calm; stillness".
Post tempestātem tranquillitās venit. After the storm calm weather comes.
Third declension, feminine: tranquillitās, tranquillitātis f. Also calm of mind. English tranquillity comes from it.

The word, taken apart.
tranquillitās is built on the adjective tranquillus 'calm, still' with the suffix -itās (EtymDB; Lewis & Short). English tranquillity comes from it.

Lesson 3 of 5.
paenīnsula, paenīnsulae — a peninsula.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a dead calm, then the Latin for calm weather.

You'll want to know: paenīnsula, paenīnsulae.
paenīnsula, paenīnsulae — "a peninsula".
Oppidum in paenīnsulā est; mare ā tribus partibus est. The town is on a peninsula; the sea is on three sides.
First declension, feminine: paenīnsula, paenīnsulae f., also spelled pēnīnsula. Almost an island. English peninsula comes from it.

The word, taken apart.
paenīnsula is built from paene 'almost' and īnsula 'island' (EtymDB; Lewis & Short). English peninsula comes from it.

Lesson 4 of 5.
lītoreus, lītorea, lītoreum — of the shore, on the beach.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for calm weather, then the Latin for a peninsula.

You'll want to know: lītoreus, lītorea, lītoreum.
lītoreus, lītorea, lītoreum — "of the shore, on the beach".
Līberī harēnam lītoream in saccōs pōnunt. The children put beach sand into sacks.
An adjective of three endings, from lītus, lītoris 'shore'. English littoral comes from the related lītorālis.

The word, taken apart.
lītoreus is built on lītus, lītoris 'shore' with the adjective suffix -eus (EtymDB; Lewis & Short). English littoral comes from the related adjective lītorālis.

Lesson 5 of 5.
ēnāvigō, ēnāvigāre — to sail out, to sail away; to sail across.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a peninsula, then the Latin for of the shore.

You'll want to know: ēnāvigō, ēnāvigāre.
ēnāvigō, ēnāvigāre — "to sail out, to sail away; to sail across".
Nāvis ē portū ēnāvigat et in altum it. The ship sails out of the harbour and goes out to sea.
ēnāvigō, ēnāvigāre, ēnāvigāvī, ēnāvigātum. With an accusative, to sail right across: mare ēnāvigāre.

The word, taken apart.
ēnāvigō is built from ē(x)- 'out' and nāvigō 'to sail' (Lewis & Short).
