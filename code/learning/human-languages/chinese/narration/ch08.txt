Mandarin Chinese, chapter 8: Saying Who You Are.
6 lessons. The first lesson already needs your eyes or your hands, so save this one for when you have stopped.


Lesson 1 of 6.
我 (wǒ) — seven strokes, and an honest admission.

Before we start: this one needs your hands, so it is not a driving lesson. You will want a pen and something to write on and your eyes, for letter shapes on the page. You can listen to everything else now — leave the sections called Script — the shape and Guided Practice until you have stopped, and I will say so again when we reach it.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Write 是. Nine strokes, sun finished on top.

Script — the shape.
我 (wǒ).
Seven strokes, and here this book owes you a straight answer.
Every character so far has had at least one piece you already owned: 你 is 亻 beside 尔, 是 sits on 日, 早 sits on 日. 我 (wǒ) has none. Its right side is a shape called 戈 — there it is, but naming it is all this lesson will do with it, because you have not been taught to write it. Its left side is a squeezed hand. Neither is a piece you own.
So learn it as a route, not as a sum. Follow the order and it holds together:
a short falling stroke at the upper left.
the upper horizontal, left to right.
the vertical, coming down and hooking left without lifting.
the lower rising stroke.
the long curved slash down to the right, hooking upward at its end.
a separate rising slash up to the left.
a dot at the upper right, pressed down-right.
Six pen lifts.
Two of these are joined strokes that must not be broken: stroke 3 (down, then hook) and stroke 5 (the long slash, then hook up). Everything you have learned about turning strokes applies here — the pen changes direction, the stroke does not end.
The dot goes last. Small marks at the upper right are finishers, and you will see that again.

Guided Practice.
[once you have stopped driving — write: 我 (wǒ) slowly, twice — and keep strokes 3 and 5 unbroken]
[once you have stopped driving — write: 我 (wǒ) once more, saving the dot for last]
[your turn — say: wǒ — low and dipping]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
Is this character built from pieces you already know? (No — and this book would rather say so than invent a story.)

Lesson 2 of 6.
我 (wǒ) — I, me, and saying who you are.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say wǒ. Low and dipping, and hold the bottom of it.

The word, taken apart.
我 wǒ — I. And also me.
English uses two words there and swaps between them depending on the job: I saw her, she saw me. Mandarin uses 我 (wǒ) for both, and this is your first sight of something that will save you a great deal of work: Mandarin never changes a word to show what job it is doing in a sentence. No I/me swap, no case endings, no agreeing with a subject. 我 (wǒ) is 我 (wǒ) in either seat.
Mandarin does bolt small extra pieces onto words for other jobs, and you will meet them — but never to mark who is doing what to whom.
Now put it to work with a word you already have:
我是 wǒ shì — I am.
是 is the word you learned for answering yes. Its actual job is to link two things: 我是 (wǒ shì) and then who you are. Say your own name after it and you have introduced yourself.
There is a second way to say this, built on a word you do not have yet. 我是 (wǒ shì) is not a stand-in for it — both are real, and this is the one you can write today.
Two third tones meet in wǒ shì? No — 是 is fourth tone. Nothing moves.

Guided Practice.
[your turn — say: wǒ, then wǒ shì — low and dipping, then falling]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: wǒ shì followed by your own name, out loud, twice]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[once you have stopped driving — write: 我是 (wǒ shì)]

Wrap-up Recall.
What does 是 do in 我是 (wǒ shì)? (It links you to whatever comes next.)
Source: Unicode CJK Unified Ideographs chart.
[question — say your answer, then pause 12 seconds]
English has two words, 'I' and 'me', and picks between them. How many does Mandarin use?


Lesson 3 of 6.
名 (míng) — a mouth with something above it.

Before we start: this one needs your hands, so it is not a driving lesson. You will want a pen and something to write on and your eyes, for letter shapes on the page. You can listen to everything else now — leave the sections called Script — the shape and Guided Practice until you have stopped, and I will say so again when we reach it.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Write 口. Three strokes, and the box is not closed until the last one.

Script — the shape.
名 (míng).
Six strokes, and three of them are 口, which you already own.
名 (míng) is 夕 sitting on top of 口. The lower half is the mouth. The upper half is 夕, a shape this book has not taught you as a character of its own — here it is a piece, and naming it is enough:
夕's upper falling stroke, down to the left.
夕's horizontal, continuing down-left without lifting — one stroke.
夕's inner dot, pressed down-right 4-6. 口, complete. Left side; top and right in one turning stroke; the bottom bar closing it.
Five pen lifts.
Finish 夕 before you begin 口 — the same instinct as finishing 日 before the body of 是, and finishing the sun before 十 in 早. You have now met that rule often enough that it should feel like the obvious thing to do.
A hook worth having, offered as a hook: 夕 is the shape used for evening, and a mouth beneath it reads easily as speaking your name in the dark, when you cannot be seen. That is a traditional story, not proven history — but 名 (míng) does mean name, and the picture makes the character stick.

Guided Practice.
[once you have stopped driving — write: 口 alone, then 名 (míng) — and notice the last three strokes are identical]
[once you have stopped driving — write: 名 (míng) again, keeping stroke 2 unbroken]
[your turn — say: míng — rising]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
Which half goes first? (The top — as always.)

Lesson 4 of 6.
字 (zì) — a child under a roof.

Before we start: this one needs your hands, so it is not a driving lesson. You will want a pen and something to write on and your eyes, for letter shapes on the page. You can listen to everything else now — leave the sections called Script — the shape and Guided Practice until you have stopped, and I will say so again when we reach it.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Write 子, the child — the piece that stands beside 女 in 好.

Script — the shape.
字 (zì).
Six strokes, and the bottom three are 子, which you have written since the first chapter.
字 (zì) is 子 under a roof. The roof is 宀, a piece rather than a character — three strokes that make a lid:
宀's top dot, pressed down-right.
宀's left side, a short stroke down-left.
宀's horizontal roof, running right and hooking down-left at its end — one stroke 4-6. 子, complete. Its top horizontal turning down-left; its vertical with a left hook; its middle horizontal last.
Five pen lifts.
Roof before child. A covering piece is finished before whatever sits under it, which is the same habit as 日 before the body of 是 and 夕 before 口 in 名 (míng).
And now the meaning, which is the reason this character is worth the effort. 字 zì means character — a written Chinese character. It is the word for the thing you have been learning to make since the first page of this book. The roof-and-child picture is the traditional hook: something raised and kept under shelter.

[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Lesson 5 of 6.
名字 (míngzi) — the word for a name.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say zǎoshang. Second syllable light and short.

The word, taken apart.
名字 míngzi — name.
Both halves are characters you have just written, and both mean something:
名 míng — name.
字 zì — character, a written unit.
So the word is "name-character", and that is not a stretched hook. A Chinese personal name is a small number of characters, usually two or three, each chosen deliberately for what it means. The word for "name" says what a name is made of.

Sounds you'll need — the second syllable goes light again.
míngzi, not míngzì.
字 (zì) is fourth tone standing alone, and you practised it that way — but in this word it goes neutral, short and light, exactly as 上 did inside 早上. Second syllables do this often, and you now know the signal: no tone mark means no tone.
míng rises; zi rides on the end of it.
[repeat that twice]

[your turn — say: míngzi twice, and say what each of its two halves means]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Lesson 6 of 6.
Practice — saying who you are.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say wǒ shì — low and dipping, then falling.

The exchange.
Two people meet in the morning. The gap in the third line is yours to fill.
A: 早上好 zǎoshang hǎo — good morning. B: 早上好 zǎoshang hǎo — good morning. A: 我是 … wǒ shì … — I am … (your name). B: 我是 … wǒ shì … — I am … (their name). A: 再见 zàijiàn — goodbye.
Say it with your own name in it, out loud. That is the first time this book has asked you for something it could not supply.

What you've built — and one thing deliberately withheld.
You can now greet by time of day, say who you are, and leave. Every line is built from characters you have written.
One honest gap, and it is worth naming rather than hiding. You have the word for name — 名字 (míngzi) — but not yet the way to ask for someone's. The natural Mandarin question needs a word this book has not given you, and asking it with only the pieces you have would mean teaching you a sentence a speaker would not say. It is coming, and it is worth waiting for.
What 名字 (míngzi) buys you now is real: you can recognise the word when someone else uses it, and you know that a name is characters — which is why a Chinese speaker asked about their name will often tell you which characters it uses.

Guided Practice.
[your turn — say: wǒ shì, then your own name, three times]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: the whole exchange, taking both parts]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[once you have stopped driving — write: 我是 (wǒ shì), then 名字 (míngzi)]

Wrap-up Recall.
Can you yet ask someone else for their name? (Not yet — and the book would rather say so than hand you a sentence nobody says.)
Source: Unicode CJK Unified Ideographs chart.
[question — say your answer, then pause 12 seconds]
