Latin, chapter 321: Minae et tumultus.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
minae, minārum — threats.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for frightened, then the Latin for to panic.

You'll want to know: minae, minārum.
minae, minārum — "threats".
Hostēs minās contrā urbem iactābant. The enemy were hurling threats against the city.
Feminine, plural. First the jutting points of a wall, then threats that hang over someone.

The word, taken apart.
minae first meant "the jutting points of a wall" (Lewis & Short); EtymDB traces it to Proto-Italic menā, "a projection". English menace comes from its family.

Lesson 2 of 5.
minitor, minitārī — to threaten.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to panic, then the Latin for threats.

You'll want to know: minitor, minitārī.
minitor, minitārī — "to threaten".
Latrō mihi gladiō minitābātur. The bandit was threatening me with a sword.
minitor, minitārī, minitātus sum, a deponent, with the dative of the person threatened.

The word, taken apart.
minitor is a stronger form of minor, "to threaten", which is built on minae, "threats" (Lewis & Short).

Lesson 3 of 5.
terreō, terrēre — to frighten.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for threats, then the Latin for to threaten.

You'll want to know: terreō, terrēre.
terreō, terrēre — "to frighten".
Tonitrus īnfantem terruit. The thunder frightened the baby.
terreō, terrēre, terruī, territum. English terrify and terror belong to this family.

The word, taken apart.
terreō is inherited from a Proto-Indo-European root meaning "to tremble, to frighten" (EtymDB). English terrify and terror come from its family.

Lesson 4 of 5.
tumultus, tumultūs — an uproar, a commotion.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to threaten, then the Latin for to frighten.

You'll want to know: tumultus, tumultūs.
tumultus, tumultūs — "an uproar, a commotion".
Magnus tumultus in forō erat. There was a great commotion in the forum.
Masculine, 4th declension. Also an emergency war close to home. English tumult.

The word, taken apart.
tumultus is perhaps related to tumeō, "to swell"; its origin is not settled (Lewis & Short). English tumult comes from it through French.

Lesson 5 of 5.
clāmor, clāmōris — a shout, shouting.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to frighten, then the Latin for an uproar.

You'll want to know: clāmor, clāmōris.
clāmor, clāmōris — "a shout, shouting".
Subitō clāmor ingēns in viā ortus est. Suddenly a huge shout went up in the street.
Masculine, 3rd declension, from clāmō (taught). Clāmōrem tollere: to raise a shout. English clamour.

The word, taken apart.
clāmor is built on clāmō, "to shout" (EtymDB). English clamour comes from it through French.
