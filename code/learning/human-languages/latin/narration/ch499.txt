Latin, chapter 499: Pleasure and delight.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
dēlectō, dēlectāre — to delight, charm.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to distrust, then the Latin for trusting.

You'll want to know: dēlectō, dēlectāre.
dēlectō, dēlectāre — "to delight, charm".
Fābulae avī puerōs dēlectant. Grandfather's stories delight the children.
dēlectō, dēlectāre, dēlectāvī, dēlectātum, with the accusative. English delight comes from it through Old French.

The word, taken apart.
dēlectō is the frequentative of dēliciō "to allure, entice" (Lewis & Short), built on dē- and laciō "to entice". English delight comes from it through Old French.

Lesson 3 of 5.
voluptās, voluptātis — pleasure, enjoyment.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to delight, then the Latin for delight.

You'll want to know: voluptās, voluptātis.
voluptās, voluptātis — "pleasure, enjoyment".
Sapiēns nōn voluptātem, sed virtūtem quaerit. The wise man seeks not pleasure but virtue.
Feminine, third declension. Pleasure of every kind, of the senses and of the mind. English voluptuous.

The word, taken apart.
voluptās is built on the old adverb volup "pleasantly" (EtymDB), heard in the phrase volup est "it is a pleasure"; Lewis & Short compares Greek elpo "to hope" instead. English voluptuous comes from it.

Lesson 4 of 5.
oblectō, oblectāre — to amuse, entertain.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for delight, then the Latin for pleasure.

You'll want to know: oblectō, oblectāre.
oblectō, oblectāre — "to amuse, entertain".
Senex sē librīs oblectat. The old man amuses himself with books.
oblectō, oblectāre, oblectāvī, oblectātum. Often with sē: to pass the time pleasantly.

The word, taken apart.
oblectō is built on ob- and lactō "to allure", a frequentative of laciō "to entice" (Lewis & Short; EtymDB), the same root as dēlectō.

Lesson 5 of 5.
placeō, placēre — to please, be pleasing.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for pleasure, then the Latin for to amuse.

You'll want to know: placeō, placēre.
placeō, placēre — "to please, be pleasing".
Haec domus nova uxōrī meae placet. This new house pleases my wife.
placeō, placēre, placuī, placitum, with the dative: mihi placet — I like it; it seems good to me. English please comes from it.

The word, taken apart.
placeō is a native Latin verb, a cousin of plācō "to calm" (EtymDB; Lewis & Short). English please and pleasure come from it through Old French.
