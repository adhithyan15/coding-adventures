Mandarin Chinese, chapter 11: After You.
5 lessons. The first lesson already needs your eyes or your hands, so save this one for when you have stopped.


Lesson 1 of 5.
请 (qǐng) — a half that tells you the sound.

Before we start: this one needs your hands, so it is not a driving lesson. You will want a pen and something to write on and your eyes, for letter shapes on the page. You can listen to everything else now — leave the sections called Script — the shape and Guided Practice until you have stopped, and I will say so again when we reach it.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Write 讠. Dot, then one turning stroke. What does it tell you about the word? (Speech.)

Script — the shape.
请 (qǐng).
Ten strokes, two pieces, and 讠 is on the left again as you would expect.
讠 + 青.
讠 (strokes 1-2). Speech. You know it.
青 (strokes 3-10). A top horizontal; a second horizontal; a vertical down through them; a wide middle horizontal; then the lower box — its left side, then its top-and-right in one turning stroke that hooks at the base, then two short inner horizontals.
Nine pen lifts. 讠 finished before 青.
Now the part worth stopping for. In 谢, both halves pointed at meaning — speech, body, inch. Here the right half does something else entirely.
青 is said qīng. The whole character is said qǐng.
Say those two aloud, one after the other. Same consonant, same rest of the syllable; only the pitch differs. The right half is not telling you what the word means. It is telling you how to say it. That is a phonetic component — the job 尔 was doing inside 你, which you could only take on trust there. This time you can hear it. Once you can spot one you have a genuine reading strategy: meet an unknown character, find the half you recognise, and you often have a guess at the sound.
It is a guess and not a guarantee — the sound has drifted in plenty of characters over a few thousand years, and the tone in particular is often different, as it is here. But a guess beats nothing, and you will be right often.

Guided Practice.
[once you have stopped driving — write: 讠, then 青 — stopping between the two pieces]
[your turn — say: qīng, then qǐng — the sound half, then the whole character]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[once you have stopped driving — write: 请 (qǐng) straight through]

Wrap-up Recall.
Is the sound half exactly right? (Not quite — the tone differs.)

[question — say your answer, then pause 12 seconds]

Lesson 2 of 5.
请 (qǐng) — please, and after you.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say qǐng — low and dipping, like hǎo and wǒ.

The word, taken apart.
请 qǐng — the word a dictionary will give you as please.
That translation is close enough to use and loose enough to mislead, so here is the more accurate version: 请 (qǐng) is closer to "I ask you to", "I invite you to". Where English please softens a demand, 请 (qǐng) hands the action over.
Which is why it works on its own. Hold a door and say 请 (qǐng) — that is after you, go ahead, be my guest. Offer someone a seat and say 请 (qǐng). No other words needed, and none of them would be an apology for asking.

Why it's said this way — where English and Mandarin part company.
English please is a softener. You bolt it onto a request to take the edge off, and you can put it almost anywhere: please sit, sit, please, could you please sit.
请 (qǐng) does not work like that. It comes before the action it invites, never bolted on after it — there is no Mandarin habit of tacking 请 (qǐng) onto the end of a request the way English tacks on please.
The consequence is practical, and it surprises learners: Mandarin uses 请 (qǐng) far less often than English uses please — and with people you are close to it can sound distant rather than polite. Leaving it out is not rude the way dropping please can be in English. Warmth in Mandarin is carried more by what you say and how you say it than by a single courtesy word.
So: use 请 (qǐng) to hand something over or to invite. Do not reach for it every time you would say please in English — you would sound stilted rather than polite.

[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.

Lesson 3 of 5.
Practice — after you.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say qǐng — low and dipping.

The exchange.
Two people reach a door at the same time.
A: 请 qǐng — after you. B: 谢谢 xièxie — thank you. A: 不谢 bú xiè — you're welcome. B: 再见 zàijiàn — goodbye.
Four lines, four words, and a complete small courtesy. Nobody in it asks for anything or explains anything, and it still works — which is most of what courtesy is.

What you've built — a component that predicts.
Consider what the two halves of this chapter's character bought you.
讠 on the left told you the word was about speech before you knew what it meant — and it did the same in 谢. Two characters, one component, one reliable guess.
青 on the right told you roughly how to say it. You were told once before that a half could do this — 尔, on the right of 你 — but you had to take it on trust, because 尔 is ěr and nothing in it sounds like nǐ. Here you can hear it for yourself: qīng, qǐng.
So the claim made in passing back then is now one you can check: a Chinese character can carry two kinds of hint at once, one for what it means and one for how it is said. That is the single most useful thing to know about reading Chinese, and it is why the writing system is learnable at all rather than being thousands of unrelated pictures.
You will not always get both hints, and neither is a promise. But from here on, when you meet a character you do not know, you have something to do other than look it up: find the half you recognise, and ask which job it is doing.

Guided Practice.
[your turn — say: the whole exchange, both parts]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: qǐng, xièxie, bú xiè — three courtesies in a row]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[once you have stopped driving — write: 请 (qǐng), then 谢 — and say what their left halves have in common]

Wrap-up Recall.
Which half of 请 (qǐng) would help you guess its pronunciation? (The right.)

Lesson 4 of 5.
Eight exchanges, one meeting — second pass over the eight two-voice exchanges, run in the order a real meeting would use them.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, because the lesson points at something written down. You can listen to all of it now and come back to the parts that need looking at once you have stopped.

Warm-up.
[pause 3 seconds]
Every chapter so far has ended on a two-voice exchange, and you have practised each one on its own. From memory: the one that opens a meeting, and the one that closes it.

You'll want to know: they were always one conversation.
Eight exchanges arrived one per chapter. Put them in the order a meeting uses them and this book's whole first act is there:
[a table of 5 rows, read aloud]
the move: greet. what it added: 你好, both voices.
the move: introduce, ask. what it added: a name given, a name asked for.
the move: identify, negate. what it added: saying it is so, and saying it is not.
the move: thank, be polite. what it added: 谢谢, and the reply that follows it.
the move: part. what it added: 再见.
Nothing in that column is new. What is new is that no chapter ever asked you to run them one after another, and a meeting is exactly that: eight small moves with no pause between them for looking anything up.

Lesson 5 of 5.
好 (hǎo, hào) — one character, and three things it is not.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, for letter shapes on the page. You can listen to everything else now — leave the section called Script: the component that carries a sound until you have stopped, and I will say so again when we reach it.

Warm-up.
[pause 3 seconds]
Say 好 (hǎo, hào) twice, once in each tone you have met, and say what each one means. One character, two words.

You'll want to know: four cautions, and 好 carries three of them.
These chapters have warned you four separate times about reading too much into what you see. 好 (hǎo, hào) is where three of the four meet:
A character is not a word. 你好 is two characters and one word. A character writes a syllable and usually a meaningful piece — not a word.
A component is not always a meaning. 好 (hǎo, hào) is 女 beside 子, woman beside child, and the lesson that built it warned you against reading a story into that. The pieces are how the character is assembled, not what it argues.
A tone is not a decoration. hǎo is good; hào is to be fond of. Same character, same strokes, different word.

Script: the component that carries a sound.
尔.
The fourth caution is the other way round. 尔 is in 你 to say how it sounds, not what it means — a component doing phonetic work. Once you expect that, a great many characters stop looking arbitrary.
Against it, 口 kǒu (mouth) and 日 rì (sun, and also day) are components that do mean what they draw. Both kinds exist, and the character does not announce which you are looking at. That is the caution worth carrying forward.

[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: 口 and 日, and what each one draws]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
How many words is 你好, and how many characters? (One word, two characters.) What are the two halves of 好 (hǎo, hào), and what do they tell you about its meaning? (女 and 子 — and nothing you should rely on.) What does 尔 contribute to 你? (Its sound, not its meaning.) And which of 口 and 日 means what it draws? (Both — which is why the two kinds have to be told apart.)
