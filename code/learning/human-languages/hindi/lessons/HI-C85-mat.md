# मत — the word Hindi keeps for saying "don't"

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[HI-LEX-C85-IMP-02, HI-LEX-C85-IMP-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say *bolo* and *boliye*. Now say the opposite of each — and notice
that the negative you already know is not the one Hindi uses here.

## You'll want to know: मत
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[HI-LEX-C85-IMP-04]; assesses=[] -->

**मत** (*mat*) — "don't," and it goes **in front of the verb**:

> **मत बोलिए** — *mat boliye* — please don't speak.
> **मत बोलो** — *mat bolo* — don't speak.

Hindi has a negative for statements, which you already have, and a **separate
one reserved for commands**. Using the statement negative to tell somebody not
to do something is the mistake an English speaker makes, because English uses
*not* for both.

## Grammar Lens: the sign you will actually meet it on
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[HI-LEX-C85-IMP-04, HI-LEX-C85-IMP-02] -->

A negative command is what a public notice **is**. This is the shape:

| notice | meaning |
|---|---|
| यहाँ मत बैठिए | please do not sit here |

Three pieces, and you have all three: *yahā̃* here, *mat* don't, *baiṭhiye* the
respectful command from *baiṭhnā*, built with the ending from the last lesson.

Notice the politeness level. A public sign addresses a stranger, so it uses the
**आप** form — which is why a reading paper's notices are full of **-iye** and why
that ending was worth taking apart.
