Latin, chapter 516: Throat, neck and limbs.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
guttur, gutturis — throat, gullet.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for eyebrow, then the Latin for eyelid.

You'll want to know: guttur, gutturis.
guttur, gutturis — "throat, gullet".
Guttur mihi dolet. My throat hurts.
Neuter, third declension. English guttural — of the throat.

The word, taken apart.
guttur is inherited; EtymDB traces it to a Proto-Indo-European root meaning "a swelling, rounding". English guttural comes from it.

Lesson 2 of 5.
faucēs, faucium — jaws, the back of the mouth; a narrow pass.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for eyelid, then the Latin for throat.

You'll want to know: faucēs, faucium.
faucēs, faucium — "jaws, the back of the mouth; a narrow pass".
Leō faucēs aperit. The lion opens its jaws.
Feminine, third declension, plural. The back of the mouth; also a narrow entrance to a valley or a harbour.

The word, taken apart.
faucēs is a native Latin noun; Lewis & Short compares a Sanskrit word for "opening", but the origin is not settled.

Lesson 4 of 5.
membrum, membrī — a limb; a member.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for jaws, then the Latin for neck.

You'll want to know: membrum, membrī.
membrum, membrī — "a limb; a member".
Post longum iter membra mea dolent. After the long journey my limbs ache.
Neuter, second declension. Also a member of a group. English member.

The word, taken apart.
membrum is inherited through Proto-Italic from a Proto-Indo-European word for "flesh" (EtymDB). English member comes from it.

Lesson 5 of 5.
artus, artuum — joints, limbs.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for neck, then the Latin for a limb.

You'll want to know: artus, artuum.
artus, artuum — "joints, limbs".
Frīgus artūs senis rigidōs facit. The cold makes the old man's joints stiff.
Masculine, fourth declension, nearly always plural; dative and ablative artubus. English article, a little joint, comes from its diminutive.

The word, taken apart.
artus belongs with arma and a Proto-Indo-European root meaning "to fit together" (Lewis & Short; EtymDB). English article comes from its diminutive articulus "a little joint".
