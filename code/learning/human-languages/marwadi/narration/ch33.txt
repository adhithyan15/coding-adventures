Marwadi (Marwari), chapter 33: Six To Ten.
13 lessons. You can do the first 3 of them in the car; after that you will want to have stopped.


Lesson 1 of 13.
Hear chha — six.

Warm-up.
[pause 20 seconds]
Recall the counting payoff, say tea, then say four.
[your turn — recall: chār from the last chapter, and the plain ch you tested with a hand at your mouth]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

You'll want to know.
chha — six.
Hand at the mouth again. chār moves nothing. chha pushes a puff of air out with the consonant.
That single difference is the whole of it. The tongue does the same thing in both words; the lungs do not. Marwari keeps that contrast everywhere, and here it separates two numbers that sit next to each other on a price ticket.
The same number is also printed chhe, with a vowel mark after the consonant. Both are in use; count with the bare form for now and read the other when a page prints it.

Wrap-up Recall.
Sources: Omniglot, Numbers in Rajasthani; Turner, A Comparative Dictionary of the Indo-Aryan Languages.
[question — say your answer, then pause 14 seconds]
Say four and six one after the other, and name what changes.


Lesson 2 of 13.
छ (chha) — six on the page.

Warm-up.
[pause 18 seconds]
Write य, write ए, then write four.

You'll want to know.
छ — chha — six.
One sign, made in chapter 9, and nothing added to it.
The other printed spelling is छे, and it is worth a moment. That is छ (chha) with the mark of the letter chapter 32 taught — the same vowel, riding on a consonant instead of standing alone. It is also, exactly, the second syllable of पाछे. A chunk the hand has been writing since chapter 9 turns out to be a number.

Guided Practice.
Write छ (chha), then write छे beneath it, then write ए beside both. Say which of the three stands alone and which two need something under them.

Lesson 5 of 13.
Hear āṭh — eight.

Warm-up.
[pause 20 seconds]
Recall the seven-word food payoff, offer formal thanks, then say seven.
[your turn — recall: chār and pāṅch, and which of the two carries a nasal]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

You'll want to know.
āṭh — eight.
It opens on the long ā of आभार, standing on its own with no consonant in front of it. Of the ten numbers this slice teaches, eight is the only one that does that.
The closing consonant is retroflex: the tongue curls back, as in ठंडी. Do not close it at the teeth, or seven and eight begin to sound alike.

Lesson 6 of 13.
आठ (āṭh) — eight on the page.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, because the lesson points at something written down. You can listen to all of it now and come back to the parts that need looking at once you have stopped.

Warm-up.
[pause 16 seconds]
Write formal thanks, then write seven.

You'll want to know.
आ + ठ becomes आठ — āṭh — eight.
Two letters, both of them years old in this book's own timeline: आ from chapter 2 and ठ from chapter 17. Nothing is joined, nothing is marked.
Compare सात (sāt) and आठ (āṭh) side by side. Seven takes the vowel as a mark on a consonant; eight takes the same vowel as a letter, because there is no consonant for it to sit on.

Lesson 7 of 13.
Hear no — nine.

Warm-up.
[pause 18 seconds]
Say this one, say very, then say eight.
[your turn — recall: ek and the letter ए you had to learn to write it]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

You'll want to know.
no — nine.
One consonant, one long vowel, and it rhymes with do. Two and nine are the only numbers in this run that short.
This is not the English word no. Marwadi has not yet given this book any word for refusing at all, which is a real gap and not a spelling accident: a learner who mishears no as a refusal will walk away from a fair price.

Lesson 9 of 13.

[pause 18 seconds]

[your turn — recall: chha and the puff of air that separates it from chār]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

You'll want to know.
das — ten.
Short vowel this time, closed off at once by s. Nothing is held.
das is the first number in this book that a Rajasthani market is likely to say out loud. Almost nothing on a stall is priced under ten, which is why a course that stopped at five could ask the price four different ways and still not understand the answer.
The comparative dictionary of the Indo-Aryan languages lists this form for Marwari by name, under the Sanskrit word for ten.

Lesson 10 of 13.
दस (das) — ten on the page.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, because the lesson points at something written down. You can listen to all of it now and come back to the parts that need looking at once you have stopped.

Warm-up.
[pause 16 seconds]
Write ख, then write nine.

You'll want to know.
द + स becomes दस — das — ten.
Bare consonants. No mark rides above the line and none hangs below it, because the vowel between them is the short a that every Devanagari consonant carries without being told.
The द is two's; the स is seven's. Ten is written out of pieces the hand already made for other numbers.

Guided Practice.
Look at दस (das), cover it, wait five seconds, write it. Then write दो beside it and say what the mark on the second one does to the vowel.

Wrap-up Recall.
Source: Omniglot, Numbers in Rajasthani.

Lesson 12 of 13.
Payoff — counting to ten.

Warm-up.
[pause 18 seconds]
Recall the five-number payoff, then write the letter that made it possible.

Guided Practice.
Four skills, scored separately.
Listening. Hear ten numbers named singly and out of order; write each in figures of your own language.
Speaking. Count one to ten, then ten to one, with no model.
Reading. Read all ten printed in Devanagari, shuffled, and say each.
Writing. Write all ten from dictation, shuffled, with no model in view.
Pass each one separately.
Look back at what the hand had to learn for this: one letter, ए. Every other sign in all ten words was already in the book. A reader who felt the numbers were going to be a wall of new writing has the evidence in front of them that they were not.

Wrap-up Recall.
Sources: Omniglot, Numbers in Rajasthani; Marwari Pathshala, Lesson 9.
