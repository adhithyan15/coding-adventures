# la limpieza — the cleaning

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-LIMPIO, ES-LEX-C448-MUDA-02] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say **limpio** and **la mudanza**. One is a description, the other
names a job of work. You are about to turn the first into the second.

## You'll want to know: la limpieza
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-C453-LIMPIEZA-01]; assesses=[ES-LEX-LIMPIO] -->

**la limpieza** — cleaning, the cleaning.

*El turno de limpieza* — the cleaning shift. *Productos de limpieza* —
cleaning products. *La limpieza del portal es los martes* — the entrance hall
is cleaned on Tuesdays.

It names the activity, not the state. For the state you still say *está
limpio*. **Limpiar** is the verb: *hay que limpiar la cocina*.

## Grammar Lens: four endings, four genders settled
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C453-LIMPIEZA-01, ES-LEX-C448-MUDA-02] -->

You now have the full set of Spanish's everyday noun-makers:

| ending | example | gender |
|---|---|---|
| **-anza** | la mud**anza** | feminine |
| **-miento** | el segui**miento** | masculine |
| **-eza** | la limpi**eza** | feminine |
| **-ción** | la instala**ción** | feminine |

Three of the four are feminine, and **-miento** is the one to remember as the
exception. Learn the ending and the article stops being a guess.

Which ending a given word takes is not predictable — *limpieza* and not
*limpiamiento*, *seguimiento* and not *seguianza* — and there is no rule
waiting to be found. What is reliable is the gender, and that is most of what
you needed.

**-eza** attaches to adjectives rather than verbs: *limpio* gives *limpieza*,
*triste* gives *tristeza*, *pobre* gives *pobreza*. It names the quality, and
in this one case also the work of producing it.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C453-LIMPIEZA-01] -->

Latin **limpidus**, clear — as in water you can see through.

English took the same word as **limpid**, and kept it for water and for prose.
Spanish let it drift from "clear" to "clean", which is a short walk: a clear
stream is a clean one.

There is an older Spanish word for clean, *neto*, from Latin *nitidus*, and it
survives in *el peso neto* on a packet. Where Spanish kept two, it usually
gave them separate jobs.
