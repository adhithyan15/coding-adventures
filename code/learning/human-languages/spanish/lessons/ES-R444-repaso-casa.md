## Grammar Lens: three things a word can do on its way into Spanish
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C444-CASA-02, ES-LEX-C444-CASA-03, ES-LEX-C444-CASA-05] -->

Every word in this chapter came from Latin, and no two arrived the same way.

| word | what happened to it |
|---|---|
| **madera** | arrived **twice** — and *materia* is the other one |
| **planta** | arrived once and **spread** into three meanings |
| **carrito** | arrived, then Spanish **built on it** |

*Madera* and *materia* are one word on two roads, the worn twin and the learned
twin. *Planta* is one road and one word that kept being reused — sole of the
foot, then the cutting pressed in by it, then the floor plan you stand on.
*Carrito* is neither: *carro* came in, and the **-ito** on the end is Spanish's
own work, added centuries after Latin had finished.

Those three cover most of what you will meet. A word arrives twice, or it
arrives once and stretches, or it arrives and the language grows something new
on it.

## What you've built
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C444-CASA-01, ES-LEX-C444-CASA-03, ES-LEX-C444-CASA-04, ES-LEX-C444-CASA-05] -->

**You can now report what is wrong with a home and say what you did about it** —
the heating, the material, the garden, the thing you covered, the trolley you
took to the shop. Between this and the contract chapter you can hold up most of
a tenancy in Spanish.

You also have a third irregular participle. **Cubierto** joins **abierto**, and
they break together because they rhyme: the irregulars come in small families,
so finding one gives you the rest.
