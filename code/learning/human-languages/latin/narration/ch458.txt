Lesson 1 of 5.
angiportum, angiportī — an alley, a narrow lane.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a timber, then the Latin for a beam.

You'll want to know: angiportum, angiportī.
angiportum, angiportī — "an alley, a narrow lane".
Nocte angiportum obscūrum et perīculōsum est. At night the alley is dark and dangerous.
Second declension, neuter: angiportum, angiportī n.; also angiportus, angiportūs m. A narrow side street between houses.

The word, taken apart.
angiportum is built, as Lewis & Short explain under angiportus, on the root of angustus 'narrow' and on portus in its old sense 'passage'. EtymDB does not list it.

Lesson 2 of 5.
vīcīnia, vīcīniae — a neighbourhood; the neighbours.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a beam, then the Latin for an alley.

You'll want to know: vīcīnia, vīcīniae.
vīcīnia, vīcīniae — "a neighbourhood; the neighbours".
In nostrā vīcīniā taberna bona est. In our neighbourhood there is a good shop.
First declension, feminine: vīcīnia, vīcīniae f., from vīcīnus 'neighbour'. English vicinity comes from the related vīcīnitās.

The word, taken apart.
vīcīnia is built on vīcīnus 'neighbour' with the suffix -ia (EtymDB; Lewis & Short), and vīcīnus comes from vīcus 'street, village'.

Lesson 3 of 5.
lātrīna, lātrīnae — a latrine, a privy, a lavatory.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for an alley, then the Latin for a neighbourhood.

You'll want to know: lātrīna, lātrīnae.
lātrīna, lātrīnae — "a latrine, a privy, a lavatory".
In thermīs lātrīna pūblica est. In the baths there is a public lavatory.
First declension, feminine: lātrīna, lātrīnae f. Lewis & Short note that it first meant a bath. English latrine comes from it.

The word, taken apart.
lātrīna is shortened from lavātrīna 'a place for washing', built on lavō 'to wash' (Lewis & Short). English latrine comes from it.

Lesson 4 of 5.
cloāca, cloācae — a sewer, a drain.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a neighbourhood, then the Latin for a latrine.

You'll want to know: cloāca, cloācae.
cloāca, cloācae — "a sewer, a drain".
Aqua sordida per cloācam in flūmen it. Dirty water runs through the sewer into the river.
First declension, feminine: cloāca, cloācae f. The great sewers of old towns still carry water.

The word, taken apart.
cloāca is built on an old verb cluō 'to cleanse, to purge' (EtymDB; Lewis & Short), so it is a channel that washes the city clean. English keeps cloaca as a term in zoology.

Lesson 5 of 5.
castellum, castellī — a water tower, a reservoir; a fort, a stronghold.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a latrine, then the Latin for a sewer.

You'll want to know: castellum, castellī.
castellum, castellī — "a water tower, a reservoir; a fort, a stronghold".
Aqua ex aquaeductū in castellum venit et inde in urbem. Water comes from the aqueduct into the water tower and from there into the city.
Second declension, neuter: castellum, castellī n., a diminutive of castrum 'fort'. The castellum aquae shared the water out to the pipes. English castle comes from it.

The word, taken apart.
castellum is a diminutive of castrum 'fort, fortified place' (EtymDB; Lewis & Short), and Lewis & Short also give the water reservoir of an aqueduct. English castle comes from it.
