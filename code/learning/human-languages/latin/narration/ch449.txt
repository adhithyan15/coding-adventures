Latin, chapter 449: Flat wine, Neat wine, To water down, Rotten, Tavern hostess.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
vappa, vappae — flat wine, wine gone off; a good-for-nothing.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a drinker, then the Latin for hoarse.

You'll want to know: vappa, vappae.
vappa, vappae — "flat wine, wine gone off; a good-for-nothing".
Caupō nōn vīnum bonum sed vappam vendit. The innkeeper sells not good wine but flat wine.
First declension, feminine: vappa, vappae f. Wine that has lost its taste; also, of a person, a worthless fellow.

The word, taken apart.
vappa is usually linked with vapor 'steam' (Lewis & Short): wine whose spirit has gone up in vapour. Lewis & Short also compare vapidus 'flat', the source of English vapid.

Lesson 2 of 5.
merum, merī — neat wine, wine without water.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for hoarse, then the Latin for flat wine.

You'll want to know: merum, merī.
merum, merī — "neat wine, wine without water".
Pōtor merum bibit, nōn vīnum cum aquā. The drinker drinks neat wine, not wine with water.
Second declension, neuter: merum, merī n., the neuter of merus 'pure, unmixed'. Romans mixed wine with water; drinking it neat was a sign of excess. English mere comes from merus.

The word, taken apart.
merum is the neuter of the adjective merus 'pure, unmixed' (Lewis & Short), used as a noun for wine with no water. English mere comes from merus.

Lesson 3 of 5.
dīluō, dīluere — to water down, to dilute; to wash away.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for flat wine, then the Latin for neat wine.

You'll want to know: dīluō, dīluere.
dīluō, dīluere — "to water down, to dilute; to wash away".
Caupō vīnum aquā dīluit et magnō pretiō vendit. The innkeeper waters the wine and sells it at a high price.
dīluō, dīluere, dīluī, dīlūtum. English dilute comes from its participle and deluge from the noun dīluvium.

The word, taken apart.
dīluō is built from dis- 'apart' and luō 'to wash', a verb akin to lavō (Lewis & Short). English dilute comes from its participle dīlūtus, and deluge from the noun dīluvium 'flood'.

Lesson 4 of 5.
pūtidus, pūtida, pūtidum — rotten, stinking.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for neat wine, then the Latin for to water down.

You'll want to know: pūtidus, pūtida, pūtidum.
pūtidus, pūtida, pūtidum — "rotten, stinking".
Carō in caupōnā pūtida est. The meat in the inn is rotten.
An adjective of three endings, from pūteō 'to stink'. Also of style: affected, tiresome.

The word, taken apart.
pūtidus is built on pūteō 'to stink, to be rotten' (Lewis & Short) with the adjective suffix -idus, as in frīgidus.

Lesson 5 of 5.
cōpa, cōpae — a tavern hostess, a barmaid.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to water down, then the Latin for rotten.

You'll want to know: cōpa, cōpae.
cōpa, cōpae — "a tavern hostess, a barmaid".
Cōpa vīnum hospitibus dat et cum eīs rīdet. The tavern hostess gives the guests wine and laughs with them.
First declension, feminine: cōpa, cōpae f. The woman who kept or served in a tavern.

The word, taken apart.
cōpa is the feminine of cōpō, another form of caupō 'innkeeper' (Lewis & Short), with the au turned to ō as in everyday speech.
