# hablo — one word, one whole sentence

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-GRAMMAR-AR-PRESENT-1SG, ES-LEX-HABLAR] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say "I speak" in Spanish. (*Hablo*.) Now count the words you said.

## Grammar Lens: the pronoun you never needed
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-GRAMMAR-PRO-DROP]; assesses=[ES-GRAMMAR-AR-PRESENT-1SG] -->

One word. And it is a complete, ordinary, grammatical Spanish sentence.

English needs two — *I* and *speak* — because English's ending is doing no work.
Spanish needs one, because the **-o** already said *I* in the previous lesson.

Spanish does have a word for *I*. You will meet it. But putting it in front of
*hablo* is a little like saying **"I, I speak"** — the information is already
there, and repeating it is something Spanish does only when it wants to make a
point.

> **The habit to build now.** When you want to say *I speak*, say **hablo**.
> Not two words. One.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-GRAMMAR-PRO-DROP] -->

This is not Spanish being clever. It is English having lost something.

Old English marked its verbs properly — *ic sprece*, *þū sprecest*, *hē
spricþ*. Those endings were as distinct as Spanish's are. Then English wore
them down until almost nothing was left: today *speak, speak, speak, speak,
speaks*. Four of the five are identical.

Once the endings stopped telling you who was speaking, the pronoun had to,
and English could never put it down again.

Spanish kept its endings. So Spanish kept the choice.
