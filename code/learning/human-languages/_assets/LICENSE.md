# Licensing for Class C illustration assets

This file records the project owner's licensing decision for the model-generated
illustrations that live under `illustrations/`. It is the file
[HL06](../../../specs/HL06-visual-system.md) requires CI to check against: no Class C
asset ships without a recorded licence and a provenance sidecar.

**This is a recorded project decision, not legal advice.**

## The decision (2026-08-06)

| Artefact | Licence | Rights asserted |
|---|---|---|
| The books, lessons, and all authored prose | **CC BY-SA 4.0** | Yes — human-authored |
| Class A script figures and Class B data diagrams | **CC BY-SA 4.0** (with the book) | Yes — generated from human-authored data |
| Class C illustrations (model-generated raster art) | **CC0-1.0** | **No** (`rightsAsserted: false`) |

The books stay CC BY-SA 4.0. Nothing about this decision relicenses the book, the
lesson data, or anything else in the repository. It applies only to model-generated
illustration assets.

## Why the illustrations are not CC BY-SA

A Creative Commons licence is a **grant of copyright permissions**. Granting
permissions presupposes holding the copyright to grant.

Purely AI-generated output likely is not copyrightable in the United States, because
copyright requires human authorship. The Copyright Office refused registration for the
images in *Zarya of the Dawn* (2023) on exactly that ground, *Thaler v. Perlmutter*
held that a work with no human author cannot be registered, and the Office's subsequent
guidance on copyright and artificial intelligence keeps the line in the same place:
prompting alone does not make the prompter the author of the output.

Two consequences follow, and both point the same way.

1. **Stamping CC BY-SA 4.0 on such an image asserts a right that may not exist.** The
   notice would be telling readers "I own this and here are your permissions" when
   there may be nothing there to own.
2. **ShareAlike would bind readers to an obligation that may be unenforceable.** A
   reader who reuses the image would be told to license their derivative work
   alike — a real burden, imposed on the strength of a claim that may be empty. That
   is the worse half of the problem, because it costs the reader something.

CC0 avoids both. It waives whatever rights may exist and asserts nothing about whether
they do. **CC0 is safe whichever way the law settles**: if the output turns out to be
uncopyrightable, CC0 changes nothing and misleads nobody; if it turns out to be
copyrightable after all, the waiver is valid and the reader's freedom is unaffected.

## Why `rightsAsserted: false` is a separate field

The licence marking and the ownership claim are two different statements, and the
sidecar keeps them apart on purpose.

`license: "CC0-1.0"` says *how this file may be used*. `rightsAsserted: false` says
*this project does not claim to hold copyright in this file* — the CC0 waiver is
offered out of caution, not as a representation that there was a right to waive.

Jurisdictions differ, which is why one global claim would be the wrong shape. The UK's
Copyright, Designs and Patents Act 1988 s9(3) attributes authorship of a
computer-generated work to the person who made the arrangements necessary for its
creation, with a 50-year term under s12(7); the US position described above is
narrower. An asset's status can therefore differ by territory. Recording provenance
**per asset** — which model, which prompt, which date, under which terms — leaves the
record accurate under either regime, instead of forcing a single answer that would be
wrong somewhere.

## Required sidecar fields

Every file in `illustrations/` has a sidecar JSON beside it. CI verifies the sidecar
exists, carries every field below, and that `sha256` matches the asset on disk.

| Field | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `license` | SPDX identifier. `CC0-1.0` for Class C illustrations. |
| `rightsAsserted` | Boolean. `false` for model-generated art, per the decision above. |
| `generator` | The tool or service that produced the image. |
| `model` | The specific model and version, as reported by the generator. |
| `prompt` | The full prompt text, verbatim. |
| `date` | ISO 8601 date the asset was generated. |
| `sha256` | Hex digest of the asset file. This is the drift gate — raster art cannot be regenerated byte-identically, so the committed file is the artefact and the hash is what CI checks. |

Example:

```json
{
  "license": "CC0-1.0",
  "rightsAsserted": false,
  "generator": "example-image-service",
  "model": "example-model-v2",
  "prompt": "a bowl of oranges on a wooden table, warm morning light, flat illustration",
  "date": "2026-08-06",
  "sha256": "e3b0c44298fc1c149afbf4c8996fb92427ae41e4649b934ca495991b7852b855"
}
```

## Two operational constraints

The licence decision settles the *output* marking. It does not settle the *input* side,
and these two checks stay live for every asset.

1. **Prompts must avoid living artists, brands, and recognizable characters.** No
   "in the style of \<living artist\>", no trademarks or logos, no characters from
   copyrighted works. CC0 waives whatever rights this project might hold in the output;
   it does nothing about a third party's rights, and no licence marking cures a prompt
   that reached for someone else's protected material. Publicity rights and trademark
   are separate regimes from copyright and are not waived by anything written here.
2. **Each generator's output terms must be checked per asset.** Generators differ, and
   they change their terms. Some grant broad rights to output, some retain rights, some
   restrict commercial use, some forbid downstream sublicensing. Marking an asset CC0
   is only honest if the generator's terms actually permit releasing that output on
   those terms. Check the terms in force **on the generation date**, and record the
   generator and model in the sidecar so the check can be re-run later against the right
   version of the terms.

## Subject restriction

Class C illustrations depict **non-linguistic subjects only**. The glyph monopoly
belongs to the Class A font-derived pipeline: no drawn, traced, or model-generated
image may render a letter, glyph, ligature, conjunct, or handwriting stroke. See
[README.md](./README.md) for the rule and the reasoning behind it.

## Precedent

This file follows the posture already set by the vendored fonts in
[`../_fonts/`](../_fonts/README.md): the third-party terms are stated plainly, the full
licence text is kept alongside the files it covers ([`../_fonts/OFL.txt`](../_fonts/OFL.txt)),
and the reason for vendoring is written down rather than left to be inferred. An
unlicensed image in a freely published book is a real problem, not a formality.

The CC0 1.0 Universal legal text is published by Creative Commons at
<https://creativecommons.org/publicdomain/zero/1.0/legalcode>. The book's CC BY-SA 4.0
text is at <https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/4.0/legalcode>.
