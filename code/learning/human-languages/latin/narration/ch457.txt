Latin, chapter 457: Block of flats, Upper room, Storey, Timber, Beam.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
īnsula, īnsulae — a block of flats, an apartment house; an island.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to live in, then the Latin for an occupant.

You'll want to know: īnsula, īnsulae.
īnsula, īnsulae — "a block of flats, an apartment house; an island".
In urbe multī pauperēs in īnsulīs altīs habitant. In the city many poor people live in tall blocks of flats.
First declension, feminine: īnsula, īnsulae f. First an island, then a block of rented flats standing alone like an island among streets. English isle and insulate come from it.

The word, taken apart.
The origin of īnsula is not settled: Lewis & Short offer a doubtful analysis as in- + sul-, and EtymDB gives no older parent. English isle comes from it through French, and insulate and peninsula from Latin.

Lesson 2 of 5.
cēnāculum, cēnāculī — an upper room, a garret, a flat on an upper floor.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for an occupant, then the Latin for a block of flats.

You'll want to know: cēnāculum, cēnāculī.
cēnāculum, cēnāculī — "an upper room, a garret, a flat on an upper floor".
Poēta pauper in cēnāculō sub tēgulīs habitat. The poor poet lives in a garret under the roof tiles.
Second declension, neuter: cēnāculum, cēnāculī n. First a dining room, which in old houses was upstairs, then any upper room.

The word, taken apart.
cēnāculum is built on cēna 'dinner' with the suffix -culum 'place for' (EtymDB; Lewis & Short): a dining room, which was upstairs, and so an upper room.

Lesson 3 of 5.
contignātiō, contignātiōnis — a storey, a floor (of beams and boards).

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a block of flats, then the Latin for an upper room.

You'll want to know: contignātiō, contignātiōnis.
contignātiō, contignātiōnis — "a storey, a floor (of beams and boards)".
Īnsula alta est: sex contignātiōnēs habet. The block of flats is tall: it has six storeys.
Third declension, feminine: contignātiō, contignātiōnis f. A floor laid on joists, so one storey of a building.

The word, taken apart.
contignātiō is built on contignō 'to join with beams', from con- and tignum 'beam, timber' (EtymDB; Lewis & Short).

Lesson 4 of 5.
tignum, tignī — a timber, a log for building; building material.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for an upper room, then the Latin for a storey.

You'll want to know: tignum, tignī.
tignum, tignī — "a timber, a log for building; building material".
Fabrī tigna in plaustrō ad īnsulam novam vehunt. The workmen carry timbers on a wagon to the new block of flats.
Second declension, neuter: tignum, tignī n. Wood for building, cut and ready.

The word, taken apart.
tignum is inherited from Proto-Italic tegnom (EtymDB), which EtymDB traces to a PIE word for a pole or stake, with English stake as a cousin. Lewis & Short connect it instead with the root of texō 'to build', so the deeper source is not settled.

Lesson 5 of 5.
trabs, trabis — a beam, a timber; (in poetry) a ship.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a storey, then the Latin for a timber.

You'll want to know: trabs, trabis.
trabs, trabis — "a beam, a timber; (in poetry) a ship".
Magnae trabēs in tēctō templī sunt. There are great beams in the roof of the temple.
Third declension, feminine: trabs, trabis f. Bigger than a tignum. English architrave comes, through Italian trave, from it.

The word, taken apart.
trabs is inherited from PIE treb- 'wooden beam' (EtymDB), and EtymDB lists English thorp 'village' as a cousin. English architrave comes from it through Italian trave.
