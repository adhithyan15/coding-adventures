# el coste — what it comes to

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-COSTAR, ES-LEX-C311-ASK-08] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say **costar** and **el precio**. The verb has a noun of its own,
and it is not *precio*.

## You'll want to know: el coste
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-C456-CUENTA-04]; assesses=[ES-LEX-COSTAR] -->

**el coste** — the cost.

*El coste total* — the total cost. *El coste del envío* — the cost of postage.
*Sin coste adicional* — at no extra cost.

In Latin America you will more often hear **el costo**; Spain says *coste*.
Both are the noun sitting beside *costar*.

## Grammar Lens: coste, precio, cargo
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C456-CUENTA-04, ES-LEX-C456-CUENTA-03] -->

Three words now stand between you and a bill you can read:

| what it names | the word |
|---|---|
| what the seller asks | **el precio** |
| what it works out at for you | **el coste** |
| an amount added to your account | **el cargo** |

*El precio* is a number on a label. *El coste* is what the thing ends up
costing once postage and the rest are in — which is why a business talks about
*costes* and a shop window shows *precios*.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C456-CUENTA-04, ES-LEX-COSTAR] -->

Latin **constare**: **con-**, together, plus **stare**, **to stand**.

*Constare* meant to stand firm, to be settled — and from "this is settled" it
became "this is what it comes to". The price is the point at which the matter
stands still.

English took the same verb twice. Through French it wore down to **cost**;
lifted straight from Latin it gave **constant**, a thing that stands firm.
*Coste* and *constant* are one word, and you can hear the *n* that Spanish
dropped and English kept.

The *stare* underneath is the one inside **estar**, and that is the sense to
hold: a cost is a standing, a place the reckoning comes to rest.
