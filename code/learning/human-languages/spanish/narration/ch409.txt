Spanish, chapter 409: Finish, Prepare and Plan the Weekend.
7 lessons. All 7 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 7.
terminar — reach the boundary.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Recall el trabajo, the work. Now add the action that brings it to its endpoint: terminar, “to finish” or “to end.”

The word, taken apart.
Latin termināre meant to set a boundary or bring something to an end. Its base was terminus, a boundary. English term, terminal and terminate keep the same boundary picture. Spanish terminar is the everyday verb: terminar el trabajo, finish the work.

Grammar Lens: the regular -ar frame.
For “I finish,” remove -ar and add -o: termino. After another verb, keep the infinitive: quiero terminar. Hear the difference between a plan and a completed statement: quiero terminar / termino.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: terminar]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: terminar el trabajo]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: quiero terminar]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
To finish? (Terminar.)
Source: DLE: terminar.


Lesson 2 of 7.
preparar — make ready beforehand.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Recall el postre, dessert. Before it is served, someone has to make it ready: preparar un postre, “to prepare a dessert.” You can terminar a task; first you may need to preparar its parts.

The word, taken apart.
Latin praeparāre joined prae, “before,” to parāre, “make ready.” Spanish preparar and English prepare preserve that family almost unchanged. The older verb parar is visible inside, but do not translate the modern compound as “pre-stop”: use the whole word, “prepare.”

Grammar Lens: prepare a thing or prepare to act.
Use a noun directly: preparar un postre. Before another action, Spanish can use prepararse para; this lesson keeps the simpler A1 object frame.

Lesson 3 of 7.
el fin de semana — the week's end.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Spanish builds “weekend” as a transparent phrase: el fin de semana, literally “the end of the week.” It gives a time for a plan: voy a preparar el postre este fin de semana.

The phrase, taken apart.
Fin continues Latin fīnis, “boundary” or “end.” Semana continues Late Latin septimāna, the seven-day period. The little de joins them: fin de semana. Learn the three words as one planning block.

Grammar Lens: one weekend, many weekends.
For one, say el fin de semana. In the plural, only the first noun changes: los fines de semana. For a regular habit, trabajo los fines de semana; for one upcoming period, este fin de semana.

[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.

Sources: DLE: fin; DLE: semana.


Lesson 4 of 7.
reservar — set it aside.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
You have chosen el fin de semana. To book a room for it, use reservar: reservar una habitación.

The word, taken apart.
Latin reservāre meant “keep back” or “preserve.” That image still works: a hotel sets a room aside for you. English reserve is a close relative. Keep the history at that safe level; resemblance to servir is not a reason to invent a separate family story.

Grammar Lens: book a thing for people.
The thing follows directly: reservar una habitación. Add the people with para: para dos personas. Together: quiero reservar una habitación para dos personas.

Lesson 5 of 7.
incluir — close it inside the whole.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
You can reservar a room. Now ask what comes inside the price: ¿Incluye el desayuno?, “Does it include breakfast?”

The word, taken apart.
Spanish borrowed incluir from Latin inclūdere, literally “close in”: in, “in,” plus claudere, “close.” English include, exclude and conclude share the same learned family. Something included is enclosed inside the whole.

Grammar Lens: the useful third-person form.
The infinitive is incluir, but hotel messages often use incluye: el precio incluye el desayuno. In a question, keep the same form and lift the intonation: ¿Incluye el desayuno?
