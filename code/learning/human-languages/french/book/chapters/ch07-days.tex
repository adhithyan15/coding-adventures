\chapter{The Days of the Week}
\label{ch:days}

The seven-day week is one of humanity's oldest inheritances, and its day-names
are a map of the sky: the Romans named each day for a planet-god. French kept
that map almost intact --- and then religion overwrote the last two.
\emph{Practice lessons: \texttt{lessons/FR-C07-*.md}.}

\section{\emph{lundi} to \emph{vendredi} --- the planets of the week}
\label{lesson:jours-1}

Once you see the trick, five days decode at once.

\begin{sounds}
\emph{lundi} $=$ \emph{luh(n)-DEE}, with the nasal \emph{un} you met in
\emph{un} (one).\\[3pt]
The shared ending \emph{-di} is always \emph{DEE}.
\end{sounds}

Every French weekday ends in \emph{-di}, which is Latin \emph{di\={e}s},
``day.'' The first part is a planet-god. Read each one as ``[god]'s day'':

\begin{center}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}lll>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}X@{}}
\toprule
French & $\leftarrow$ Latin & planet-god & English echo \\
\midrule
\emph{lundi} & \emph{l\={u}nae di\={e}s} & the \textbf{Moon} (\emph{lune}) & \emph{Mon}day $=$ Moon-day \\
\emph{mardi} & \emph{Martis di\={e}s} & \textbf{Mars} & \emph{Tues}day (Tiw, the war-god $=$ Mars) \\
\emph{mercredi} & \emph{Mercuri\={\i} di\={e}s} & \textbf{Mercury} & \emph{Wednes}day (Woden $=$ Mercury) \\
\emph{jeudi} & \emph{Jovis di\={e}s} & \textbf{Jupiter} (\emph{Jove}) & \emph{Thurs}day (\textbf{Thor} $=$ Jupiter) \\
\emph{vendredi} & \emph{Veneris di\={e}s} & \textbf{Venus} & \emph{Fri}day (Frigg/Freya $=$ Venus) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

\begin{cousinweb}
Here's the beautiful part: \textbf{mardi} and \textbf{Tuesday} are the same day
--- both ``the day of the war-god'' --- but Latin used \emph{Mars} while the
Germanic peoples swapped in \emph{their} war-god, \emph{Tiw}.\\[3pt]
The Romans mapped their gods onto the planets; the Germanic tribes then mapped
\emph{their} gods onto the Roman ones (\emph{interpretatio germanica}). So
French \emph{jeudi} (Jupiter) and English \textbf{Thursday} (Thor) are the
king-god's day in two pantheons.
\end{cousinweb}

\section{\emph{samedi} and \emph{dimanche} --- the week's religious edge}
\label{lesson:jours-2}

Five days named for planets --- and then two that aren't. The weekend is where
religion overwrote astronomy: Saturn's day and the Sun's day were renamed by
Judaism and Christianity, and French shows both layers.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
French & $\leftarrow$ Latin & meaning \\
\midrule
\emph{samedi} & \emph{sambat\={\i} di\={e}s} $\leftarrow$ \emph{Sabbatum} & ``\textbf{Sabbath} day'' \\
\emph{dimanche} & \emph{di\={e}s Dominica} & ``the \textbf{Lord's} day'' \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\emph{samedi} still has the \emph{-di} (\emph{di\={e}s}), but the god is gone
--- replaced by the Hebrew \emph{shabbat}, ``rest.'' In \emph{dimanche} the
\emph{-di} jumped to the \emph{front} (\emph{di-manche}); \emph{Dominica} comes
from \emph{Dominus}, ``lord, master.''

\begin{cousinweb}
Two different rewrites.\\[3pt]
\textbf{samedi} would have been ``Saturn's day'' (as English
\textbf{Satur}day still is --- the one weekday English kept a Roman god for!).
But Latin Christians took the Jewish \textbf{Sabbath} (\emph{Sabbatum}
$\leftarrow$ Hebrew \emph{shabb\={a}t}, ``to rest'') and it became
\emph{samedi}. So English \emph{Saturday} and French \emph{samedi} are the same
day under two names --- the planet versus the Sabbath.\\[3pt]
\textbf{dimanche} dropped Sun-worship entirely: where English keeps
\textbf{Sun}day, French made it \emph{di\={e}s Dominica}, ``the Lord's day''
(\emph{Dominus} $\to$ English \textbf{dominion}, \textbf{dominate},
\textbf{dame}). Notice the \emph{di-} is now in front --- a fossil of
\emph{di\={e}s} that moved.
\end{cousinweb}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}
\toprule
French & English \\
\midrule
\emph{lundi} & Monday \\
\emph{mardi} & Tuesday \\
\emph{mercredi} & Wednesday \\
\emph{jeudi} & Thursday \\
\emph{vendredi} & Friday \\
\emph{samedi} & Saturday \\
\emph{dimanche} & Sunday \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
