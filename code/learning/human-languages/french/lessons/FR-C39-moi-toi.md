# moi et toi — the pronouns that can stand alone

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[FR-LEX-JE-01, FR-LEX-TU-VOUS-08, FR-GRAM-PRONOUN-REQUIRED-03, FR-LEX-QUOI-03] -->

[PAUSE 2s] **Je** and **tu** cannot stand alone either — they must lean on a
verb. You have seen that happen to *que* in the last lesson, and the same
accident produced the same result here.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[FR-LEX-MOI-01, FR-LEX-TOI-02]; assesses=[FR-LEX-JE-01, FR-LEX-TU-VOUS-08, FR-LEX-QUOI-03, FR-GRAM-VOUVOIEMENT-09] -->

> **moi** — me. · **toi** — you, informally.

> **Moi ?** — Me? · **Et toi ?** — And you? · **C'est moi.** — It's me.

You have said **et toi ?** and **et vous ?** for a long time as a fixed piece of
conversation. This is the word inside it.

**je** and **moi** are one Latin pronoun, **tu** and **toi** are another, and
each split for the reason *que* and *quoi* split: the form that lived unstressed
in front of a verb wore down, and the form that stood alone or after a
preposition kept its weight. Three pairs, one accident.

The polite twin of *toi* is **vous**, which does both jobs and does not split —
*et vous ?* is the same word as the *vous* in *vous parlez*.
