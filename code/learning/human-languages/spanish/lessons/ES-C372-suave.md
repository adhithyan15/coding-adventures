## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-C372-REF-50]; assesses=[] -->

**suave** — "smooth, soft, gentle."

Latin **suāvis** meant agreeable to the senses — pleasant to taste, to touch,
to hear.

English **sweet** is the same word. Not a translation of it: the same ancient
root, come down the Germanic line instead of the Latin one, changing sound by
sound until nothing recognisable was left on the surface. *Suave* and *sweet*
are twins raised in different houses.

Latin built a verb on that root too: **suādēre**, to advise, to urge. Which
means **persuade** is *per-* plus that verb — to make a thing thoroughly
**sweet** to somebody. You do not persuade a person by proving them wrong; the
word says you persuade them by making your position pleasant to accept. And
**dissuade** is the same move in reverse.

**Assuage** belongs here as well, arriving through French: to sweeten a grief
until it can be borne.

English also borrowed **suave** itself, straight off the Latin in the
fifteenth century. Its modern sense — smooth manners, a shade too smooth —
came later, under French influence, and is the language's own judgement rather
than anything the Romans put there.

Further back still, Greek *hēdys*, sweet, belongs to the same root — and
**hedonism** is built on it. Sweetness as a philosophy.
