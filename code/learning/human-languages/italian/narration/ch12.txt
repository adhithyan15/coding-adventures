Lesson 1 of 2.
undici becomes sedici — the ten you can still see.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
True to form, Italian keeps the teens closest to Latin — so close that the word for "ten" is still sitting in plain sight inside each one. Where French wore decem down to a bare -ze, Italian kept -dici.

Grammar Lens: six fusions.
There is a table here I cannot read to you — 4 columns and 6 rows, and it has more columns than can be held in the ear at once. Its columns are: one with no heading, Italian, from Latin, build. Come back and look at it when you have stopped.
Every one is digit + -dici, and -dici is Latin decem, "ten" — the same ten you say as dieci (10). Compare the sisters and Italian's clarity jumps out:
Latin sēdecim becomes Italian sedici (ten still audible), French seize (ten worn to a -ze), Portuguese dezesseis (rebuilt from scratch).
The digits are your Chapter 6 numbers barely changed: tre becomes tre-, quattro becomes quattor-, cinque becomes quin-, sei becomes se-.

Lesson 2 of 2.
diciassette becomes venti — the count turns around.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes for one table that cannot be read aloud. You can listen to everything else now — leave the section called Grammar Lens: the reversal until you have stopped, and I will say so again when we reach it.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Watch the language change direction. Through sedici (16), Italian put the digit first and the ten last. At 17, it turns the whole thing around and puts the ten first — and it keeps the -dici right there so you can see it happen.

Grammar Lens: the reversal.
There is a table here I cannot read to you — 4 columns and 5 rows, and it has more columns than can be held in the ear at once. Its columns are: one with no heading, Italian, build, order. Come back and look at it when you have stopped.
Same building blocks — dici ("ten") and your Chapter 6 digits — but from 17 the ten leads. The little linking sounds (diciAssette, diciANnove) are just Italian smoothing the joint, the doubling it loves.

What you've built: three different breaks.
This "give up fusing, go transparent" seam happens in every Romance sister, but not at the same place:
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
language: Portuguese. fuses up to: quinze (15). goes transparent at: 16 — dezesseis.
language: French. fuses up to: seize (16). goes transparent at: 17 — dix-sept.
language: Italian. fuses up to: sedici (16). goes transparent at: 17 — diciassette.
Three languages, one inherited Latin system, three different breaking points. (And German, you may recall, never breaks at all.)
venti (20) from vīgintī, the same root as French vingt and English vigesimal.
