Latin, chapter 314: Cāsus in viā.
6 lessons. All 6 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 6.
offendō, offendere — to strike against, to stumble; to offend.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to fall in, then the Latin for to fall down.

You'll want to know: offendō, offendere.
offendō, offendere — "to strike against, to stumble; to offend".
Nocte pedem ad saxum offendī et cecidī. At night I struck my foot against a stone and fell.
offendō, offendere, offendī, offēnsum. First a knock against something, then hurt feelings. English offend.

The word, taken apart.
offendō joins ob-, "against", and an old verb fendō, "to strike" (EtymDB), also seen in dēfendō, "to ward off". English offend comes from it.

Lesson 2 of 6.
praeceps, praecipitis — headlong; steep.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to fall down, then the Latin for to strike against.

You'll want to know: praeceps, praecipitis.
praeceps, praecipitis — "headlong; steep".
Senex praeceps per scālās cecidit. The old man fell headlong down the stairs.
One ending, genitive praecipitis: head first. Also 'steep': locus praeceps.

The word, taken apart.
praeceps joins prae, "in front", and caput, "head" (EtymDB): head first. English precipice comes from its family.

Equus subitō sē in fossam praecipitāvit. The horse suddenly threw itself headlong into the ditch.
praecipitō, praecipitāre, praecipitāvī, praecipitātum, from praeceps. English precipitate.

The word, taken apart.

Lesson 4 of 6.
plāga, plāgae — a blow, a wound.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for headlong, then the Latin for to hurl headlong.

You'll want to know: plāga, plāgae.
plāga, plāgae — "a blow, a wound".
Plāga in capite gravis erat. The blow on the head was serious.
Feminine, with a long ā. Do not confuse plaga, 'a region' or 'a hunting net'. English plague comes from it.

The word, taken apart.
plāga belongs with plangō, "to strike, to beat" (EtymDB), and matches Greek plēgē, "a blow" (Lewis & Short). English plague, a blow from heaven, comes from it.

Lesson 5 of 6.
saucius, saucia, saucium — wounded, hurt.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to hurl headlong, then the Latin for a blow.

You'll want to know: saucius, saucia, saucium.
saucius, saucia, saucium — "wounded, hurt".
Medicus mīlitēs sauciōs cūrābat. The doctor was looking after the wounded soldiers.
Wounded but alive; vulnus (taught) is the wound itself. Caesar counts the sauciī after a battle.

The word, taken apart.
saucius is a Latin word whose origin is not settled (Lewis & Short, EtymDB).
