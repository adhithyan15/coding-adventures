Latin, chapter 491: Tears and sighs.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
lacrimō, lacrimāre — to weep, shed tears.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for grief, then the Latin for sorrowful.

You'll want to know: lacrimō, lacrimāre.
lacrimō, lacrimāre — "to weep, shed tears".
Īnfāns tōtam noctem lacrimat. The baby cries all night.
lacrimō, lacrimāre, lacrimāvī, lacrimātum, from lacrima — a tear. English lachrymose comes from the same family.

The word, taken apart.
lacrimō is built on lacrima "a tear" (Lewis & Short), which EtymDB traces to a Proto-Indo-European word for "tear"; English tear is a cousin.

Lesson 2 of 5.
plōrō, plōrāre — to wail, cry aloud.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for sorrowful, then the Latin for to weep.

You'll want to know: plōrō, plōrāre.
plōrō, plōrāre — "to wail, cry aloud".
Puella plōrat, quia pila eius in flūmen cecidit. The girl is wailing because her ball fell into the river.
plōrō, plōrāre, plōrāvī, plōrātum. Louder than fleō: crying out loud. English deplore and implore come from its compounds.

The word, taken apart.
plōrō is a native Latin verb whose origin is not settled; Lewis & Short calls it doubtful. English deplore and implore come from its compounds dēplōrō and implōrō.

Lesson 3 of 5.
gemō, gemere — to groan, moan.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to weep, then the Latin for to wail.

You'll want to know: gemō, gemere.
gemō, gemere — "to groan, moan".
Aeger in lectō gemit. The sick man groans in bed.
gemō, gemere, gemuī, gemitum. Also of a dove cooing or a wheel creaking.

The word, taken apart.
gemō is a native Latin verb; Lewis & Short compares a Greek verb meaning "to be full", but EtymDB gives no parent, so the origin is not settled.

Lesson 4 of 5.
gemitus, gemitūs — a groan, moan.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to wail, then the Latin for to groan.

You'll want to know: gemitus, gemitūs.
gemitus, gemitūs — "a groan, moan".
Ē cubiculō gemitūs audīmus. We hear groans from the bedroom.
Masculine, fourth declension, from gemō.

The word, taken apart.
gemitus is the action noun of gemō "to groan", with the fourth-declension ending -tus (Lewis & Short; EtymDB derives it from gemō).

Lesson 5 of 5.
suspīrō, suspīrāre — to sigh.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to groan, then the Latin for a groan.

You'll want to know: suspīrō, suspīrāre.
suspīrō, suspīrāre — "to sigh".
Iuvenis suspīrat et dē puellā cōgitat. The young man sighs and thinks about the girl.
suspīrō, suspīrāre, suspīrāvī, suspīrātum: sub 'up from below' + spīrō 'to breathe'.

The word, taken apart.
suspīrō is built on sub- "up from below" and spīrō "to breathe" (Lewis & Short; EtymDB): a sigh is a breath drawn up from deep down. English inspire and respire share spīrō.
