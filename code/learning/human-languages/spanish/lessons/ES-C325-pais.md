## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-C325-PLACE-06]; assesses=[] -->

**el país** — "the country," meaning a nation.

Latin **pāgus** was a country district — a stretch of land with a village in it,
far from any city. Somebody who lived there was a **pāgānus**. Late Latin built
**pāgēnsis**, *belonging to the district*, and that word travelled north into
French as *pays* and came back over the mountains into Spanish as **país**.

English took *pāgānus* twice, by two different roads, and never noticed. Through
Church Latin it arrived as **pagan**: when the cities had changed religion the
country districts had not, so *country-dweller* and *unconverted* became the
same word. Through French *paisant* — from that same *pays* — it arrived as
**peasant**. A pagan and a peasant are one Latin word wearing two English coats.

Spanish keeps the *pays* family alive: **el paisaje** is the landscape, and **el
paisano** is a fellow countryman.
