# uno, dos, tres, cuatro, cinco — counting to five

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Numbers are among the most reusable words in any language. Spanish
keeps them close enough to Latin that English cousins can help you remember
them. This lesson asks for only **five**: **uno, dos, tres, cuatro, cinco**.

## Sounds you'll need
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[] -->

- `diphthong-ue` — **cuatro** begins *KWA-*.
- `r-tap` — give *tres* and *cuatro* one light tap, not an English *r*.

## The numbers, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-NUMBERS-1-5, ES-ETYMON-NUMBERS-1-5]; assesses=[] -->

| Spanish | Latin source | English memory hook |
|---|---|---|
| **uno** (1) | *ūnus* | **unit**, unique |
| **dos** (2) | *duo* | **duo**, dual |
| **tres** (3) | *trēs* | **trio**, triple |
| **cuatro** (4) | *quattuor* | **quart**, quarter |
| **cinco** (5) | *quīnque* | **quintet**, quintuplet |

The spellings changed, but the family resemblance remains. *Cinco* and
*quintet* both descend from *quīnque*; Spanish changed the opening sound while
English borrowed a more visibly Latin form.

## Grammar Lens: uno becomes un or una
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-GRAMMAR-UNO-APOCOPE]; assesses=[ES-LEX-CAFE, ES-GRAMMAR-CAFE-MASCULINE, ES-GRAMMAR-NOUN-GENDER, ES-LEX-NUMBERS-1-5] -->

When **uno** stands alone, it means “one.” Before a noun it shortens:

- **un** before a masculine noun: **un café** — one coffee / a coffee.
- **una** before a feminine noun: **una noche** — one night / a night.

This shortening is called **apocope**: the end drops away. Spanish uses the
same forms for “one” and “a/an”; context supplies the difference.
