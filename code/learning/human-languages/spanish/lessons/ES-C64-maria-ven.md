# María, ven — naming who you are talking to

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-GRAMMAR-COMMAND-TU-AFFIRMATIVE, ES-LEX-COMO-ESTAS] -->

[PAUSE 2s] *Ven* — come. *¿Cómo estás?* — how are you.

[PAUSE 1s] Both are aimed at somebody. Now say **who**.

## Grammar Lens: the comma is the grammar
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-GRAMMAR-VOCATIVE-COMMA]; assesses=[ES-LEX-PROFESOR] -->

> ***María****, ven.*
> *¿Cómo estás,* ***María****?*
> ***Profesor****, ¿puedo hablar?*

[PAUSE 2s] The name can go at the front or at the end, and either way it is
fenced off by a **comma**. That comma is not decoration — it is the entire
grammatical marking. There is nothing else to learn here.

## Why it's said this way
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-GRAMMAR-VOCATIVE-NOT-OBJECT]; assesses=[ES-GRAMMAR-PERSONAL-A, ES-ETYMON-QUIEN-QUEM] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Latin had a whole **case** for this — a set of endings whose only
job was addressing somebody. Spanish replaced all of it with a pause.

[PAUSE 2s] That is the shortest possible summary of what became of Latin's
endings. Twice now you have watched the same thing: the endings went, and
something small and audible took over the work — a preposition for the personal
*a*, and here a comma.

[PAUSE 2s] And notice the person you address takes **no** *a*. *Veo a María*
has María on the receiving end of the seeing; *María, ven* has her doing the
coming. Same name, opposite roles, and the comma is what tells them apart.
