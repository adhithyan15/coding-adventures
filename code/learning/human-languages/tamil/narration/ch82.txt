Lesson 1 of 6.
முதல் (mutal) — first, and you have had it all along.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, because the lesson points at something written down. You can listen to all of it now and come back to the parts that need looking at once you have stopped.

You'll want to know: முதல்.
முதல் (mutal) — "first".
You already know this word. Look at முதலில் (mutalil), "first of all", which this book already taught as a way of putting one thing before another:
முதல் (mutal) + இல் becomes முதலில்.
இல் is the ending that means "in" or "at". Take it off and what is left is முதல் (mutal), first — and it was standing there in your mouth the whole time, carrying the meaning, without ever being named.
முதல் நாள் (mutal nāḷ), the first day. முதல் பாதை (mutal pātai), the first road.

Across the family: the word four languages built on.
முதல் (mutal) is one of the oldest words in this family, and its sisters did four different things with it:
[a table of 4 rows, read aloud]
language: Tamil. what it did with mutal: kept the meaning outright. its word for "first": முதல் mutal.
language: Kannada. what it did with mutal: built an ordinal on it. its word for "first": ಮೊದಲನೆಯ modalaneya.
language: Telugu. what it did with mutal: built an ordinal on it. its word for "first": మొదటి modaṭi.
language: Malayalam. what it did with mutal: kept the word, changed the sense to "from". its word for "first": uses ഒന്നാം onnāṁ instead.
None of the four builds "first" on its own word for one, and three of them build it on this word. Tamil is where it still simply means first.

You'll want to know: இரண்டாவது.
இரண்டாவது (iraṇḍāvadu) — "second".
இரண்டு answers how many. இரண்டாவது (iraṇḍāvadu) answers which one in the line.
இரண்டாவது நாள் (iraṇḍāvadu nāḷ), the second day. இரண்டாவது கதவு (iraṇḍāvadu kadavu), the second door.

Grammar Lens: one ending, and one word that does not take it.
[a table of 1 row, read aloud]
cardinal: இரண்டு (iraṇḍu). ordinal: இரண்டாவது (iraṇḍāvadu).
Add -ஆவது (-āvadu) to a number and you have its ordinal. That is the rule, and it works on every number Tamil has.
There is exactly one exception in the whole set, and you met it last lesson: you do not say oṉṟāvadu for first. Tamil reaches for முதல் (mutal) instead.
So the one word a learner would have to memorise here is the one word this book did not have to teach you.

[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

[pause 3 seconds]

You'll want to know: முதலாவது.
முதலாவது (mutalāvadu) — "first", said the long way.
Look at what happened:
முதல் + ஆவது becomes முதலாவது (mutalāvadu).
The ending is the same ending. முதல் (mutal) takes -ஆவது exactly as இரண்டு did. So the exception is narrower than it looked a lesson ago: first does not refuse -ஆவது at all. It simply takes it on முதல் (mutal) rather than on ஒன்று.
The stem is the exception. The ending is not.
Both forms are current: முதல் நாள் and முதலாவது (mutalāvadu) நாள் are both the first day, and முதல் is the shorter and commoner of the two in front of a noun.

You'll want to know: மூன்றாவது.
மூன்றாவது (mūṉṟāvadu) — "third". மூன்று (mūṉṟu) takes -ஆவது, and nothing else changes.
Three is the number the Dravidian family never quarrelled over — Tamil mūṉṟu, Malayalam mūnnŭ, Kannada mūru, Telugu mūḍu, all keeping the long ū at the front. The ordinal keeps it too, because the ending only touches the end of the word.
மூன்றாவது கதவு (mūṉṟāvadu kadavu), the third door.
