Hindi, chapter 105: Speaking to Somebody.
5 lessons. You can do the first 3 of them in the car; after that you will want to have stopped.


Lesson 1 of 5.
मीरा जी (Mīrā jī) — putting a name in front of somebody.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say the small word you learned that turns a bare हाँ into a polite one.

You'll want to know: मीरा जी.
मीरा जी (Mīrā jī) — how you address somebody called Mira.
A name, then जी. That is the whole of it.

Grammar Lens: the word you already had, in a new position.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
what you say: जी हाँ. what it does: makes a yes polite.
what you say: मीरा जी (Mīrā jī). what it does: makes a name an address.
जी has been in your mouth since the early chapters, doing politeness work after a yes. Behind a name it does the same work in a different place: it turns somebody being talked about into somebody being talked to.
Hindi has no separate ending for this the way some languages do. The position is the grammar — the name comes first, जी follows it, and nothing else in the sentence changes.
It works for anybody you would use आप with, which is most people you have not known since childhood. With a close friend or a child, the bare name on its own is the ordinary thing.

Lesson 2 of 5.
प्रिय (priya) — the word a letter starts with.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Address somebody called Mira out loud. Today's word does the same work on the page.

You'll want to know: प्रिय.
प्रिय (priya) — dear.
It goes before the name: प्रिय (priya) मीरा.

Grammar Lens: the opposite side of the name.
[a table of 1 row, read aloud]
written: प्रिय (priya) मीरा. said: मीरा जी (Mīrā jī).
One goes in front and one goes behind, which is worth noticing because it is the kind of thing a reader gets backwards once and then remembers. प्रिय (priya) is an adjective describing the person, so it leads; जी is an honorific attached to them, so it follows.
प्रिय (priya) belongs to writing. Saying it to somebody's face is the sort of thing that would make them look up. A note, a card or a letter is where it lives, and that is the one place this chapter is heading.

Lesson 3 of 5.
संबोधन (sambodhan) — two openings, one question to pick between them.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say the word a written message opens with, and say which side of the name it sits on.

Grammar Lens: the two side by side.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
where: out loud. what you say or write: मीरा जी (Mīrā jī).
where: on paper. what you say or write: प्रिय (priya) मीरा.
One question chooses between them: are you speaking or writing? Answer it and the opening is decided, along with which side of the name the extra word goes on.

Grammar Lens: what the two have in common.
Neither of them changes the sentence that follows. Hindi marks address by putting a word beside the name rather than bending anything, so whatever you were going to say stays exactly as it was and the opening sits in front of it.
That is worth holding on to, because a great deal of what this track has taught you does the opposite. Endings agree, vowels shorten, verbs bend for who is speaking. Address is the corner of the grammar that asks nothing of the rest of the sentence.

Lesson 4 of 5.
प्रिय (priya) मीरा — a page with somebody at the top of it.

Before we start: this one needs your hands, so it is not a driving lesson. You will want a pen and something to write on. You can listen to all of it now and come back to the parts that need looking at once you have stopped.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say the written opening for a reader called Mira. Then recall the upright stroke that closes a Hindi sentence.

Writing: the three parts, with the model in front of you.
A short Hindi message has three parts stacked down the page:
प्रिय मीरा, (priya Mīrā,) नमस्ते। कल मिलते हैं। — अरुण.
Copy it once, keeping the three parts on their three lines.
Every word of that is something you have written before. What is new is the arrangement — two sentences you could already produce, lifted onto a page that now has a reader at the top of it and a writer at the bottom.

Grammar Lens: why the opening earns its own line.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
part: प्रिय मीरा, (priya Mīrā,) what it carries: who is being written to.
part: नमस्ते। कल मिलते हैं।. what it carries: what is being said.
part: — अरुण. what it carries: who is writing.
The opening is not a sentence and takes no । after it. A comma does that work, which is the one piece of punctuation here that behaves the way English punctuation does.
The two sentences underneath keep their । each. Being inside a message leaves the end of a Hindi sentence exactly as it was.

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
What are the three parts of a message? Which of them takes a comma rather than a ।? Who goes at the foot of the page?


Lesson 5 of 5.
No model — a whole message to a named reader.

Before we start: this one needs your hands, so it is not a driving lesson. You will want a pen and something to write on. You can listen to all of it now and come back to the parts that need looking at once you have stopped.

Warm-up.
Close the preceding lesson. Wait ten seconds. Name the three parts of a message to yourself without looking at them.

Writing: produce the message from the cue alone.
There is no Devanagari model and no romanized answer below. Keep only this cue:
Write to Arun. Greet him, then say you will meet tomorrow. Put your own practice name at the foot.
Write all three parts from memory. Then compare in four separate passes:
three parts present, in order;
the comma after the opening and one । after each sentence;
spelling and vowel marks;
the name at the foot.
Choose the first differing pass, repair only that dimension, and stop.

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Which part of a message names the reader? Which takes a comma rather than a ।? Whose name sits at the foot?
This is the short message the A1 writing task asks for in outline. It is not a timed mock, and its length is well under what that task sets.
[question — say your answer, then pause 40 seconds]
With no model, write the opening line of a short Hindi message to a reader called Arun.
