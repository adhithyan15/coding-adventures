# c'était, il y avait — the words a scene is made of

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[FR-GRAM-IMPARFAIT-01, FR-GRAM-CEST-01, FR-LEX-ILYA-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Build "I was speaking". (*Je parlais.*) Say "it's good". (*C'est
bon.*) Then "there is bread". (*Il y a du pain.*)

[PAUSE 1s] Hold on to all three. This lesson moves the last two into the
past.

## You'll want to know: c'était
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[FR-LEX-CETAIT-01]; assesses=[FR-VERB-ETRE-SOMMES-04, FR-GRAM-AVOIR-AGE-11, FR-LEX-SAMEDI-09] -->

One verb refuses last lesson's recipe: **être**. Its *nous* form, *nous
sommes*, gives nothing to build on, so it has a stem of its own, **ét-**,
with the same endings: *j'étais*, *tu étais*, *il était*, *nous étions*,
*vous étiez*, *ils étaient*.

The form you will use most is the one inside *c'est*:

> **C'était** bon. — It was good.
> **C'était** samedi. — It was Saturday.

*C'est* is now; **c'était** is how things were, all through some stretch of
the past.

**avoir** does follow the recipe: *nous avons* gives **j'avais**. Age is the
clearest case. *J'ai vingt ans* is now; *j'avais vingt ans* is the age you
were, back then.

## You'll want to know: il y avait
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[FR-LEX-IL-Y-AVAIT-02]; assesses=[FR-GRAM-PAS-DE-01, FR-LEX-BEAUCOUP-01] -->

You have **il y a**, "there is" and "there are". Its past is **il y avait**:

> **Il y avait** beaucoup de monde. — There were a lot of people.
> **Il n'y avait pas de** pain. — There was no bread.

Like *il y a*, it does not change for a plural: one *avait* for one thing or
for a hundred. And after the negative, *pas de* stays exactly as it was in
the present.

## The phrase, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[] -->

**était** goes back to Latin *stābat*, "he was standing", the standing verb
that lent *être* every form beginning *ét-*. **avait** goes back to
*habēbat*, "he was having", the past of *habēre*, the Latin verb that gave
**avoir** (EtymDB 2.1). In both, the Latin *b* wore away.

The little **y** of *il y a* is inherited from Latin *ibī*, "there" (EtymDB
2.1; Lewis & Short, under *ibi*). So *il y avait* is, word for word, "it had
there".
