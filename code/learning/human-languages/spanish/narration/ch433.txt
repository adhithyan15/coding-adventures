Spanish, chapter 433: El Edificio Y Sus Normas.
7 lessons. All 7 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 7.
el portal — the big door, and the space behind it.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say la puerta. Say el edificio. Between the street and the stairs of a Spanish block there is a space you have no word for. Here it is.

You'll want to know: el portal.
el portal — the street door of a block of flats, and the hall behind it.
One word covers both the door and the space. Te espero en el portal means downstairs, inside, out of the rain.

The word, taken apart.
puerta plus -al, an ending that makes a bigger or more general thing of the noun it lands on.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
the thing: la puerta. the larger thing: el portal.
the thing: el árbol. the larger thing: la arboleda.
the thing: la rama. the larger thing: el ramal.
English has no single word here, which is why translations reach for doorway, entrance, lobby or hallway and none quite fits. When a Spanish word has no clean English partner, learning the situation beats learning a gloss: the portal is where you buzz, where the post boxes are, and where you wait.

[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: the door, then the entrance hall]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: I will wait for you in the portal]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]

Lesson 2 of 7.
la portera — the person of the portal.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say el portal. Say el vecino. There is somebody who is neither a neighbour nor a stranger, and who is in the portal most mornings.

You'll want to know: la portera.
la portera — the concierge, the caretaker. el portero if a man.
She cleans the stairs, takes in parcels, knows who is away, and is the person a notice tells you to leave a key with.

The word, taken apart.
portal and portera share puerta, and the ending -ero / -era makes the person associated with a thing:
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
the thing: la puerta. the person: la portera.
the thing: el pan. the person: el panadero.
the thing: el zapato. the person: el zapatero.
You met -ería for the place and -dor for the doer. This is the third: -ero for the person whose job is that thing. Three endings, three different questions, and all of them turn nouns you have into words you have not met.
el portero also means the goalkeeper. The one who guards the gate, in both cases.

The concierge? (la portera.) What does -ero name? (The person whose job it is.) What else is el portero? (The goalkeeper.)


Lesson 3 of 7.
el garaje — spelled to be said.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say el coche. Say la portera. Now name the floor below the portal where the cars live.

You'll want to know: el garaje.
el garaje — the garage, the underground car park of a building.
Said ga-RA-je, with the j as the throaty jota.

The word, taken apart.
French garage came into Spanish and was respelled. French writes the sound at the end with -ge; Spanish has no such spelling, so it wrote what it heard with the letter it uses for that sound: j.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
language: French. spelling: garage.
language: Spanish. spelling: garaje.
That habit is worth more than the word. Spanish borrows freely but respells to its own rules, which is why fútbol, champú and chófer look the way they do. A borrowed word you meet in Spanish has usually been made to spell itself the Spanish way, so read it aloud by Spanish rules and the original often appears.

Lesson 4 of 7.
la norma — what is written on the wall.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say el portal. Say el garaje. Both have a list on the wall. Name what that list is called.

You'll want to know: la norma.
la norma — the rule. las normas — the rules, the regulations.
You meet it in the plural almost always, as a heading: NORMAS DE USO, NORMAS DE PRÉSTAMO.

The word, taken apart.
Latin norma was a carpenter's square — the tool for checking a right angle. A thing that matched it was correct; a thing that did not was off. The word travelled from the tool to the standard itself, and from there into every European language.
That history explains a family you can now read at sight:
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
word: la norma. what it holds: the standard.
word: normal. what it holds: matching the standard.
word: enorme. what it holds: out of the square, huge.
enorme is the one worth noticing. E- out of, plus norma: outside the square. Something enormous is something that will not fit the measure.

Lesson 5 of 7.
junto — together, or beside.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say la calle. You can put a thing in a street. Say that two people did something together and see whether you have the word.

You'll want to know: junto.
juntos — together. junto a — next to, beside.
Same word, two jobs, and the little a is what separates them.

Grammar Lens: the a decides.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
you say: cenamos juntos. it means: we had dinner together.
you say: junto a la puerta. it means: next to the door.
Without a, it describes the people and agrees with them: juntos, juntas. With a, it stops describing anybody and becomes a position — and then it stops agreeing, staying junto whatever follows.
That is a small thing to watch, and it is the sort of small thing a reader trips on: las cajas, juntas is a comment about the boxes; junto a las cajas is where you are standing.

[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Together? (juntos.) Next to? (junto a.) Which one changes for gender? (The one without a.)


Lesson 6 of 7.
(portal, portera, garaje, norma, junto) — from cold.

Warm-up.
[pause 3 seconds]
Close the lessons before this one. Say the entrance hall, the concierge and the garage.

Grammar Lens: three endings, three questions.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
ending: -dor. the question it answers: who or what does it?
ending: -ería. the question it answers: where is it kept?
ending: -ero. the question it answers: whose job is it?
All three land on puerta or a word like it and hand you a noun you never learned. Do not read a rule into it — many words have no ending at all, and el garaje and la norma in this very chapter came in whole from elsewhere.
What these three chapters have built is a habit: before treating a long word as new, look for a piece you already hold.

What you've built.
You could describe a home. You could not describe a building. La casa, la puerta and el vecino were yours; the hall, the person who cleans it, the car park beneath it and the list of rules on its wall were not.
Those four are where written Spanish actually meets a resident — on a notice, in a message from an administrator, in an instruction about a key. And junto a is the small word that lets you say where any of it is.
