# Gujarati / ગુજરાતી — Reference

A **reference**, not a chapter — the lessons teach reading through real words.
This page gathers the system and flags what is special about the **Gujarati
script** and Gujarati sounds.

## How the Gujarati script works (the three facts)

1. **Consonants carry a built-in "a" — and there is no top line.** ક = ka, ન =
   na, મ = ma, સ = sa — written left to right. Gujarati is Devanagari's sister,
   but it **dropped the *shirorekhā*** (the horizontal bar Devanagari, Bengali,
   and Gurmukhi hang their letters from). This missing top line is the fastest
   way to recognise Gujarati — the "headless script."
2. **A vowel sign changes the built-in "a":** કા kā · કિ ki · કી kī · કુ ku ·
   કે ke · કો ko. An *anusvāra* ં nasalises (છું *chhũ*); a *chandra* ઁ marks
   the light nasal on some words.
3. **Standalone vowels have their own letters:** અ a · આ ā · ઇ i · ઉ u · એ e ·
   ઓ o — used when a word starts with a vowel (આભાર *ābhār*).

## What is special about Gujarati sounds and grammar

- **Three genders.** Masculine / feminine / **neuter**, visible on adjectives
  and possessives as *-o / -ī / -ũ*: *sāro / sārī / sārũ* ("good"), *māro /
  mārī / mārũ* ("my"). Hindi kept only two; Gujarati (like Marathi) kept three.
- **A copula all its own — *chhe*.** Gujarati's "is" is neither Hindi's *hai*
  nor Sanskrit's *asti*; it grew from an Old Gujarati form (*achhaï*): *hũ chhũ*
  (I am), *tũ chhe*, *te chhe*, *tame chho*.
- **The retroflex ળ (ḷ).** Gujarati keeps this extra "hard l," like Marathi and
  the Dravidian south, that Hindi lost — as in *maḷvũ* ("to meet").
- **Verb goes last; "in" comes after the noun.** Gujarati is
  subject–object–verb and uses **post**positions: *Amdāvad-mā rahũ chhũ* ("I
  live in Ahmedabad").
- **The trade-language layer.** Centuries of Arabian-Sea commerce wove
  **Perso-Arabic** and Portuguese words into everyday Gujarati — even "how are
  you" is answered with the Persian loan *majā* ("enjoyment" ← *maza*).

## A note for the LaTeX book

Noto Sans Gujarati carries no basic-Latin punctuation glyphs, so `.` `?` `!` `-`
must stay **outside** the `\gu{}` span in the book (they render as a tofu box
inside it). Keep sentence punctuation in the surrounding roman text.

## Gujarati in the family

Gujarati (*gujarātī*) is **Indo-Aryan** — from Sanskrit, like Hindi, Marathi,
and Punjabi — and its name comes from the **Gurjar**, a people who settled
western India and gave their name to the land of *Gujarāt*. Spoken by some 55
million people in Gujarat and across a vast trading diaspora, it is the mother
tongue of **Mohandas K. Gandhi**. The lessons trace its Sanskrit roots, its
Perso-Arabic trade layer, and — where they reach — the wider Indo-European
family that also shaped English.
