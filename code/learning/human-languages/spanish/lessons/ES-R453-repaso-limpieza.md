## Grammar Lens: the same Latin word, twice, differently
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C453-LIMPIEZA-03, ES-LEX-C453-LIMPIEZA-04] -->

Two pairs in this chapter show the single most useful fact about Spanish
vocabulary: a Latin word can arrive **twice**, once through speech and once
through books, and the two arrivals look nothing alike.

| worn down by speech | lifted from the page |
|---|---|
| la r**ue**da | r**o**tativo |
| el cons**ejo** | cons**ultar** |

The left-hand column went through hundreds of years of mouths: vowels broke,
consonants softened, syllables fell out. The right-hand column was copied out
by a clerk who could read Latin, so it kept its shape.

The practical payoff: when a Spanish word looks **more** like its Latin or
English cousin, it is usually the later, bookish arrival — and it will belong
to writing, forms and notices rather than to the kitchen. That is a register
clue you can read straight off the spelling.

## Grammar Lens: the four noun-makers, complete
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C453-LIMPIEZA-01, ES-LEX-C453-LIMPIEZA-02] -->

| ending | example | gender |
|---|---|---|
| -anza | la mudanza | feminine |
| -miento | el seguimiento | masculine |
| -eza | la limpieza | feminine |
| -ción | la instalación | feminine |

Three feminine, one masculine. Which ending a word takes has to be met
word by word; the gender never does.
