Lesson 1 of 7.
mirar — pointing the eyes on purpose.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, because the lesson points at something written down. You can listen to all of it now and come back to the parts that need looking at once you have stopped.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say mira, the little word that opens a sentence. Say ver and el ojo. Mira is an order from a verb you have never been given. Name it.

You'll want to know: mirar.
mirar — to look, to look at, to watch.
Mírame — look at me. Estaba mirando por la ventana — I was looking out of the window. Mirar la tele is to watch television, though ver la tele is commoner in Spain.
mira and mire — the forms you already use to mean look…, listen… — are this verb told to somebody, nothing more. You have been giving an order for chapters without knowing which verb it came from.

Grammar Lens: ver and mirar are not interchangeable.
English has this split too and hides it, because see and look at feel like one idea. They are not.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
verb: ver. what it describes: the eyes receive — it happens to you.
verb: mirar. what it describes: the eyes are aimed — you do it.
Veo el mar desde aquí — I can see the sea from here, whether or not I meant to. Miro el mar — I am looking at it, deliberately.
So ver belongs with oír (to hear) and mirar with escuchar (to listen). The test is whether you chose: if you could have not done it, the verb is mirar.

The word, taken apart.
Latin mīrārī meant to wonder at, to be amazed — not to look at all. The looking came from the wondering: you stare at what astonishes you, and after enough centuries the staring is all that is left.
The wonder survives in the relatives:
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
word: el milagro. the wonder in it: a miracle — a thing wondered at.
word: admirar. the wonder in it: to admire — to wonder at.
word: mirar. the wonder in it: to look.
English took the same root for miracle, admire, marvel and mirage — a thing that astonishes you and is not there.

Lesson 2 of 7.
mover — and the phone that is named after it.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, because the lesson points at something written down. You can listen to all of it now and come back to the parts that need looking at once you have stopped.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say el móvil and trasladar. Say mirar. Your phone is called móvil for a reason. Name the verb inside it.

You'll want to know: mover.
mover — to move something. moverse — to move yourself.
¿Me ayudas a mover la mesa? — will you help me move the table? No te muevas — do not move. El niño no para de moverse — the child will not sit still.
The stem breaks under stress, like doler and dormir: muevo, mueves, mueve, but movemos.

The word, taken apart.
Latin movēre, to move. El móvil is mōbilis — movable — from the same verb, so a mobile phone is literally the movable one.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
word: mover. the movement in it: to move it.
word: el móvil. the movement in it: the movable thing.
word: el automóvil. the movement in it: the self-moving thing.
That last one is the whole of automobile explained: auto- from Greek autós, self, on a Latin stem. A car is a thing that moves without being pulled.

Why it's said this way.
Mover and trasladar are not the same act, and a notice picks carefully.
Mover is physical and local: shift the table, shift your arm. Trasladar is institutional and from-somewhere-to-somewhere: the office was moved, the patient was transferred. You mueves a chair across a room; a company traslada its headquarters.
Use mover for a thing you could push with your hands, and trasladar when an address changes. Getting it backwards is not a grammar mistake — it is a register mistake, and it is the kind a reader notices.

Lesson 3 of 7.
acompañar — going as the one who shares bread.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say el compañero and recall what it is made of. Say mover. Turn the companion into something you do.

You'll want to know: acompañar.
acompañar — to go with somebody, to accompany.
Te acompaño — I will come with you, and also I am here with you at a funeral. ¿Me acompañas al médico? — will you come with me to the doctor? Acompañado de — accompanied by, on a form or a menu.
It is the ordinary way to offer to come along, and it is warmer than the English accompany, which sounds formal.

The word, taken apart.
a- plus compañ- plus -ar, and the middle is the word you already took apart.
el compañero is com- together plus pānis, bread — the one you share bread with. So acompañar is to go along as that person, and a Spanish speaker offering te acompaño is, at root, offering to share a meal's worth of company.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
word: el compañero. the bread in it: the one you share it with.
word: acompañar. the bread in it: to be that person for somebody.
word: el pan. the bread in it: the bread itself.
That is the third verb in these chapters built the same way — a- plus a word you own plus -ar, meaning to make it that: acercar from cerca, acompañar from compañero.

Why it's said this way.
The warmth is not decoration; it decides where the word is used.
At a Spanish funeral the standard thing to say is te acompaño en el sentimiento — I accompany you in the feeling. English has no comfortable equivalent and reaches for I'm sorry for your loss, which places the speaker outside the grief. The Spanish places them alongside it.
That is worth knowing before you need it, because it is a fixed phrase and improvising in its place is conspicuous.

Lesson 4 of 7.
directo — straight through, and live.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say recto and el tren. Say acompañar. Recto is the shape of a line. Name the word for a train that does not stop.

You'll want to know: directo.
directo — direct, straight through.
Un tren directo — a through train, no changes. Un vuelo directo — a direct flight. En directo — live, on radio or television, which is the use you will meet most.
Directo also describes a person: es muy directo means blunt, and in Spain it is usually a compliment.

The word, taken apart.
di- apart, thoroughly, plus -recto, which you already have.
Recto is Latin rectus, straightened, ruled — the participle of regere, to rule or guide. To dirigere was to set something straight, to aim it, and directo is the result of that aiming.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
word: recto. the straightening in it: straight.
word: directo. the straightening in it: straightened towards.
word: dirigir. the straightening in it: to direct, to conduct.
English keeps regere everywhere once you look: correct (straightened with), erect (straightened up), regular (by the rule), and rule itself.

Why it's said this way.
En directo is the one to fix, because it is not obvious from the parts.
A broadcast en directo is going straight from the event to you with nothing in between — no recording, no editing. English calls that live, naming the event; Spanish names the path. Once you see that, en directo stops needing translation: the line is straight.
The opposite is grabado, recorded. A Spanish television corner reading EN DIRECTO is telling you that what you are watching is happening now.

Lesson 5 of 7.
el partido — a side split off.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say la parte, jugar and el deporte. Two teams are a whole divided in two. Name what they are about to play.

You'll want to know: el partido.
el partido — the match, the game.
El partido del domingo — Sunday's match. ¿Viste el partido? — did you see the match? Jugar un partido — to play a game.
It is also the political party: el partido socialista. And sacar partido de algo is to make the most of something.

The word, taken apart.
partido is the past participle of partir, to divide — and partir is la parte, which you have.
So partido means divided, and everything it names is something a whole was split into.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
what it names: el partido (match). what was divided: the players, into two sides.
what it names: el partido (party). what was divided: the electorate, into factions.
what it names: sacar partido. what was divided: the advantage, cut out of a situation.
One word, no coincidence. A football match and a political party are both a side that a whole was split into, and English needs two unrelated words — match, from an old sense of equal, and party, which is the same Latin pars arriving through French.

Why it's said this way.
Partir has a second life worth having, because it looks like a contradiction.
Partir also means to leave, to set off — el tren parte a las ocho. How does divide become depart?
Because leaving is a division: you separate yourself from the place and the people. English does exactly the same thing and hides it in the Latin — depart is de- plus the same partire, and a parting is both a splitting and a goodbye.
So a train that parte is cutting itself away from the platform, and a train that is directo does not divide the journey at all. The two words in this chapter that describe a train describe opposite things about it.

What you've built.

Why it's said this way.
Directo appears twice and means two different things, and nothing but the noun tells you which.
En directo, about the match: nothing between the event and the screen — live. Un tren directo: nothing between the two stations — no changes. Both are the same picture, a straight line with nothing interrupting it, applied once to time and once to distance. Spanish did not need two words because it named the line rather than the thing.
Then look at vemos and miro. ¿Vemos el partido? — the match arrives at their eyes, and ver is right. Miro los horarios — she is aiming her eyes at a timetable on purpose, and mirar is right. A Spanish speaker would not swap them, and the pair in one short exchange is the clearest test of the distinction you will get.
