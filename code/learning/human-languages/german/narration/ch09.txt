German, chapter 9: Nine, Ten, and the Months That Are Numbers.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
neun — "nine," in the vowel of Deutsch.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Ninth number, and again nothing new to learn in the mouth — you got this vowel from the name of the language itself.

You'll want to know: neun.
neun — "nine." Said noyn.
The eu is the oy of English boy, which is exactly what it does in Deutsch. So neun rhymes with English coin, and it is one syllable with an n at each end.
Do not let the spelling talk you into new-n. German eu is never two separate vowels.

The word, taken apart.
neun is English nine, and the two have kept the same skeleton: n, a vowel, n. Only the vowel wandered — German to oy, English to eye.
The Latin cousin is the odd one out here. Novem has a v where the Germanic words have nothing, which is why Spanish nueve and French neuf look like a different word. Between neun and nine there is nothing to explain.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: "neun" — noyn, rhyming with coin]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: "Deutsch" then "neun" — the same eu]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: "sieben, acht, neun"]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
What is "nine"? (Neun, said noyn.) How is eu said? (Like the oy of boy, as in Deutsch.) What do neun and nine share? (N, a vowel, n — only the vowel moved.)


Lesson 2 of 5.
zehn — "ten," and one consonant in three photographs.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
The last one. And it is the number that shows both of German's sound laws working, one after the other, on the same letter.

You'll want to know: zehn.
zehn — "ten." Said tsayn.
The z is the ts you met in zwei, and the h does what it did in wohnen and gehen: it says nothing and stretches the vowel in front of it. So: ts, a long ay, and an n.

The word, taken apart.
Latin decem, English ten, German zehn. One word, photographed at three moments of the same consonant walking:
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
Stage: what Latin kept. Word: decem. The sound: d.
Stage: the first law. Word: ten. The sound: d became t.
Stage: the second law. Word: zehn. The sound: t became ts.
The first step is the law from the drei lesson, the one that separated Germanic from Latin. The second is the later shift you already know from gut and machen — the one that only German went through, which is why English stopped at ten and German carried on to zehn.
So the distance between ten and zehn is not a different word. It is one extra step down a road English stopped walking.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: "zehn" — tsayn, long vowel, silent h]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: "decem, ten, zehn" — the same consonant, three times]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: "acht, neun, zehn"]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
What is "ten"? (Zehn, said tsayn.) What does the h do? (Nothing — it lengthens the vowel.) Walk decem to zehn. (D became t, and German took the t on to ts.)


Lesson 3 of 5.
sechs bis zehn — finishing the count.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Five more words learned one at a time. Same job as before: make them one run, then join the two runs together.

You'll want to know: the second run.
sechs, sieben, acht, neun, zehn.
Said straight through: zeks, ZEE-ben, akht, noyn, tsayn.
And the second five are the same story as the first — English twins, Latin only a cousin:
[a table of 5 rows, read aloud]
German: sechs. English: six. What changed: almost nothing; chs is x.
German: sieben. English: seven. What changed: the v softened to a b.
German: acht. English: eight. What changed: German still says the scrape English writes as gh.
German: neun. English: nine. What changed: only the vowel moved.
German: zehn. English: ten. What changed: an old d to t, then t to ts.

[your turn — say: all ten from the start, in one breath if you can]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: the ten backwards, from "zehn" down to "eins"]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[repeat that twice]
"eins, zwei, drei, vier, fünf, sechs, sieben, acht, neun, zehn".

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Count from six to ten. (Sechs, sieben, acht, neun, zehn.) Now all ten. Which of the ten is borrowed from Latin? (None — every one is the English number's twin.)


Lesson 4 of 5.
Homegrown numbers, imported months — German kept its own numbers and borrowed its month names, and the seams show.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
You have ten numbers and not one of them came from Latin. That is worth holding onto, because the very next thing German counts with did come from Latin — and you can hear the join.

Grammar Lens: a language keeps some words and buys others.
A language does not import evenly. The words people use every hour of every day are the hardest to displace, and the words that arrive with an institution come in whole.
Numbers are the first kind. German counts with its own inherited words, and so does English. Month names are the second kind: they arrived with the Roman calendar, and both languages took them whole.

Why it's said this way.
The Roman year began in March. Count from there and the last four months of the year land on the Latin words for seven, eight, nine and ten — which is why those four month names are numbers wearing a disguise, in German exactly as in English.
Say each Latin number and then the German one that means the same thing:
[a table of 4 rows, read aloud]
The Latin number inside: septem — seven. German counts it: sieben.
The Latin number inside: octo — eight. German counts it: acht.
The Latin number inside: novem — nine. German counts it: neun.
The Latin number inside: decem — ten. German counts it: zehn.
Not one pair sounds like a pair. The month names came from one language and the counting words from another, and German never reconciled them — because there was never any reason to.
You will meet the German month names themselves in a later chapter. What matters now is that when you get there, they will look Latin, and the numbers you have already learned will still be German.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: "sieben, acht, neun, zehn" — the German four]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: "septem, octo, novem, decem" — the Latin four]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: which kind of word a language keeps, and which it buys]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Did German borrow its numbers? (No — they are its own.) Did it borrow its month names? (Yes, from Latin, with the calendar.) Why do four of them carry the wrong number? (The Roman year began in March, so septem was the seventh month.)


Lesson 5 of 5.
Practice — counting, and asking for one.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Ten numbers, one article, and one fact about which words a language keeps. Put them to work.

Grammar Lens: the ten, and what each one cost.
[a table of 10 rows, read aloud]
German: eins. Said: eyns. English twin: one.
German: zwei. Said: tsvy. English twin: two.
German: drei. Said: dry. English twin: three.
German: vier. Said: feer. English twin: four.
German: fünf. Said: fewnf. English twin: five.
German: sechs. Said: zeks. English twin: six.
German: sieben. Said: ZEE-ben. English twin: seven.
German: acht. Said: akht. English twin: eight.
German: neun. Said: noyn. English twin: nine.
German: zehn. Said: tsayn. English twin: ten.
Ten numbers and not one of them borrowed. Every one is the English word with a predictable coat on, which is the whole reason this chapter was cheap.

The exchange.
[a table of 5 rows, read aloud]
Eins, zwei, drei … means One, two, three…
Und? means And?
… vier, fünf, sechs, sieben, acht, neun, zehn! means …four, five, six, seven, eight, nine, ten!
Sehr gut! means Very good!
Danke! — Bitte. means Thanks! — You're welcome.
Every word in that exchange is one you already own, and seven of them you learned this chapter.
Then the article, on the two nouns guten Tag and gute Nacht are built from:
der Tag becomes ein Tag, die Nacht becomes eine Nacht.
Eins on its own keeps its -s, because nothing follows it. The moment a naming word arrives, the -s comes off.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: all ten, straight through]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: the exchange, both voices]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: "eins" as a count, then "ein" as an article — the -s comes off]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[repeat that twice]
"eins, zwei, drei, vier, fünf, sechs, sieben, acht, neun, zehn".

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Count to ten. When does eins become ein? (In front of a naming word.) Are the German numbers borrowed from Latin? (No — they are the English numbers' twins.) Which German words were borrowed from Latin? (The month names.)
