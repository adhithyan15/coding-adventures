You'll want to know: 得.
得 — de — the particle between a verb and how well it is done.
你说得很好 nǐ shuō de hěn hǎo — You speak well.
The next lesson puts the character in your hand. It is the de you already say at the end of juéde.

The word, taken apart.
得 (de) is written with the character of dé 'to get', inherited from Middle Chinese tok (Guangyun, in Baxter's transcription), glossed 'gain and loss'. Its use as a light particle after a verb is a later development, assumed here, not quoted.

What you've built.
得 and 的 are two different words, and both are said de, with no tone.
的 joins an owner or a description to a noun: 我的书. 得 (de) joins a verb to how well it went: 说得很好.

Script — the left of 很, then 日, a bar and 寸.
得 (de).
Four pieces, and you write every one of them already.
彳, three strokes, the left of 很: a short left-falling stroke, a longer one under it, then the vertical down to the foot.
日, four strokes, small, at the upper right: the left side, the top that turns down the right side, the middle bar, the bottom bar.
A long bar under 日, across the whole right side.
寸, three strokes, under the bar, as on the right of 对 and 时: the bar, the vertical that hooks to the left at its foot, then the dot.
3 + 4 + 1 + 3 = eleven.

Lesson 3 of 7.
你说得很好 (nǐ shuō de hěn hǎo) — how well, with 得.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Three words in a fixed order, and the middle one is the character you have written.
[once you have stopped driving — read: 说得很好, aloud — shuō de hěn hǎo]
[your turn — recall: say 快 — kuài, fast]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — recall: say 汉语 — Hànyǔ, Chinese]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Grammar Lens: the verb, 得, then how well.
After a verb, 得 (de) brings in how well or how fast it is done. The adjective after 得 (de) usually takes 很.
你说得很好 nǐ shuō de hěn hǎo — You speak well. 他说得很快 tā shuō de hěn kuài — He talks fast.
With an object, say the verb twice: once with the object, once with 得 (de):
他说汉语说得很快 tā shuō Hànyǔ shuō de hěn kuài — He speaks Chinese fast.
很 here is light, as it is in 你很好: it carries the sentence more than it means very.

[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: 你做饭做得很好 — nǐ zuò fàn zuò de hěn hǎo — You cook well.]

Lesson 4 of 7.
liúlì — fluent.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: read the last lesson's sentence, then a word for slow.
[once you have stopped driving — read: 你说得很好, aloud — nǐ shuō de hěn hǎo]
[your turn — recall: say màn, slow — R3, durable]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

You'll want to know: liúlì.
liúlì — "fluent".
tā Hànyǔ shuō de hěn liúlì — He speaks Chinese fluently.
Its characters come later; for now it is a word for the ear.

The word, taken apart.
liúlì is built from liú 'to flow' and lì. Both are inherited from Middle Chinese, ljuw and lijH (Guangyun, in Baxter's transcription); the Guangyun explains ljuw, after an older dictionary, as water moving, and glosses lijH 'favourable' and, after the same dictionary, 'sharp'. 'Fluent' is the pair's own sense.

What you've built.
liúlì sits after 得 with 很, as the other adjectives do: shuō de hěn liúlì.
Here the topic, Hànyǔ, goes first, so the verb is said only once.

You'll want to know: búcuò.
búcuò — "not bad; pretty good".
nǐ zuò de búcuò — You did pretty well.
不 rises before the fall of cuò: búcuò, as in bú qù.
Its second character comes later; for now it is a word for the ear.

The word, taken apart.
búcuò is built from 不 'not' and cuò 'wrong', the word you know: not wrong, so pretty good. Both are inherited from Middle Chinese, pjut and tshak (Guangyun, in Baxter's transcription); the Guangyun glosses tshak 'mixed' and 'crossing slantwise', and how it came to mean wrong is not settled.

What you've built.
búcuò is praise that sounds modest. After 得 it needs no 很: zuò de búcuò.
On its own it answers how something was: hěn búcuò, really quite good.

You'll want to know: qīngchu.
qīngchu — "clear; clearly".
tā shuō de hěn qīngchu — He speaks clearly.
Its characters come later; for now it is a word for the ear.

The word, taken apart.
qīngchu is built from qīng 'clear' and chu. Both are inherited from Middle Chinese, tshjeng and tsrhjoX (Guangyun, in Baxter's transcription); the first is glossed 'clear, far from mud'. The Guangyun gives tsrhjoX as a plant and a place name, so how it came into 'clear' is not settled.

What you've built.
qīngchu answers how well something came across: shuō de hěn qīngchu, said clearly.
It is light at the end, chu with no tone.

The exchange.
A: 他说汉语说得很快 tā shuō Hànyǔ shuō de hěn kuài — He speaks Chinese fast. B: 对 他说得很好 duì, tā shuō de hěn hǎo — Yes, and he speaks well. A: tā Hànyǔ shuō de hěn liúlì — His Chinese is fluent. B: 你呢 nǐ ne — And you? A: wǒ shuō de hěn màn — I speak slowly. B: nǐ shuō de hěn qīngchu — You speak clearly. A: 你做饭做得很好 nǐ zuò fàn zuò de hěn hǎo — You cook well. B: búcuò, wǒ juéde hěn jiǎndān — Not bad. I find it simple.
Every compliment has the same order: the verb, 得 (de), then how well. When the object comes along, the verb is said twice; when the language goes first as the topic, once.

Reading — the exchange at once.
[once you have stopped driving — read: the exchange through once without stopping, then answer the question below]
[question — say your answer, then pause 12 seconds]
How does A say they speak?

What you've built — how well.
The verb, 得 (de), then how well; say the verb twice when it has an object:
你说得很好 nǐ shuō de hěn hǎo — you speak well.
他说汉语说得很快 tā shuō Hànyǔ shuō de hěn kuài — he speaks Chinese fast.
tā shuō de hěn qīngchu — he speaks clearly.
nǐ zuò de búcuò — you did pretty well.
Listen. Tā Hànyǔ shuō de zěnmeyàng?
