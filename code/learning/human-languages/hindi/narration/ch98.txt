Hindi, chapter 98: A Day, In The Order It Happens.
8 lessons. You can do the first 6 of them in the car; after that you will want to have stopped.


Lesson 1 of 8.
से (se) — where you came from, and what you came by.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
You have पर for being at a place. Try to say you came from one, and notice you have the place and no way to leave it.

You'll want to know: से.
से (se) — "from," and also "by."
घर से (se) — from home. हाथ से (se) — by hand.
Like पर, it trails its noun.

The word, taken apart.
English splits this into two words and Hindi does not:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
English: from home. Hindi: घर से (se).
English: by hand. Hindi: हाथ से (se).
Both are the same idea seen from two angles. Where something starts and what it travels through are, to Hindi, one relation: the thing proceeds from the noun. A journey proceeds from your house; an action proceeds from your hand.
Once you see that, the second use stops being a second word to learn. A vehicle you travel in takes से for exactly the same reason your hand does.

Grammar Lens: two postpositions, one habit.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
postposition: पर. what it marks: at a point.
postposition: से (se). what it marks: from it, or by way of it.
Both trail the noun, both are one syllable, and neither has an English word sitting in the same place. That is the whole class: the small word comes second, and knowing the noun tells you where to put it.
A question you will be asked in a speaking interview turns on this word: आप कहाँ से (se) हैं? — where are you from?

Lesson 2 of 8.
बस से (bas se) — the same three letters, twice over.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
You already read बस as a reply meaning enough, that's all. Read it again in बस से (bas se) and notice it cannot possibly mean that here.

You'll want to know: बस से.
बस — the vehicle. बस से (bas se) — by bus.
मैं बस से (bas se) स्कूल जाता हूँ। — I go to school by bus.

The word, taken apart.
These are two different words that happen to be spelled alike:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
word: बस. where it comes from: Persian bas. what it means: enough, that's all.
word: बस. where it comes from: English bus. what it means: the vehicle.
The first has been in the language for centuries. The second arrived with the vehicle, and English had shortened it from omnibus — Latin for "for everybody" — which is a fair description of a bus and a strange thing to find inside three Devanagari letters.
Nothing marks them apart on the page. The sentence does the work: a reply on its own is the old word, and a noun with a postposition behind it is the vehicle.

Grammar Lens: reading the one from the other.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
what you see: बस।. which word: enough.
what you see: बस से (bas se). which word: by bus.
The moment से follows it, it is a thing you can travel in. That is the sort of decision a reading paper tests without ever saying so, and you now make it from the shape of the sentence rather than from the word.

Lesson 3 of 8.
उठना (uṭhnā) — where a day starts.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
You can say सुबह and you can build a habit with -ता. Try to say what you do first every morning, and find the verb missing.

You'll want to know: उठना.
उठना (uṭhnā) — to get up, to rise.
मैं सुबह उठता हूँ। — I get up in the morning. मैं सुबह सात बजे उठती हूँ। — I get up at seven in the morning.

The word, taken apart.
The stem is उठ-, and it opens with a vowel where no consonant can carry the mark — so the word begins with the full letter उ, which is why this is the first word in the track to start that way.
The verb is broader than English get up. It covers a person leaving a bed, a person standing from a chair, smoke rising, and a price going up. What all of those share is movement away from the ground, and Hindi treats that as one action.
Its opposite, बैठना, is to sit — the same idea in the other direction.

Grammar Lens: it takes the endings you already have.
[a table of 4 rows, read aloud]
what you mean: I get up (habit). the form: उठता हूँ.
what you mean: I am getting up. the form: उठ रहा हूँ.
what you mean: I used to get up. the form: उठता था.
what you mean: I will get up. the form: उठूँगा.
Four tenses on one new stem, and you had all four endings before you had the verb. That is what a stem costs you now: one word, and the whole grid comes with it.

Lesson 4 of 8.
काम (kām) — the noun that was there all along.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
You learned काम (kām) करना long ago as one expression meaning to work. Say it, and then ask yourself what the first half of it means on its own.

You'll want to know: काम.
काम (kām) — work, a job, a task.
काम (kām) पर — at work. मैं काम (kām) पर जाता हूँ। — I go to work.

The word, taken apart.
काम (kām) करना was never one word. It is काम (kām) plus करना — literally to do work — and Hindi builds an enormous number of verbs this way: a noun, plus the verb to do.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
the pattern: काम (kām) + करना. what it makes: to work.
the pattern: a noun + करना. what it makes: to do that noun.
Knowing this turns a memorised phrase into a machine. Any noun you meet can be handed to करना, and the result is usually the verb a learner would otherwise look up separately.
The word comes from Sanskrit कर्म (karma), "act, deed" — the same word English borrowed whole. A karma is a thing done, and a काम (kām) is a thing to do.

Grammar Lens: which postposition a workplace takes.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
मैं काम (kām) पर जाता हूँ। means I go to work.
मैं बस से (bas se) काम (kām) पर जाता हूँ। means I go to work by bus.
पर for the place you end up at, से (se) for what carries you there. Both of them trail their nouns, and the verb stays at the end however many of them you stack in front of it.

Lesson 5 of 8.
पहले (pahle) — putting one thing in front of another.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
You can say that you get up and that you go to work. Try to say which of the two happens first, and notice the track has never given you a way.

You'll want to know: पहले.
पहले (pahle) — first, beforehand, earlier.
पहले (pahle) मैं उठता हूँ। — First I get up.

The word, taken apart.
पहला is first, the ordinal, and पहले (pahle) is the same word in the shape an adverb takes — the -ए ending you have now met on adjectives, on the past copula and on the continuous.
It does two jobs, and the difference is where it stands:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
in front of the sentence means first, to begin with.
after a time word means before that time.
सुबह से पहले (pahle) is before morning — the time word, से, then पहले (pahle). That is the second use, and it is why this lesson comes after the one on से.

Grammar Lens: sequencing a day.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
पहले (pahle) मैं उठता हूँ। means First I get up.
मैं काम (kām) पर जाता हूँ। means I go to work.
Two sentences and an order between them. That is the whole of what a one-minute spoken description needs — and until this word existed, every sentence in such a description floated free of the others.

Lesson 6 of 8.
के बाद (ke bād) — the other end of the pair.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
You have पहले (pahle) for first. Guess its partner, and then read on to see what Hindi makes you put in front of it.

You'll want to know: के बाद.
बाद (bād) — after. In a sentence it almost always arrives as के बाद (ke bād).
काम (kām) के बाद (ke bād) — after work.

The word, taken apart.
बाद comes from Arabic بعد (baʿd), "after" — a plain borrowing that kept its job exactly.
What is Hindi's own is the के in front of it. A two-part postposition like this one is built out of the possessive you already have: the after of work. Hindi has a shelf of these, and they all share the shape:
[a table of 1 row, read aloud]
shape: noun + के + a word. example: काम (kām) के बाद (ke bād).
So के बाद (ke bād) is not a new kind of thing. It is the possessive का / के / की you met with uskā and hamārā, put to work as glue.

Grammar Lens: the pair, and a day in order.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
what you mean: first. the shape: पहले (pahle).
what you mean: before X. the shape: X से (se) पहले (pahle).
what you mean: after X. the shape: X के बाद (ke bād).
Notice the asymmetry: before takes से (se) and after takes के. There is no tidy reason to give you; it is a pair of fixed habits, and the way to keep them apart is to say each one a few times rather than to reason about it.

Lesson 7 of 8.
dincaryā kī tālikā — a day, in the order it happens.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, because the lesson points at something written down. You can listen to all of it now and come back to the parts that need looking at once you have stopped.

Warm-up.
[pause 3 seconds]
Name the verb a day starts with and the noun you travel to, before you look at the table.

Grammar Lens: the six.
[a table of 6 rows, read aloud]
word: उठना (uṭhnā). what it adds to a day: the action it starts with.
word: काम (kām). what it adds to a day: the place it goes to.
word: बस. what it adds to a day: what carries you there.
word: से (se). what it adds to a day: from, and by.
word: पहले (pahle). what it adds to a day: which of two came first.
word: के बाद (ke bād). what it adds to a day: which came second.
Four of the six are not vocabulary in the ordinary sense. They are joints: things that let two sentences be put in a relation instead of standing side by side.

Grammar Lens: the three small words, side by side.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
small word: पर. what it marks: at a point.
small word: से (se). what it marks: from it, or by way of it.
small word: के बाद (ke bād). what it marks: later than it.
All three trail their noun. That is now the whole postposition habit, and the only thing left to learn about any new one is which meaning it carries.

Grammar Lens: a day, assembled.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
पहले (pahle) मैं सुबह उठता हूँ। means First I get up in the morning.
मैं बस से (bas se) काम (kām) पर जाता हूँ। means I go to work by bus.
काम (kām) के बाद (ke bād) मैं घर आता हूँ। means After work I come home.
Three sentences, in order, every word of which is yours.

Lesson 8 of 8.
मेरी दिनचर्या (merī dincaryā) — the minute you are asked to fill.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say what time you get up, using the clock word from the future chapter. That is the first line of what follows.

Reading: a whole day.
पहले (pahle) मैं सुबह सात बजे उठती हूँ। मैं बस से (bas se) काम (kām) पर जाती हूँ। काम (kām) के बाद (ke bād) मैं घर आती हूँ। घर पर मैं हिंदी बोलती हूँ।.
Four sentences, and every word in them is one you have been taught. Read it twice — once for meaning, once listening for how the joints do the work.

Grammar Lens: what is holding it together.
[a table of 4 rows, read aloud]
joint: पहले (pahle). what it does: opens the sequence.
joint: से (se). what it does: names what carries her.
joint: पर. what it does: names where she ends up.
joint: के बाद (ke bād). what it does: hooks sentence three to sentence two.
Take those four out and you have four unrelated statements. Put them back and you have a description, which is what the one-minute task is graded on.

How to answer.
Say your own, out loud, in four sentences:
what time you get up.
how you travel, and where to.
what you do after that.
what you do once you are home.
[pause 6 seconds]
Say it a second time and change nothing but the endings, as the other gender would. The habit endings and the joints stay exactly where they are.
