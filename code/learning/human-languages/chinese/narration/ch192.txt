Mandarin Chinese, chapter 192: Signs in a Building.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
cèsuǒ — toilet; lavatory.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Mandarin for to go abroad, then the Mandarin for to travel for work.

You'll want to know: cèsuǒ.
cèsuǒ — "toilet; lavatory".
qǐngwèn cèsuǒ zài nǎr — Excuse me, where's the toilet?
Its characters come later; for now it is a word for the ear.

The word, taken apart.
cèsuǒ is built from cè 'a privy' and suǒ 'a place', both inherited from Middle Chinese, tsrhiH and srjoX (Guangyun, in Baxter's transcription). The modern cè is not the regular outcome of that form, and how it came is not settled.

What you've built.
One more word for reading notices and messages.

You'll want to know: diàntī.

Its characters come later; for now it is a word for the ear.

The word, taken apart.
diàntī is built from 电 'electricity' and tī 'a ladder', the tī of lóutī, both inherited from Middle Chinese, denH and thej (Guangyun, in Baxter's transcription), which glosses the second as wooden steps.

Lesson 4 of 5.
páiduì — to queue; to line up.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Mandarin for lift (in a building), then the Mandarin for car park.

You'll want to know: páiduì.
páiduì — "to queue; to line up".
dàjiā dōu zài páiduì — Everyone is queuing.
Its characters come later; for now it is a word for the ear.

The word, taken apart.
páiduì is built from pái 'to set in a row' and duì 'a file of people'. The second is inherited from Middle Chinese dwojH (Guangyun, in Baxter's transcription), glossed 'a troop'; the dictionary glosses the first 'to push aside', so how it came to mean lining up is not settled.
