French, chapter 438: Flâner en ville.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
flâner — to stroll, to wander about.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the French for the boulevard, then the French for the passer-by.

You'll want to know: flâner.
flâner — "to stroll, to wander about".
Le dimanche, j'aime flâner au marché. On Sundays I like strolling round the market.
Un flâneur — someone who strolls with no aim.

The word, taken apart.
flâner came into French from a Norman dialect word, which is usually traced to Old Norse flana 'to rush about'. EtymDB lists the verb without a source.

Lesson 2 of 5.
balader — to go for a walk (mostly reflexive: se balader); to take round.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the French for the passer-by, then the French for to stroll.

You'll want to know: balader.
balader — "to go for a walk (mostly reflexive: se balader); to take round".
Après le dîner, nous nous baladons au bord de la rivière. After dinner we go for a walk by the river.
Mostly reflexive and informal: se balader. Une balade — a walk.

The word, taken apart.
balader is built on balade 'walk, outing', from ballade 'dancing song', which goes back to Late Latin ballāre 'to dance' (Lewis & Short). EtymDB lists the verb without a source.

Le chien errait seul dans la rue. The dog was wandering alone in the street.
Stronger than flâner: no aim at all, sometimes lost.

errer is inherited through Old French errer from Latin errāre 'to wander, to go astray' (EtymDB).

Lesson 5 of 5.
arpenter — to pace up and down; to stride along.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the French for to walk about, then the French for to wander.

You'll want to know: arpenter.
arpenter — "to pace up and down; to stride along".
Pendant deux jours, nous avons arpenté la ville à pied. For two days we walked the town from end to end.
Also arpenter le couloir — to pace up and down the corridor.

The word, taken apart.
arpenter is built on arpent, an old measure of land (EtymDB), from Latin arepennis 'half an acre', a word Latin took from Gaulish (Lewis & Short): first to measure a field by walking it.
