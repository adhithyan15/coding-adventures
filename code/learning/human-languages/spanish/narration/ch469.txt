Spanish, chapter 469: La impresora de la sala 2.
6 lessons. You can do the first 5 of them in the car; after that you will want to have stopped.


Lesson 1 of 6.
imprimir — to print.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say el impreso and la prisa. The first is this verb's participle and the second is its distant cousin.

You'll want to know: imprimir.
imprimir — to print.
Voy a imprimir el documento.
Imprimir a doble cara — to print on both sides, where cara is the face of the sheet: the same word you learned for a face, lent to paper.
You already have the participle. When you learned el impreso, it was given to you as the irregular past participle of imprimir — so the verb has been named in this book once, without ever being yours to use. Now it is.

Grammar Lens: the participle you already hold.
Most Spanish verbs build a participle by rule. This one does not, and you have had the exception longer than the verb.
The irregular participles gave you hecho, dicho, visto and puesto; cubierto and roto joined the list later. Impreso belongs on it too.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
verb: imprimir. participle: impreso.
verb: escribir. participle: escrito.
He impreso el documento — I have printed the document. You will also meet imprimido, which the Academy allows, but impreso is what a notice prints and what a printer's menu says.
And el impreso as a noun — a form to fill in — is that same participle hardened, which is the move el pedido taught you.

The word, taken apart.
Latin imprimere: in-, into, plus premere, to press. To print is to press something into a surface, which is exactly what a press does and exactly what the word has always meant.
Premere is the root under la prisa — a hurry is a pressing. Follow it outward and English fills up: press, pressure, print, imprint, express (pressed out), compress, impression — a mark pressed in, whether into wax or into a person.
So a printer does not "produce" a page. Historically it presses one, and the word kept the machine in it long after the machine changed.

Lesson 2 of 6.
la impresora — the printer.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say la lavadora and el impreso. One lends the ending, the other lends the stem, and together they make the machine.

You'll want to know: la impresora.
la impresora — the printer.
La impresora de la sala 2 está fuera de servicio.
Una impresora en color — a colour printer.
You could have reached this word before being told, which is what la lavadora promised: a machine is often its verb plus -dora.

Grammar Lens: the article says which one you mean.
El contador settled one thing already: the ending does not decide the article, since la lavadora is feminine and el contador masculine off the same -dor. Treat that as known.
What this word adds is a case where the article is not merely a fact to memorise but is carrying the meaning. Lavar has no human washer in ordinary speech, so -dora was free to mean the machine and nothing else. Imprimir is not like that. Printing was a trade long before it was a box on a desk, and the stem kept both.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
Spanish: el impresor. what it is: the person who prints.
Spanish: la impresora. what it is: the machine that prints.
Say el impresor about the box and you have promoted it to a tradesman. With contador and lavadora the article was something to learn; here it is something to choose.
English will not help you: it calls both of them a printer and leaves the sentence to sort it out. Spanish has decided in advance, in one letter.

The word, taken apart.
Same imprimere as the verb: in- plus premere, to press into.
Why feminine rather than masculine? El contador told you the ending does not decide; this is the mechanism underneath. Impresora started life as an adjective leaning on a noun — la máquina impresora, the printing machine. The noun dropped away and left its gender behind. So the article you cannot predict from the ending was fixed by a word that is no longer said.

Lesson 3 of 6.
conectar — to connect.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say el ordenador, then imprimir. Nothing gets printed until one more verb has happened.

You'll want to know: conectar.
conectar — to connect, to join one thing to another.
No puedo conectar la impresora al ordenador.
The noun is la conexión, and it is worth a second look: the verb has a c, the noun has an x. Say co-ne-XIÓN, weight on the last syllable.

Grammar Lens: conectar and conectarse.
The same verb does two different jobs depending on whether the thing being joined is an object out in front of you or yourself.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
what you mean: join a thing to something. what you say: conectar la impresora.
what you mean: get yourself online. what you say: conectarse a internet.
So no me puedo conectar means I can't get connected — you, not the printer. The pronoun is doing the whole of that work, and dropping it turns the sentence into a report about some object you never named.

The word, taken apart.
The Academy's dictionary derives conectar from English to connect — and English had built that out of Latin conectere: con-, together, plus nectere, to tie.
So the Latin is real, and the route is not the one it looks like. The word did not sit in Spanish since Rome waiting for wires. It came in from English in the modern era, carrying Latin parts back to a Latin language.
You have now met that happening three ways in a row:
el ordenador — an old Spanish word that borrowed a new meaning from French;
internet — an English word borrowed whole, with only the beat changed;
conectar — an English word borrowed as a shape, its pieces Latin all along.
Nectere is a knot-word. English keeps it in nexus, a tying-together, and in annex, tied on. A nexus is not a vague link — it is the place where the threads are actually knotted.

Lesson 4 of 6.
la llamada — the call.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say el pedido and conectar. One is a thing made from a verb; the other is what you do before the thing will work.

You'll want to know first.
el pedido — the lesson whose table already showed you today's word.
perder — you will want its participle in a moment.

You'll want to know: la llamada.
la llamada — the call.
You have seen this word once, in the middle of somebody else's lesson. El pedido listed it as an example of a participle hardened into a noun, and then moved on. Here it is as yours.
Voy a hacer una llamada.
Devolver la llamada — to return the call. Una llamada perdida — a missed call, the notice your phone leaves you.

Grammar Lens: the vowel is a rule, the gender is not.
Which vowel the participle takes is decided for you, and the participle lesson gave you that rule.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
the verb ends: -ar. the participle takes: -ado, -ada.
the verb ends: -er, -ir. the participle takes: -ido, -ida.
Llamar is an -ar verb, so -ada. Pedir is an -ir verb, so -ido. That much you can work out.
What you cannot work out is the article. El pedido is masculine. La llamada and la entrada are feminine. Nothing in pedir, llamar or entrar predicts which way each one went — the gender was settled by whatever noun the participle used to lean on, and that noun is long gone.
So learn the article with the word, the way you learned el impreso and la impresora. Working out the ending is free; the gender is not.

The word, taken apart.
Llamada is llamar made into a thing, and llamar you have had since the very beginning — the lesson where you learned to say your name traced it to Latin clamare, to cry out, and showed you the cl- that became ll-.
What is worth adding is how literal the noun still is. A llamada is a crying-out, and Spanish has not forgotten it: una llamada de atención is a warning — a call for attention, not a phone call at all. The telephone borrowed a word that was already doing the job.

Lesson 5 of 6.
(imprimir, la impresora, conectar, la llamada) — from cold.

Warm-up.
[pause 3 seconds]
Four words, no prompts. What the machine does, what the machine is, what you do to the machine first, and what you make when it will not work.

You'll want to know: the four, cold.
[a table of 4 rows, read aloud]
English: to print. Spanish: imprimir.
English: the printer. Spanish: la impresora.
English: to connect. Spanish: conectar.
English: the call. Spanish: la llamada.

Grammar Lens: two of these you could have guessed.
Two of the four were reachable before you were told, and for the same reason: you already held the verb and the ending.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
built from: imprimir + -dora. gives you: la impresora.
built from: llamar + -ada. gives you: la llamada.
La lavadora taught the first ending and el pedido the second, and el contador already warned you that the ending settles nothing about the article. What this chapter added is a case where the article is not only unpredictable but meaningful: la impresora is the machine, el impresor is the person, and the one letter is the whole difference.

The words, taken apart.
This chapter put three different routes side by side, and the words look alike on the page whichever route they took.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
word: imprimir. how it got here: inherited — Latin imprimere, all the way down.
word: conectar. how it got here: borrowed from English, its parts Latin.
word: la llamada. how it got here: inherited — Latin clamare, through llamar.
Conectar is the odd one. It is the only word here whose Latin arrived by way of English, which is why it looks as native as the other three and is not.

Lesson 6 of 6.
(el aviso de la impresora) — read it, then say what you will do.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, because the lesson points at something written down. You can listen to all of it now and come back to the parts that need looking at once you have stopped.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say la impresora and imprimir. The notice below is about the first and stops you doing the second.

Reading: the notice, and the message.
Taped to the machine:
AVISO La impresora de la sala 2 está fuera de servicio. La de la tercera planta funciona.
And a message from a colleague:
No me puedo conectar a internet desde mi ordenador. ¿Puedes imprimir tú el documento? Si no, te devuelvo la llamada en diez minutos.

Grammar Lens: what each text asks of you.
The notice reports a state. The message asks for something and offers a way out if the answer is no. They want different replies.
[a table of 1 row, read aloud]
the notice: está fuera de servicio. the message: ¿puedes imprimir tú el documento?
Two details in the message repay a second look.
La de la tercera planta. The notice drops impresora and leaves la standing for it. Spanish does this freely once the noun is on the table, and a notice will do it to save a line.
¿Puedes imprimir tú? The tú is not needed for the grammar — the verb already says who. It is there to point: you, rather than me, because I cannot. A pronoun that could be dropped and is not is doing work.

Guided Practice.
[pause 3 seconds]
Say which printer is broken and which one works, using la on its own for the second the way the notice does.
[pause 3 seconds]
Now answer the message: say you will print the document, and say why your colleague could not — the connection, not the machine.

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
What is wrong with the machine? (Está fuera de servicio.) What is wrong for your colleague? (No se puede conectar.) What will you do? (Imprimir el documento.)
