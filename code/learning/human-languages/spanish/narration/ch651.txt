Spanish, chapter 651: En el tren.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
el vagón — the carriage, the railway car.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Spanish for the change, then the Spanish for to validate.

You'll want to know: el vagón.
el vagón — "the carriage, the railway car".
Nuestro asiento está en el vagón tres. Our seat is in carriage three.
El vagón restaurante — the dining car.

The word, taken apart.
vagón was borrowed from English wagon (EtymDB), which itself came from Dutch wagen.

Lesson 2 of 5.
el maquinista — the train driver, the engine driver.

Warm-up.

You'll want to know: el maquinista.
el maquinista — "the train driver, the engine driver".
El maquinista para el tren en la estación. The train driver stops the train at the station.
Built on la máquina: the first trains were pulled by steam engines.

The word, taken apart.
maquinista is built on máquina "machine" with -ista (EtymDB); máquina is a learned borrowing from Latin māchina, from Greek mēkhanḗ.

la taquilla — "the ticket office, the box office; the locker".
Compramos los billetes en la taquilla. We bought the tickets at the ticket office.
In Latin America also la boletería.

The word, taken apart.
taquilla is a little taca "a small cupboard". Where taca comes from is not settled.

Lesson 4 of 5.
la litera — the berth, the bunk.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Spanish for the train driver, then the Spanish for the ticket office.

You'll want to know: la litera.
la litera — "the berth, the bunk".
Dormimos en litera en el tren de noche. We slept in a berth on the night train.
At home, una litera is a bunk bed.

The word, taken apart.
litera came from Catalan llitera (EtymDB), built on llit "bed", which comes from Latin lectus "a couch, a bed" (Lewis & Short). French litière is a cousin.

Lesson 5 of 5.
apearse — to get off (a train or bus), to step down.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Spanish for the ticket office, then the Spanish for the berth.

You'll want to know: apearse.
apearse — "to get off (a train or bus), to step down".
Nos apeamos en la próxima estación. We are getting off at the next station.
A formal word heard in announcements; in speech people say bajarse.

The word, taken apart.
apearse is usually traced to a Vulgar Latin verb appedāre "to set foot down", built on Latin pēs, pedis "a foot" (Lewis & Short).
