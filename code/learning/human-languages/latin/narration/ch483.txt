Latin, chapter 483: Pool, Whirlpool, Chasm, To trickle, To flood.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
stagnum, stagnī — a pool, a pond; standing water.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to flow, then the Latin for to swim across.

You'll want to know: stagnum, stagnī.
stagnum, stagnī — "a pool, a pond; standing water".
In silvā stagnum quiētum est, et rānae cantant. In the wood there is a quiet pool, and frogs sing.
Second declension, neuter: stagnum, stagnī n. Still water, not running. English stagnant comes from the verb stagnāre.

The word, taken apart.
The origin of stagnum is not settled: Lewis & Short compare Greek tenagos 'shallow water', but EtymDB gives no parent. English stagnant comes from stagnāre, built on it.

Lesson 2 of 5.
gurges, gurgitis — a whirlpool, an abyss of water; a flood.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to swim across, then the Latin for a pool.

You'll want to know: gurges, gurgitis.
gurges, gurgitis — "a whirlpool, an abyss of water; a flood".
Nauta in gurgitem cadit, sed amīcī eum servant. A sailor falls into a whirlpool, but his friends save him.
Third declension, masculine: gurges, gurgitis m. Also a glutton, one who swallows everything. English regurgitate comes from it.

The word, taken apart.
gurges is inherited and Lewis & Short link it with gula 'throat'; EtymDB lists Greek gorgyra 'underground drain' as a cousin. English regurgitate comes from a word built on it.

Lesson 3 of 5.
vorāgō, vorāginis — a chasm, an abyss; a whirlpool.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a pool, then the Latin for a whirlpool.

You'll want to know: vorāgō, vorāginis.
vorāgō, vorāginis — "a chasm, an abyss; a whirlpool".
In mediō forō terra sē aperit, et vorāgō magna fit. In the middle of the forum the ground opens, and a great chasm forms.
Third declension, feminine: vorāgō, vorāginis f., from vorō 'to swallow'.

The word, taken apart.
vorāgō is built on vorō 'to swallow, to devour' (Lewis & Short): a hole that swallows things up. English voracious comes from vorō.

Lesson 4 of 5.
mānō, mānāre — to trickle, to flow, to drip.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a whirlpool, then the Latin for a chasm.

You'll want to know: mānō, mānāre.
mānō, mānāre — "to trickle, to flow, to drip".
Aqua frīgida ē saxō mānat. Cold water trickles out of the rock.
mānō, mānāre, mānāvī, mānātum. Also of news: to spread. English emanate comes from ēmānāre.

The word, taken apart.
The origin of mānō is not settled: Lewis & Short connect it, as probable, with madeō 'to be wet', but EtymDB gives no parent. English emanate comes from the compound ēmānāre.

Lesson 5 of 5.
inundō, inundāre — to flood, to overflow.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a chasm, then the Latin for to trickle.

You'll want to know: inundō, inundāre.
inundō, inundāre — "to flood, to overflow".
Vēre flūmen agrōs inundat. In spring the river floods the fields.
inundō, inundāre, inundāvī, inundātum, from in- and unda 'wave'. English inundate comes from it.

The word, taken apart.
inundō is built from in- and undō 'to rise in waves', from unda 'wave' (EtymDB; Lewis & Short). English inundate comes from it.
