# firmar — to make it firm

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C305-NAME-01, ES-LEX-ESCRIBIR-07, ES-LEX-C441-TRATO-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say **el nombre**, **escribir** and **el contrato**. Writing your
name at the bottom of one of those is not the same act as writing it at the top.

## You'll want to know: firmar
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-C441-TRATO-02]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C305-NAME-01] -->

**firmar** — to sign. **la firma** — the signature, and also *a firm* in the
business sense.

*Firme aquí, por favor* — sign here, please. *Sin firmar* — unsigned, the state
a form is in when the clerk hands it back to you.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C441-TRATO-02] -->

Latin **firmus**, firm, solid, not moving. To **firmar** is **to make firm**.

The name at the bottom is not information; it is the act that hardens a piece of
paper from a proposal into an obligation. Spanish says that outright, where
English borrowed *sign* from *signum*, a mark, and kept only the mark.

| the word | what it is built on |
|---|---|
| firmar | *firmus*, firm |
| to sign | *signum*, a mark |

English has *firmus* too, in **firm**, **affirm** and **confirm** — all of them
about making something hold.

## Why it's said this way
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C441-TRATO-02, ES-LEX-C441-TRATO-01] -->

Look at what the two words in this chapter are doing so far.

**Contrato** draws the parties together. **Firmar** makes the result solid. They
are two halves of one physical picture: pull the sides in, then set them.

That picture is why **la firma** can also mean a company. A firm is a name that
has been made firm — an entity that holds, that you can sign with and be bound
by. Spanish did not need a second word for it, and the double meaning is not a
coincidence you have to memorise.
