Mandarin Chinese, chapter 4: One Box, Two Characters, and the First Thing You Can Refuse.
7 lessons. The first lesson already needs your eyes or your hands, so save this one for when you have stopped.


Lesson 1 of 7.
口 (kǒu) — the box, on its own.

Before we start: this one needs your hands, so it is not a driving lesson. You will want a pen and something to write on and your eyes, for letter shapes on the page. You can listen to everything else now — leave the sections called Script — the shape and Guided Practice until you have stopped, and I will say so again when we reach it.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Write 四. Walls, then the two inner strokes, then the bottom last.

Script — the shape.
口 (kǒu).
Your hand can already write this. Three strokes, in exactly the order you learned inside 四, and the rule from there is the whole instruction:
the left vertical, top to bottom.
the top and right side in one turning stroke — the héngzhé, across then down without lifting.
the bottom bar, closing it — last.
Three strokes, two pen lifts. Walls, then the door.
One honest note, because it will save you trouble later. The enclosing frame around 四 and this character are not the same piece, even though they are written identically. Chinese has a separate enclosure component that does the surrounding, and 口 (kǒu) is a character in its own right. What transfers here is the hand movement, not the identity — and the hand movement is most of the work.

Guided Practice.
[once you have stopped driving — write: 口 (kǒu) — left wall, turning stroke, bottom last]
[once you have stopped driving — write: 四, then 口 (kǒu) — the same movement, two different pieces]

Wrap-up Recall.
How many strokes? (Three — the turning corner counts as one.)

Lesson 2 of 7.
口 (kǒu) — mouth, and a piece you will meet everywhere.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, because the lesson points at something written down. You can listen to everything else now — leave the section called Guided Practice until you have stopped, and I will say so again when we reach it.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Draw the box. Three strokes, bottom last.

Sounds you'll need.
口 kǒu means mouth.
Look at it again now that it means something. It is an open mouth, seen from the front — squared off, the way every Chinese character is squared off to fit its block, but a picture all the same. The oldest forms are rounder and even more obviously a mouth.
kǒu is third tone, the low dipping one you have had since nǐ. Keep it low.
[repeat that twice]

Why it's said this way — the piece inside other characters.
You have already met pieces that live inside other characters — 女 and 子 sit inside 好. 口 (kǒu) is the commonest of them all. When you see it as a small element in a corner of a bigger character, it very often says the word has something to do with the mouth: speaking, eating, calling, tasting.
The size matters. A full-height frame around a whole character is a different piece doing a different job; the mouth hint is for the small one tucked into a corner.
That is worth more than the word itself. You are not going to memorise five thousand characters as pictures; you are going to learn a few hundred pieces and read the rest as combinations. 口 (kǒu) is one of the commonest pieces there is.

[your turn — say: kǒu — low and dipping]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[once you have stopped driving — write: 口 (kǒu) — and say what it is a picture of]

Wrap-up Recall.

Lesson 3 of 7.
日 (rì) — one bar inside the box.

Before we start: this one needs your hands, so it is not a driving lesson. You will want a pen and something to write on and your eyes, for letter shapes on the page. You can listen to everything else now — leave the sections called Script — the shape and Guided Practice until you have stopped, and I will say so again when we reach it.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Draw the box. Now hold it in your head; you are about to fill it.

Script — the shape.
日 (rì).
The same box, with one horizontal bar across the middle. That is the entire difference, and it buys you a second character.
Four strokes, and the order has one thing worth getting right:
the left vertical.
the top and right side, one turning stroke.
the middle bar.
the bottom bar, closing it — still last.
The middle bar goes in before the bottom. Fill the box, then shut it. That is the same instinct as the box rule itself, applied one level in: you never close a space you still have to write inside.
Three pen lifts. If your box was comfortable, this cost you one line.

[once you have stopped driving — write: 日 (rì) — and check that the middle bar went in before the bottom]

Lesson 4 of 7.
日 (rì) — the sun, and the day it measures.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Write the box, then the box with a bar. Two characters, one shape.

Sounds you'll need.
日 rì means sun — and also day.
The picture is the sun: a disc with a mark in the middle. Squared off, like everything else, but that is what the bar is doing there. The oldest forms are a circle with a dot.
rì is fourth tone, falling. The r is not an English r and not a Spanish one either: start where your tongue sits for the s in English measure, then curl the tip back a little. Let the vowel be short and tight.
[repeat that twice]

Why it's said this way — one word, two jobs.
Sun and day in the same word is not a Chinese quirk. It is what happens in language after language, because the sun is how a day was counted before anybody had a clock. English does the same thing from the other end: we say the sun rose and three days later, and nobody finds it strange that a day is both the light and the span.
So this is one of the rare characters where you get two meanings and have to learn neither: they are the same idea, and the context tells you which.
Like 口 (kǒu), this one turns up inside other characters, and when it does it is almost always about the sun, light, or time.

Guided Practice.
[your turn — say: rì — falling, and the r of measure]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[once you have stopped driving — write: 日 (rì) — and say both of its meanings]

Lesson 5 of 7.
不 (bù) — four strokes, all of them separate.

Before we start: this one needs your hands, so it is not a driving lesson. You will want a pen and something to write on and your eyes, for letter shapes on the page. You can listen to everything else now — leave the sections called Script — the shape and Guided Practice until you have stopped, and I will say so again when we reach it.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Write 日 (rì). Middle bar before the bottom.

Script — the shape.
不 (bù).
Four strokes, in this order:
a top horizontal, left to right.
lift, then a long stroke from the upper centre down to the left.
lift, then the central vertical, straight down.
lift, then a short right-falling dot.
Three pen lifts, and every stroke stands alone. Nothing joins here. That is unusual enough to be worth saying plainly: the boxes you have just written were one continuous movement in places, and this is the opposite — four deliberate, separate marks under a single roof.
The commonest beginner's version runs the second and third strokes together into a corner. They are not a corner. Lift.

Guided Practice.
[once you have stopped driving — write: 不 (bù) — top bar, the long left-falling stroke, the vertical, the dot]
[once you have stopped driving — write: it again, counting the lifts aloud. There should be three.]

Wrap-up Recall.
Do the second and third strokes meet in a corner? (No — lift between them.)

Lesson 6 of 7.
不 (bù) — how Mandarin says no.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Write 不 (bù), counting three lifts. Then say hǎo — good.

Sounds you'll need.
不 bù means not.
bù is fourth tone, falling — the same firm drop you learned on èr and sì.
[repeat that twice]

Grammar Lens: there is no word for "no".
Here is something that surprises nearly every learner, and it is better met now than discovered later.
Mandarin has no single word meaning "no."
English has one. So does French, Spanish, Russian. Mandarin does not. What it has instead is 不 (bù), which goes in front of a word and turns it around:
好 hǎo — good 不好 bù hǎo — not good.
So when someone asks you a yes-or-no question in Mandarin, you do not answer with a word for "no". You answer by negating the thing they asked about. Ask whether something is 好, and "no" comes back as 不好 (bù hǎo).
This sounds like extra work and is actually less: you already know 好, so you already know how to refuse it, and the same holds for every word you have so far.
One word will break the pattern later — Mandarin has a second negator for having, and you will meet it with the word it belongs to. Everything in this book up to now takes 不 (bù).

[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: hǎo, then bù hǎo — good, then not good]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[once you have stopped driving — write: 不好 (bù hǎo)]

Lesson 7 of 7.
Practice — one box, two characters, and a refusal.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Write 口 (kǒu). Then write 日 (rì). One line apart.

The exchange.
Two voices, using only what you have:
A: 你好 ní hǎo B: 你好 ní hǎo A: 好 hǎo — good? B: 不好 bù hǎo — not good.
Small, and it is the first time in this book you have been able to disagree. Up to now every exchange has been agreement: a greeting answered by the same greeting, a number read back. This one has a no in it.

What you've built this chapter.
Three characters, and not one of them cost what it looks like it cost:
口 kǒu, mouth — you already knew the shape; it is the box from the number four, standing on its own.
日 rì, sun and day — the same box with one bar added.
不 bù, not — four separate strokes, and it turns every word you own into its opposite.
That last one is the largest thing in the chapter. 不 (bù) does not add one word to your vocabulary; it doubles what you already have. Everything you have learned to say so far, you have now learned to refuse.
And the two boxes are a down payment of a different kind. 口 (kǒu) and 日 (rì) both turn up inside other characters — mouth for speaking and eating, sun for light and time — so the next character that contains one will already be half read.
