# giocare and suonare — the two verbs English calls "play"

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[IT-LEX-INCONTRARE-02, IT-LEX-ASPETTARE-02, IT-LEX-COMPRARE-02] -->

[PAUSE 2s] English has one word for playing a game and playing an instrument.
Italian has two unrelated ones, and picking the wrong one is the most
recognisable mistake an English speaker makes.

## You'll want to know: two words, not one
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[IT-LEX-GIOCARE-02]; assesses=[IT-LEX-MI-PIACE-02, IT-GRAMMAR-MI-PIACE-03] -->

> **giocare** — to play a game, a sport, a match.
> **suonare** — to play an instrument; also, to ring or to sound.

The line is what you are doing, not the English word:

| what you are doing | Italian |
|---|---|
| playing a game | **giocare** |
| playing an instrument | **suonare** |
| a bell ringing | **suona** |

Chapter 19 gave you the frame — *mi piace* plus a dictionary form, as in *mi
piace leggere*:

> **Mi piace giocare.** — I like playing.
> **Mi piace suonare.** — I like playing music.

A grammatical tell too: *giocare* wants **a** before the game's name, while
*suonare* takes the instrument bare.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[IT-ETYMON-GIOCARE-03]; assesses=[IT-NOTICE-INCONTRARE-04, IT-ETYMON-INCONTRARE-03] -->

Two roots, never near each other.

**giocare** ← Latin **iocārī**, "to jest," from **iocus**, *a joke*. English took
*iocus* as **joke**, and through French as **jeopardy** — *jeu
parti*, "a divided game." Italian still calls a game **il gioco**.

**suonare** ← Latin **sonāre**, "to sound," from **sonus**, *a noise*. That is
English **sound** itself, plus **sonata** (from Italian: a thing *sounded*,
against a *cantata*, a thing sung), **resonate**, **unison**, **dissonant**. And
**counterpoint** — Italian *contrappunto*, note against note — carries the
*contrā* of the last lesson's *incontrare* into music.

The split is no rule to memorise: two unrelated Latin words, covered in English
by a single native verb.

## Grammar Lens: the h that keeps a c hard
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[IT-GRAMMAR-GIOCARE-04]; assesses=[IT-SOUND-CHIEDERE-02, IT-SOUND-MI-CHIAMO-02, IT-ETYMON-ETA-03, IT-GRAMMAR-ETA-04, IT-GRAMMAR-PARLARE-04, IT-GRAMMAR-ASPETTARE-04] -->

Both are ordinary *-are* verbs — until the *tu* form of the first one.

> **gioco, giochi, gioca** · **suono, suoni, suona**

*Gioco* is *JO-ko*: the **g** is soft before *i*, the **c** hard before *o*. Now
change the ending to *-i*: written *gioci*, that *c* would soften to *JO-chee*.
Italian will not let a consonant shift because an ending did, so it spells in an
**h**: **giochi**, *JO-kee*.

You have met that letter doing opposite work:

| where | what the h does |
|---|---|
| *chiedere*, *mi chiamo*, *giochi* | keeps a **c** hard before *e* and *i* |
| *hanno* against *anno* | separates two identical sounds in writing |

The second row is Chapter 14: **hanno venti anni**, where *hanno* ("they have")
and *anno* ("year," ← *annus*, the *annus* of *annual*) sound identical and only
the silent *h* tells them apart.
