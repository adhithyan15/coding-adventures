Lesson 1 of 3.
ஐ (ai) — the letter that opens ஐந்து.

Before we start: this one needs your hands, so it is not a driving lesson. You will want a pen and something to write on, your eyes, for letter shapes on the page and your eyes or your hands for the steps that ask you to read, point, write or gesture. You can listen to everything else now. I will say so again when we reach them, but leave these sections until you have stopped: Warm-up, Script you'll notice: ஐ and Guided Practice.

Warm-up.

[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: read ◌ீ and ◌ெ aloud, and write each once.
[pause 2 seconds]
Now say ஐந்து (aintu), "five". You have read it for a long time. This lesson takes one character out of it and puts it in your hand.

Script you'll notice: ஐ.
[once you have stopped driving — this part needs your eyes or your hands; if you are driving, skip ahead to the next part]
ஐ — ai.
It opens ஐந்து (aintu), "five". You wrote its sign ◌ை two chapters ago, on the end of இல்லை. This is the independent shape of the same vowel, for a word that begins with ai.

Writing: ஐ.
1. start inside the upper-left spiral, curl outward round it, and run down the central upright to its foot.
2. without lifting, draw the central upright back up.
3. without lifting, sweep around the upper-right loop and return left across the middle.
4. without lifting, circle the lower-left bowl back up to the centre.
5. without lifting, come back down the centre stem and circle through the lower-right bowl — and only now lift.
Pen lifts: 0. The pen never leaves the paper.
Stroke order is one attested teaching order, not a national standard — Tamil handwriting is taught with school-to-school variation. Source: Info-farmer, Writing Tamil 10.gif, ஐ (ai) stroke-order animation (Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 3.0, 2008), cross-checked with Sankaran Radhakrishnan, Tamil Script Learners Manual, Appendix I Frame 11 (University of Texas at Austin), p. 194.

Now say மூன்று (mūṉṟu), "three". You have read it for a long time. This lesson takes one character out of it and puts it in your hand.

Script you'll notice: ◌ூ.
[once you have stopped driving — this part needs your eyes or your hands; if you are driving, skip ahead to the next part]
◌ூ — ū.
It is the long ū on மூன்று (mūṉṟu), "three". Like its short partner ◌ு, which you already write, this sign changes shape with the consonant it joins, so copy it as it sits on மூ, the form you already read.

Writing: ◌ூ.
Put your pen on ◌ூ (ū) and follow its line. Copy the shape you can see — slowly, and larger than it is printed.
This book does not yet tell you where to start the character or which way to travel. That is a real thing, taught with real variation from school to school, and it is not written down here until it can be written down with a source. Copying what is in front of you needs no such source, and it is how the shape gets into your hand in the meantime.

Warm-up.
[once you have stopped driving — this part needs your eyes or your hands; if you are driving, skip ahead to the next part]
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: read ஐ (ai) and ◌ூ (ū) aloud, and write each once.
[pause 2 seconds]
Now say ஏழு (ēḻu), "seven". You have read it for a long time. This lesson takes one character out of it and puts it in your hand.

Script you'll notice: ஏ.
[once you have stopped driving — this part needs your eyes or your hands; if you are driving, skip ahead to the next part]
ஏ — ē.
It opens ஏழு (ēḻu), "seven". You already write its sign ◌ே from பேசு; ஏ is the independent shape of the same long vowel, for the front of a word.

Writing: ஏ.
1. start here: sweep from the inner curl around the broad left loop.
2. without lifting, climb the outer left side into the crown.
3. without lifting, carry the top bar to the right.
4. without lifting, retrace left and draw the right upright down.
5. without lifting, continue down-left along the diagonal foot.
6. without lifting, turn at the foot and finish back up-right — and only now lift.
Pen lifts: 0. The pen never leaves the paper.
Stroke order is one attested teaching order, not a national standard — Tamil handwriting is taught with school-to-school variation. Source: Sankaran Radhakrishnan, Tamil Script Learners Manual, Module 7, ஏ (ē), with Appendix I: Hand-movements, Frame 7 (University of Texas at Austin), p. 193.
