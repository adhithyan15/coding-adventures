# el plazo — by when

## You'll want to know: el plazo
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-C458-PLAZO-01]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C343-CLOCK-06] -->

**el plazo** — the period allowed, and so the deadline.

> **Plazo** de presentación: hasta el 30 de abril.

*Fuera de plazo* — past the deadline. *En el plazo de diez días* — within ten
days. *A corto plazo* — in the short term.

A second sense on any purchase: **pagar a plazos** — to pay in instalments,
each one a period agreed in advance.

## Grammar Lens: plazo is not fecha
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C458-PLAZO-01, ES-LEX-C343-CLOCK-06] -->

| what it names | the word |
|---|---|
| a day on the calendar | **la fecha** |
| the stretch of time you are allowed | **el plazo** |

*La fecha límite* is the last day; *el plazo* is the whole window that ends
there. So *el plazo termina el viernes* and *la fecha límite es el viernes*
say the same thing from opposite ends — one names the run, the other the wall
at the end of it.

Most official Spanish prefers *plazo*, because a form cares how long you have,
not which square on the calendar.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C458-PLAZO-01, ES-LEX-C321-COURT-06] -->

Latin **placitum** — "a thing agreed", the past participle of **placēre**, to
please. What pleased both parties was what they had settled on.

That makes *el plazo* a relative of **el placer**, which you already have.
Pleasure and a deadline sit oddly together until you see the middle step:
*placitum* meant an agreement, then the term of an agreement, then the time
you have got.

French took the same *placitum* and made **plaid**, a court hearing, and
English has **plea** and **plead** from it — all of them agreements argued
over. Spanish kept the calmest branch: a *plazo* is what was agreed, and the
paperwork holds you to it.
