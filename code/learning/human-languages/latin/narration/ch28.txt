You'll want to know: diēs — "day".
Diēs — "day" — is a genuinely common, everyday Classical Latin word, not a rare or bookish one. You've already met it embedded in:
diēs Lūnae ("Monday," literally "day of the Moon" — Chapter 5).
medius diēs ("noon," literally "mid-day" — Chapter 12).
Now it's the headword itself.

The word, taken apart: A rich family of English cousins.
Diēs has its own derivative, diurnum ("daytime, daily"), which gives English diurnal directly — and, through French's jour, journée, and jornel (all descendants of diurnum in their own right), journal and journey as well (a "journey" was originally "a day's travel"). Diary takes a separate path: it comes from a sibling derivative of diēs, diārium ("daily allowance"), not from diurnum itself — two related but distinct branches off the same root. Further out, a genuine Proto-Indo-European root, dyēws- ("to shine, sky"), connects diēs to Latin's own deus ("god") and to Iuppiter (Jupiter, literally "sky-father") — the same root that shines through the sky-god imagery of gods across the Indo-European world.
