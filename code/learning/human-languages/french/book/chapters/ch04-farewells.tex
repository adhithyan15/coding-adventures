\chapter{Farewells}
\label{ch:farewells}

You can open a French conversation; now close it. French has one plain goodbye
and a small family of soft ones, all built the same way --- the preposition
\emph{\`{a}} (``to'') followed by a time word:

\begin{center}\itshape
--- Bon\ldots \`{a} demain, Marc.\\
--- \`{A} demain, Chlo\'{e}. Au revoir!
\end{center}

\emph{Practice lessons: \texttt{lessons/FR-C04-*.md}.}

\section{\emph{au revoir} --- ``goodbye,'' i.e. ``until we see each other again''}
\label{lesson:au-revoir}

\begin{sounds}
\emph{oh ruh-VWAHR}, with two guttural French \emph{r}'s made at the back of the
throat. The letter pair \emph{oi} is pronounced \emph{wa}, so \emph{-voir} $=$
\emph{vwahr} (the same \emph{oi} as in \emph{soir}, \S\ref{lesson:soir}).
\end{sounds}

\begin{cousinweb}
\textbf{au revoir} is \emph{au} $+$ \emph{revoir}, ``at the re-seeing.''
\emph{au} is a fusion of \emph{\`{a}} (``to, at'') $+$ \emph{le} (``the'').
\emph{revoir} is \textbf{re-} (``again'') $+$ \textbf{voir} (``to see''), so the
whole phrase literally means \textbf{``until the seeing-again''} --- a small
promise that this is not the end.\\[3pt]
\emph{voir} comes from Latin \emph{vid\=ere}, ``to see,'' one of the richest
roots English owns: \textbf{vis}ion, \textbf{vis}ible, \textbf{vis}it,
\textbf{vis}a, \textbf{view}, \textbf{video} (``I see''); and with prefixes,
\textbf{re}vise (``look again''), e\textbf{vid}ent (``seen out''),
pro\textbf{vide}, super\textbf{vise}, sur\textbf{vey}.
\end{cousinweb}

\begin{culture}
\textbf{A goodbye that sees.} German builds its farewell out of exactly the same
idea: \emph{Auf Wiedersehen} is \emph{auf} (``on'') $+$ \emph{wieder}
(``again'') $+$ \emph{sehen} (``to see'') --- ``on the seeing-again.'' French
and German, side by side, both promise a re-seeing. Spanish \emph{adi\'{o}s}
takes a different road entirely: it commends you \emph{to God} (\emph{a Dios}).
Same moment of parting, two quite different pictures.
\end{culture}

\section{\emph{\`{a} plus tard} --- the casual ``see you later''}
\label{lesson:a-plus-tard}

\begin{sounds}
In the full phrase, \emph{plus} is \emph{ploo} with a silent \emph{s}, and
\emph{tard} is \emph{tar} with a silent \emph{d}: \emph{ah ploo TAR}. But in the
clipped form \emph{\`{a} plus}, the \emph{s} \emph{is} sounded: \emph{ah PLUSS}.
\end{sounds}

\begin{cousinweb}
\textbf{\`{a}} (``to, at'') $\leftarrow$ Latin \emph{ad} --- French's
all-purpose farewell preposition. \textbf{plus} (``more'') $\leftarrow$ Latin
\emph{pl\=us}, the very \textbf{plus} you already write in English, and the
\emph{plu-} of \textbf{plu}ral and sur\textbf{plus}. \textbf{tard} (``late'')
$\leftarrow$ Latin \emph{tarde}, ``slowly, late'' $\to$ English \textbf{tardy}
and \textbf{retard} (``to hold back, make late'').\\[3pt]
So \emph{\`{a} plus tard} is literally ``to more late'' --- see you at some
later point. Among friends it shrinks to just \emph{\`{a} plus}.
\end{cousinweb}

\begin{culture}
Spanish inherited that identical Latin \emph{tarde} --- but the two languages
grew it in different directions. French kept it meaning \textbf{late}
(\emph{tard}); Spanish grew it into \textbf{afternoon} (\emph{tarde}). One Latin
word, two everyday meanings. And where French leans on \emph{\`{a}} for its soft
goodbyes, Spanish leans on \emph{hasta} (``until''): \emph{hasta luego} is the
twin of \emph{\`{a} plus tard}.
\end{culture}

\section{\emph{\`{a} bient\^{o}t} --- ``see you soon''}
\label{lesson:a-bientot}

\begin{sounds}
\emph{bien} is nasal --- \emph{byan}, air through the nose, no hard \emph{n}.
\emph{bient\^{o}t} $=$ \emph{byan-TOH}; the circumflex on \emph{\^{o}} simply
marks a long \emph{o}, and the final \emph{t} is silent. The whole phrase:
\emph{ah byan-TOH}.
\end{sounds}

\begin{cousinweb}
\textbf{\`{a} bient\^{o}t} $=$ ``to soon'' $\to$ ``see you soon.'' The second
word is a compound you can pull apart: \textbf{bien} (``well,''
\S\ref{lesson:bien}, from Latin \emph{bene} --- the \emph{bene} of
\textbf{bene}fit) $+$ \textbf{t\^{o}t} (``early, soon'') $=$ ``well-soon,'' that
is, \emph{quite soon}.\\[3pt]
The circumflex on \emph{\^{o}} is itself a history lesson: in French a
circumflex usually marks an \textbf{s that dropped out}. \emph{h\^{o}tel} was
once \emph{hostel}; \emph{for\^{e}t} was once \emph{forest}; and \emph{t\^{o}t}
came from an older \emph{tost}. The little hat is the ghost of a lost \emph{s}.
\end{cousinweb}

\begin{grammarlens}
\textbf{\emph{bien}, the intensifier.} You met \emph{bien} as ``well,'' an
answer to \emph{\c{C}a va?} Here it does a second job: it glues onto another
word to strengthen it. \emph{bien} $+$ \emph{t\^{o}t} $=$ \emph{bient\^{o}t},
``soon indeed.'' French loves this move --- \emph{bienvenue} (``welcome'') is
literally \emph{bien} $+$ \emph{venue}, ``well-come.'' Same word, new trick.
\end{grammarlens}

\section{\emph{\`{a} demain} --- ``see you tomorrow''}
\label{lesson:a-demain}

\begin{sounds}
\emph{demain} $=$ \emph{duh-MAN}, where the ending \emph{-ain} is a nasal vowel
(the same nasal as in \emph{pain}, \emph{main}) and the final consonant is
silent. The whole phrase: \emph{ah duh-MAN}.
\end{sounds}

\begin{cousinweb}
\textbf{\`{a} demain} $=$ ``to tomorrow'' $\to$ ``see you tomorrow.''
\textbf{demain} (``tomorrow'') $\leftarrow$ Latin \emph{d\=e m\=ane}, ``from the
morning'': \emph{d\=e} $=$ ``from,'' \emph{m\=ane} $=$ ``in the morning.'' The
next morning is \emph{tomorrow}, and the two words fused into
\emph{demain}.\\[3pt]
English keeps that \emph{m\=ane} too, in the poetic \textbf{matins} (morning
prayers) and in \textbf{matin\'{e}e}, a daytime show.
\end{cousinweb}

\begin{culture}
\textbf{The hidden twin.} Spanish \emph{ma\~{n}ana} (``tomorrow, morning'')
comes from Latin \emph{(h\=ora) maneana}, built on the \emph{same} \emph{m\=ane}
(``morning''). So French \emph{demain} and Spanish \emph{ma\~{n}ana} are cousins
through one Latin word --- both turning ``morning'' into ``tomorrow,'' just
assembled differently: French says \emph{from-morning}, Spanish says
\emph{morning-ish hour}.
\end{culture}

\begin{grammarlens}
\textbf{The \emph{\`{a} \ldots} farewells, complete.} All of French's soft
goodbyes are \emph{\`{a}} $+$ a time word, where Spanish uses \emph{hasta}
(``until'') $+$ a time word:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
French & Spanish & When \\
\midrule
\emph{\`{a} plus tard} (or \emph{\`{a} plus}) & \emph{hasta luego} & Later --- the default \\
\emph{\`{a} bient\^{o}t} & \emph{hasta pronto} & Soon \\
\emph{\`{a} demain} & \emph{hasta ma\~{n}ana} & Tomorrow \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent And \emph{au revoir} for the plain goodbye.
\end{grammarlens}

\section{Saying goodbye, start to finish}
\label{lesson:ch4-practice}

No new words. The farewells now close a real conversation and fold back into
everything since \emph{salut}.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}
\toprule
You are\ldots & Say \\
\midrule
Parting plainly or politely & \emph{Au revoir.} \\
Expecting to meet again shortly & \emph{\`{A} bient\^{o}t.} \\
Casually saying ``see you'' & \emph{\`{A} plus (tard).} \\
Leaving for the day & \emph{\`{A} demain.} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent The whole exchange, drawing on Chapters \ref{ch:greetings} through
\ref{ch:farewells}:

\begin{center}\itshape
--- Salut! Je m'appelle Chlo\'{e}. Comment tu t'appelles?\\
--- Je m'appelle Marc. Enchant\'{e}.\\
--- \c{C}a va?\\
--- \c{C}a va bien, merci, et toi?\\
--- Tr\`{e}s bien. Bon\ldots \`{a} demain, Marc.\\
--- \`{A} demain, Chlo\'{e}. Au revoir!
\end{center}

What this chapter built: \emph{au revoir}, the plain goodbye that promises a
\emph{re-seeing} (the \emph{vid\=ere} family, twin of German \emph{Auf
Wiedersehen}); and \emph{\`{a} plus tard} / \emph{\`{a} bient\^{o}t} /
\emph{\`{a} demain} --- later, soon, tomorrow, all \emph{\`{a}} $+$ a time,
mirroring Spanish's \emph{hasta}. You can now hold a whole short French
exchange, hello to goodbye. Next chapter: your first verbs, and your first
sentence built from scratch.
