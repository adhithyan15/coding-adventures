# dormivo / ho dormito — the same sleep, told two ways

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[IT-LEX-FACEVA-01, IT-LEX-C-ERA-02] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Paint a cold morning in two short sentences. (*Faceva freddo. Non
c'era il sole.*)

[PAUSE 1s] Nothing has happened in it yet. This lesson is about what happens.

## Grammar Lens: the scene and the events
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[IT-GRAMMAR-SCENA-EVENTO-01]; assesses=[IT-GRAMMAR-PASSATO-PROSSIMO-02, IT-GRAMMAR-IMPERFETTO-01] -->

Take one fact, that you slept, and let the tense decide what kind of fact it is:

> **Dormivo.** — I was sleeping. *(no edge: what was going on)*
> **Ho dormito.** — I slept. *(an edge: a thing that happened and ended)*

Neither is more correct. They answer different questions, and a told story
needs both, in a division of labour:

> The **imperfetto** paints the **scene**: what was going on, what things were
> like.
> The **passato prossimo** drops the **events** into it: what happened.

*Faceva freddo. Dormivo. Mia madre ha telefonato.* — It was cold. I was asleep.
My mother phoned.

**Mentre**, "while", puts both into one sentence. *Mentre dormivo, mia madre ha
telefonato.* The *mentre* half is the scene, still running; the other half is
the event that cut into it.

English has no separate ending for this. It leans on *was ‑ing* and *used to*
and lets the context carry the rest. Italian settles it in the ending.

## The word, taken apart: why "imperfect"
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[IT-ETYMON-IMPERFECTUS-02]; assesses=[IT-LEX-FACEVA-01] -->

**imperfetto** is a learned word from Latin *imperfectus*, "unfinished"
(EtymDB 2.1): *in-*, "not", plus *perfectus*, "carried all the way through",
which is itself *per-*, "through", plus *facere*, "to do, to make" (Lewis &
Short). That *facere* is the one inside last lesson's *faceva*.

So the name is a description of the job. The imperfetto is the
**not-carried-through** past: unfinished in time, with no edge on it. English
**perfect** still means finished all the way through.
