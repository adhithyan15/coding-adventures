# las comillas — three shapes, and the preferred one points outward

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-ORTH-C424-04] -->

[PAUSE 2s] The last lesson said a Spanish dialogue carries no quotation marks.
So Spanish does have them — they are for something else. This lesson says what.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-ORTH-C424-05]; assesses=[] -->

**las comillas** — "quotation marks." Plural, like *los dos puntos*, because
they come as a pair.

Spanish uses three shapes and puts them in an order of preference:

| shape | name | when |
|---|---|---|
| « » | comillas latinas, or angulares | preferred in printed Spanish |
| " " | comillas inglesas | inside a quotation already in « » |
| ' ' | comillas simples | inside that again |

The outermost pair is the angular one. Nesting works from the outside in, so the
English-looking pair appears **inside** the Spanish-looking one, rather than
first.

## Grammar Lens: what they are actually for
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-ORTH-C424-05, ES-ORTH-C424-04] -->

Since **la raya** already handles dialogue, the marks are free for other work,
and the one an A1 paper shows is the **foreign word**:

> El camarero trajo un «sandwich».

A word borrowed and not yet naturalised is set apart from the sentence around
it. Spanish also uses them for a title inside a text, and for a word being
talked about rather than used.

The name is a diminutive of **coma**: a *comilla* is a small comma, which is
what the mark once looked like.
