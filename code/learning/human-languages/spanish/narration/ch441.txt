Spanish, chapter 441: El Contrato.
7 lessons. All 7 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 7.
el contrato — drawn together.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say el trabajo and el documento. One document turns a job into an obligation on both sides. Name it.

You'll want to know: el contrato.
el contrato — the contract.
Un contrato de trabajo is an employment contract; un contrato de alquiler is a tenancy agreement. The verb is contratar, to hire or to contract: me han contratado — they have hired me.

The word, taken apart.
con- together, plus -trat- from Latin trahere, to pull or draw.
A contract is a drawing-together — two parties pulled into the same agreement. English says the same thing and has stopped hearing it: contract, and also contraction, which is a muscle drawing itself in.
Trahere is busy in Spanish too. traer is to bring, to draw something to you; atraer is to attract; contraer is to contract a debt or an illness, the same word as the noun above with its verb ending still on.

Lesson 2 of 7.
firmar — to make it firm.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say el nombre, escribir and el contrato. Writing your name at the bottom of one of those is not the same act as writing it at the top.

You'll want to know: firmar.
firmar — to sign. la firma — the signature, and also a firm in the business sense.
Firme aquí, por favor — sign here, please. Sin firmar — unsigned, the state a form is in when the clerk hands it back to you.

The word, taken apart.
Latin firmus, firm, solid, not moving. To firmar is to make firm.
The name at the bottom is not information; it is the act that hardens a piece of paper from a proposal into an obligation. Spanish says that outright, where English borrowed sign from signum, a mark, and kept only the mark.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
the word: firmar. what it is built on: firmus, firm.
the word: to sign. what it is built on: signum, a mark.
English has firmus too, in firm, affirm and confirm — all of them about making something hold.

Why it's said this way.
Look at what the two words in this chapter are doing so far.
Contrato draws the parties together. Firmar makes the result solid. They are two halves of one physical picture: pull the sides in, then set them.
That picture is why la firma can also mean a company. A firm is a name that has been made firm — an entity that holds, that you can sign with and be bound by. Spanish did not need a second word for it, and the double meaning is not a coincidence you have to memorise.

Lesson 3 of 7.
fijo — nailed down.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say la empresa and firmar. A company offers you two kinds of contract, and only one of them ends on a date.

You'll want to know: fijo.
fijo — fixed, permanent, settled.
Un contrato fijo is a permanent contract, the one a Spanish job advert contrasts with temporal. Un precio fijo is a fixed price. Un cliente fijo is a regular. The verb is fijar, to fix or to set: fijar una fecha — to set a date.
It agrees: fijo, fija, fijos, fijas.

The word, taken apart.
Latin fīgere, to drive in, to fasten — the verb for putting a stake in the ground. Fijo is its past participle, worn down: the thing that has been fastened.
English took the same participle intact as fix, and kept the original sense in transfix, to pin something through.
So the chapter now has three ways of binding, and they are all physical:
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
word: contrato. the picture: pulled together.
word: firmar. the picture: made solid.
word: fijo. the picture: driven in.

Why it's said this way.
cerrado is a participle you can still feel as one: cerrar is right there, and nobody would call cerrado a plain adjective.
fijo is a participle too, and nothing about it feels like one. The verb fijar exists, but it was built backwards from the adjective rather than the other way round, and the form you would expect — fijado — also exists and means something narrower.
That is what age does. A participle used constantly enough stops being read as the result of an action and becomes a quality in its own right. Cerrado is still at the first stage; fijo finished the journey centuries ago.
When you meet a short adjective ending in a bare vowel where a verb also exists — fijo, limpio, seco — it is worth asking which came first. The answer is often not the one the modern shape suggests.

Lesson 4 of 7.
la factura — a making, itemised.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say pagar, la cuenta and el precio. In a restaurant you ask for la cuenta. For work done on a contrato, the paper has a different name.

You'll want to know: la factura.
la factura — the invoice, the bill.
La factura de la luz — the electricity bill. ¿Me hace una factura? — could you make me out an invoice? The verb is facturar, to invoice, and also to check in luggage at an airport.
la cuenta is what you ask for at a table; la factura is the formal document, with a number on it, that a business issues and a tax office may want to see.

The word, taken apart.
Latin facere, to make or do, plus -tūra, an ending that names the thing an action produces.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
verb: facere, to make. the thing it produces: factura, a making.
verb: escribir, to write. the thing it produces: escritura, a writing.
verb: leer, to read. the thing it produces: lectura, a reading.
A factura is literally a making — the itemised account of what was made or done for you. The ending is still productive, so when you meet an unfamiliar noun in -ura, look for the verb inside it before you look it up.

Why it's said this way.
Facere is one of the two or three hardest-working roots in the language, and you can see it under a lot of words that do not look related.
hacer is its direct Spanish descendant — facere lost its f to an h the way filius became hijo. satisfacer is satis plus facere, to make enough. fácil is what can be done.
So la factura and hacer are the same word arriving by different routes: one worn smooth by a thousand years of speech, one lifted off the page by clerks who needed a formal noun. That is the same doublet shape as nadar and la natación — and, as there, the learned twin is the one on the official paper.
A precio fijo and a factura belong to the same world: both are things written down so they stop moving.

Lesson 5 of 7.
el préstamo — what lending leaves behind.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say prestar, el banco and el dinero. You have the verb for handing something over. Name the thing that exists afterwards.

You'll want to know: el préstamo.
el préstamo — the loan.
Pedir un préstamo — to apply for a loan. Devolver el préstamo — to pay it back, with the same devolver the lending cycle already gave you.
The stress is three syllables from the end — PRÉS-ta-mo — and the written accent is what tells you so. Without it the word would be read pres-TA-mo.

The word, taken apart.
It is prestar with a noun ending, and both go back to Latin praestāre — prae- in front, plus stāre, to stand. To praestāre was to stand out in front of someone: to furnish, to supply, to put something forward.
So a préstamo is what has been put forward, and English keeps the same idea in to advance a sum.
This completes a set you already had. Prestar hands it over, recoger fetches it, devolver brings it back — and now el préstamo is the name of the thing while it is out of your hands.

Why it's said this way.
Préstamo has a second life worth knowing, because you will meet it in this kind of explanation rather than at a bank.
Un préstamo lingüístico is a loanword — a word one language has taken from another. Spanish calls garaje and sofá préstamos, and it uses the borrowing metaphor exactly as English does, down to the fact that nobody gives them back.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
the word on the page: un préstamo del banco. what it is a loan of: dinero.
the word on the page: un préstamo del francés. what it is a loan of: una palabra.
That is the sense in which factura is not a préstamo and garaje is: factura was taken from the Latin that Spanish itself grew out of, which makes it an inheritance claimed late rather than a borrowing from a neighbour.

Lesson 6 of 7.
(contrato, firmar, fijo, factura, préstamo) — from cold.

Warm-up.
[pause 3 seconds]
Close the lessons before this one. Say all five: the contract, to sign, permanent, the invoice, the loan.

Grammar Lens: paperwork is a set of physical acts.
Nothing in this chapter began as an abstraction. Every one of the three binding words is a thing done with the hands.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
word: contrato. the act underneath: pull the two sides together.
word: firmar. the act underneath: make the result solid.
word: fijo. the act underneath: drive it in so it cannot move.
Read a Spanish contract with those three in mind and the vocabulary stops looking like jargon. Contratar, firmar, fijar — draw, harden, fasten. The law borrowed its whole vocabulary from carpentry and rope.

What you've built.
You can now read the four pieces of paper that decide where your money goes — what you agreed to, that you agreed to it, what you owe, and what you owe it on. That is the administrative half of living somewhere, and it is what an A2 reading paper is mostly made of.
You also have two endings that keep paying. -ura names the thing an action produces, so factura, escritura and lectura stop being three separate words to learn. And fijo showed that a participle used long enough stops feeling like one at all.

Why it's said this way.
Notice the last line of the second notice: debe comunicarse firmado.
There is no you in it. Comunicarse drains out the person exactly as celebrarse did on the notice about the meeting, and firmado is the participle doing an adjective's work, the way cerrado does on a shop door. The sentence says any change must be communicated in-a-signed-state, and names nobody at all.
That impersonal shape is the register of official Spanish, and it is worth recognising rather than translating. When a notice stops naming who must act, it is not being vague — it is saying the obligation attaches to the document, not to a particular clerk.
Contrast the job advert: se busca, se valora, enviar. Same move. An advert and a bank notice are written by the same hand.
