\section{Greetings \& Introductions}

\begin{vocabtable}
hola & --- & hello \\
buenos d\'{i}as & --- & good morning \\
buenas tardes & --- & good afternoon \\
buenas noches & --- & good evening / good night \\
\textquestiondown c\'{o}mo est\'{a}s? & --- & how are you? \\
me llamo\ldots & --- & my name is\ldots \\
\end{vocabtable}

\begin{etymology}
\textbf{buenos d\'{i}as}: \emph{bueno} $\leftarrow$ Latin \emph{bonus}
(``good,'' cognate with English \emph{bonus}, \emph{bounty}). \emph{d\'{i}a}
$\leftarrow$ Latin \emph{dies} (``day,'' cognate with English
\emph{diurnal}, and via French, \emph{journal}/\emph{journey}).
\textbf{buenas tardes}: \emph{tarde} $\leftarrow$ Latin \emph{tardus}
(``slow, late'' --- cognate with English \emph{tardy}).
\textbf{buenas noches}: \emph{noche} $\leftarrow$ Latin \emph{nox, noctis}
(cognate with English \emph{nocturnal}, \emph{equinox}).
\textbf{me llamo}: \emph{llamar} $\leftarrow$ Latin \emph{clamare} (``to
shout, call out'' --- cognate with English \emph{claim}, \emph{exclaim},
\emph{clamor}). See the Root Spotlight later in this chapter for the full
family. \emph{hola} itself has a contested etymology, possibly an Old
Spanish nautical hailing call --- treat any link to English
``hello/holler'' as coincidence, not shared history.
\end{etymology}

\begin{grammarlens}
\emph{me llamo} is built from a \textbf{reflexive verb} --- one where the
action bounces back onto the person doing it. Literally, ``I call myself,''
not just ``I am named.'' English \emph{can} do this (``I call myself
Sam''), but usually doesn't bother, saying simply ``I'm Sam.'' Spanish
grammatically requires the bounce-back here. Reflexive verbs get their full
treatment in Chapter~12, on daily routines.
\end{grammarlens}

\section{Subject Pronouns}

\begin{vocabtable}
yo & --- & I \\
t\'{u} & --- & you (informal) \\
\'{e}l / ella & --- & he / she \\
usted & --- & you (formal) \\
nosotros & --- & we \\
ellos / ellas & --- & they \\
\end{vocabtable}

\begin{etymology}
\emph{yo} $\leftarrow$ Latin \emph{ego} (not a cognate of English ``I,''
which is Germanic --- but \emph{ego} itself was later borrowed straight
into English). \emph{t\'{u}} $\leftarrow$ Latin \emph{tu}, cognate with
archaic English \emph{thou}. \emph{\'{e}l/ella} $\leftarrow$ Latin
demonstrative \emph{ille/illa} (``that one''), the same source behind
French \emph{il/elle} and Italian \emph{egli/ella}. \emph{usted} is a
contraction of \emph{vuestra merced} (``your grace/mercy'') --- \emph{merced}
$\leftarrow$ Latin \emph{merces} (cognate with English \emph{mercy},
\emph{mercenary}, \emph{commerce}): a title of respect worn down into a
pronoun. \emph{nosotros} $\leftarrow$ \emph{nos} + \emph{alteros}, ``we
others,'' a Romance-era compound Latin itself never had.
\end{etymology}

\begin{grammarlens}
A \textbf{pronoun} stands in for a noun so you don't have to repeat it. A
\textbf{subject pronoun} marks who is doing the action. English has the
same set of words doing the same job. The real difference appears once
verbs get conjugated (next section): Spanish verb endings already encode
who's acting, so subject pronouns are frequently \textbf{dropped} ---
\emph{``Soy Ana''} needs no \emph{yo}, because \emph{soy} can only mean ``I
am.'' English can't drop it (``Am Ana'' isn't a sentence) because English
verb endings carry almost no person information, so English leans on the
pronoun instead. Languages that can drop subject pronouns are called
\textbf{pro-drop} languages; Spanish is one, English isn't.
\end{grammarlens}

\section{\emph{Ser}: Identity \& Origin}

\begin{vocabtable}
soy & --- & I am \\
eres & --- & you (informal) are \\
es & --- & he/she/it is, you (formal) are \\
somos & --- & we are \\
son & --- & they are, you all are \\
\end{vocabtable}

\begin{etymology}
The infinitive \emph{ser} descends from Latin \emph{sed\={e}re} (``to
sit,'' cognate with English \emph{session}, \emph{sedentary}, \emph{sedate}).
Its conjugations --- \emph{soy, eres, es, somos, son} --- descend instead
from a \emph{different} Latin verb, \emph{esse} (``to be,'' cognate with
English \emph{is}, \emph{essence}, \emph{entity}). Spanish \emph{ser} fuses
two separate Latin verbs into one.
\end{etymology}

\begin{grammarlens}
\emph{Ser} is a \textbf{linking verb} (or ``copula'') --- it connects a
subject to a description rather than describing an action. English has
exactly one linking verb for this job: ``to be.'' Spanish also uses ``to
be'' --- except Spanish splits it into \emph{two} verbs, \emph{ser} and
\emph{estar}, where English has one. \S\ref{sec:estar} covers the second
half; \S\ref{sec:ser-vs-estar} lays out how to choose between them.
\end{grammarlens}

\begin{morphologybox}
\textbf{Root: Latin \emph{clamare}} --- ``to shout, call out.'' Related to
\emph{clamor}, borrowed into English unchanged.

\textit{Spanish derivatives:} \emph{llamar} (to call), \emph{llamada} (a
phone call), \emph{exclamar} (to exclaim, \emph{ex-} ``out'' + \emph{clamare}).

\textit{English cognates:} \emph{claim}, \emph{exclaim}, \emph{acclaim}
(\emph{ad-} ``toward'' + \emph{clamare}), \emph{clamor}, \emph{proclamation}
(\emph{pro-} ``forth'' + \emph{clamare}).

Spanish and English didn't borrow these from each other --- both borrowed
independently from the same Latin source, centuries apart, through
different paths. Same root, different cousins.
\end{morphologybox}

\section{Numbers 0--10}

\begin{vocabtable}
cero & --- & 0 \\
uno, dos, tres, cuatro, cinco & --- & 1--5 \\
seis, siete, ocho, nueve, diez & --- & 6--10 \\
\end{vocabtable}

\begin{etymology}
\emph{uno--cinco} $\leftarrow$ Latin \emph{unus, duos, tres, quattuor,
quinque} $\to$ English \emph{unit, dual, trio, quarter, quintet}.
\emph{seis--diez} $\leftarrow$ Latin \emph{sex, septem, octo, novem, decem}
--- the same roots buried in \emph{September} (originally the 7th month),
\emph{October} (8th), \emph{November} (9th), \emph{December} (10th), plus
\emph{sextet, octagon, decade}. \emph{cero} $\leftarrow$ Italian
\emph{zero} $\leftarrow$ Arabic \emph{\d{s}ifr} (``empty'') --- the same
root that gives English \emph{cipher}, and the first thread in this book
toward Spanish's roughly 4{,}000 Arabic-derived words (Chapter~35 goes
deep on this).
\end{etymology}

\section{\emph{Estar}: State \& Location}
\label{sec:estar}

\begin{vocabtable}
estoy & --- & I am (state/location) \\
est\'{a}s & --- & you are \\
est\'{a} & --- & he/she/it is \\
estamos & --- & we are \\
est\'{a}n & --- & they are \\
\end{vocabtable}

\begin{etymology}
\emph{estar} $\leftarrow$ Latin \emph{stare} (``to stand'') --- an enormous
English cognate family: \emph{stand, state, status, static, stable,
establish, instant, constant, distance, circumstance}, all from the same
root.
\end{etymology}

\begin{grammarlens}
Linguists call this split \textbf{stative vs.\ dynamic}: \emph{ser} answers
``what is this, fundamentally?''; \emph{estar} answers ``what condition is
this in, right now?'' English's single ``to be'' doesn't distinguish these
at all --- ``he is tall'' and ``he is tired'' use the identical verb, even
though one is a lasting trait and the other a passing state. Spanish forces
a choice every time; there is no neutral option.
\end{grammarlens}

\section{\emph{Ser} vs.\ \emph{Estar}}
\label{sec:ser-vs-estar}

Three minimal pairs, same sentence shape, different verb, different
meaning:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Soy feliz} (I am a happy person, a trait) vs.\ \emph{Estoy
    feliz} (I am happy right now, a mood).
  \item \emph{Es alto} (he is tall, an inherent trait) vs.\ \emph{Est\'{a}
    aqu\'{i}} (he is here, a location).
  \item \emph{Somos amigos} (we are friends, lasting) vs.\ \emph{Estamos
    bien} (we are doing well, current).
\end{itemize}

\begin{grammarlens}
When unsure which to use, ask: am I describing what this fundamentally
\emph{is} (identity, origin, lasting trait --- \emph{ser}), or what
condition it's \emph{in right now} (location, mood, a changeable state ---
\emph{estar})? That question has no equivalent in English, because English
never needed one --- it looks like a vocabulary problem but is really a
grammar problem, and no amount of memorizing more words fixes it.
\end{grammarlens}

\section{Days of the Week}

\begin{vocabtable}
lunes & masc. & Monday \\
martes & masc. & Tuesday \\
mi\'{e}rcoles & masc. & Wednesday \\
jueves & masc. & Thursday \\
viernes & masc. & Friday \\
s\'{a}bado & masc. & Saturday \\
domingo & masc. & Sunday \\
\end{vocabtable}

\begin{etymology}
Almost all Roman god names: \emph{lunes} $\leftarrow$ \emph{dies Lunae}
(Moon, $\to$ \emph{lunar, lunatic}); \emph{martes} $\leftarrow$ \emph{dies
Martis} (Mars, $\to$ \emph{martial}, \emph{March}); \emph{mi\'{e}rcoles}
$\leftarrow$ \emph{dies Mercurii} (Mercury, $\to$ \emph{mercurial,
commerce}); \emph{jueves} $\leftarrow$ \emph{dies Iovis} (Jupiter/Jove,
$\to$ \emph{jovial}); \emph{viernes} $\leftarrow$ \emph{dies Veneris}
(Venus, $\to$ \emph{venerate, venereal}). Two break the pattern:
\emph{s\'{a}bado} $\leftarrow$ Hebrew \emph{Shabbat} via Latin
\emph{sabbatum} ($\to$ English \emph{sabbath}); \emph{domingo} $\leftarrow$
Latin \emph{dies Dominica} (``the Lord's day,'' $\to$ \emph{dominion,
dominant}, and the Spanish honorific \emph{don}) --- both a later
Judeo-Christian reframing of the Roman calendar.
\end{etymology}

\begin{grammarlens}
Every one of these seven words is masculine --- \emph{el lunes}, not
\emph{la lunes}. This is the first real brush with \textbf{grammatical
gender}: Spanish sorts every noun into masculine or feminine, a pure
grammatical classification with nothing to do with biology, that then
forces the article and any describing adjective to match. English dropped
this almost entirely; the only survivors are pronouns (\emph{he/she/it}),
which track real-world sex, not an arbitrary category. From here on, every
noun in this book carries a gender tag, as in the table above. Chapter~3
gives the full pattern and its exceptions.
\end{grammarlens}

\section{Chapter Capstone: Introduce Yourself}

Two connectors close the loop: \textbf{de} (``of/from,'' $\leftarrow$
Latin \emph{de}) and \textbf{hoy} (``today,'' $\leftarrow$ Latin
\emph{hodie}, a fusion of \emph{hoc} + \emph{die}, ``this day'' --- the same
\emph{dies} root as \emph{buenos d\'{i}as}). Every piece of the following
script is drawn from this chapter:

\begin{quote}
\itshape
Hola, buenos d\'{i}as. Me llamo [your name]. Yo soy de [your city/country].
Hoy es [day of the week]. \textquestiondown C\'{o}mo est\'{a}s?
\end{quote}

Five chapter concepts, one introduction. Chapter~2 continues with numbers
11--100, telling time, and the core question words.
