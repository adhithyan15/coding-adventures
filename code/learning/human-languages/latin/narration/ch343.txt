Latin, chapter 343: Triumphus.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
captīvus, captīvī — a prisoner, a captive.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to lay waste, then the Latin for a defeat.

You'll want to know: captīvus, captīvī.
captīvus, captīvī — "a prisoner, a captive".
Captīvī post currum imperātōris ambulābant. The prisoners were walking behind the general's chariot.
Masculine, from capiō (taught). Also an adjective, captīvus, -a, -um. English captive.

The word, taken apart.
captīvus is built on captus, the participle of capiō, "to take" (Lewis & Short). English captive comes from it, and caitiff through French.

Lesson 2 of 5.
dēdō, dēdere — to give up, to surrender.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a defeat, then the Latin for a prisoner.

You'll want to know: dēdō, dēdere.
dēdō, dēdere — "to give up, to surrender".
Cīvēs sē et urbem imperātōrī dēdidērunt. The citizens surrendered themselves and the city to the general.
dēdō, dēdere, dēdidī, dēditum: dē + dō. Sē dēdere: to surrender.

The word, taken apart.
dēdō is built on dō, "to give", with dē, "away" (EtymDB): to give away from oneself.

dēditiō, dēditiōnis — "a surrender".
Post longam obsidiōnem dēditiō facta est. After a long siege a surrender took place.
Feminine, from dēdō. In a dēditiō, the defeated put everything into the winner's hands.

The word, taken apart.

Lesson 4 of 5.
foedus, foederis — a treaty, an alliance.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to give up, then the Latin for a surrender.

You'll want to know: foedus, foederis.
foedus, foederis — "a treaty, an alliance".
Duae cīvitātēs foedus fēcērunt. The two states made a treaty.
Neuter, 3rd declension. Not the adjective foedus, -a, -um, 'foul'. English federal.

The word, taken apart.
foedus is inherited from the Proto-Indo-European root bʰeydʰ- (EtymDB), which also gives fidēs, "trust" (taught); Lewis & Short link it with fīdō, "to trust". English federal comes from it.

Lesson 5 of 5.
triumphus, triumphī — a triumph, a victory procession.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a surrender, then the Latin for a treaty.

You'll want to know: triumphus, triumphī.
triumphus, triumphī — "a triumph, a victory procession".
Imperātor in triumphō per urbem vectus est. The general rode through the city in a triumph.
Masculine. The winner rode in a chariot through the city, and his soldiers sang rude songs about him.

The word, taken apart.
triumphus was taken, EtymDB says, from Greek thriambos, "a hymn to Dionysus", probably through Etruscan; old Latin wrote triumpus. English triumph comes from it.
