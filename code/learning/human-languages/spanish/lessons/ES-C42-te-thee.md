# te — the pair English used to have

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-TU, ES-LEX-TE-OBJECT] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Two shapes for one person: *tú* when they act, *te* when it lands on
them.

[PAUSE 1s] English once had exactly the same two shapes, and they are the same
two words.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-ETYMON-TE-TU]; assesses=[ES-GRAMMAR-TE-DIRECT-OBJECT] -->

*Te* is Latin **tē** — the object form of **tū**, which you already have.

[PAUSE 2s] Old English had **thou** for the doer and **thee** for the one it
happened to. Here is the striking part: *tú*/*te* and *thou*/*thee* are not
merely similar. They are **one ancient pair, inherited twice** — once down the
Latin line into Spanish, once down the Germanic line into English.

[PAUSE 2s] English later dropped both and let *you* cover the two jobs. Spanish
kept them apart, which is why you have two words to learn where a speaker of
English feels there should be one.
