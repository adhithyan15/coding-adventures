Arabic, chapter 504: Inside the body.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
كبد (kabid) — the liver.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Arabic for organized, then the Arabic for careless.

You'll want to know: كبد.
كبد — kabid — "the liver".
أكل جدي كبد الخروف. akala jaddī kabid al-kharūf. My grandfather ate the sheep's liver.
Plural: أكباد, akbād.

The word, taken apart.
كبد (kabid) is built on the native Arabic root k-b-d, "the liver" (Lane). The word is old; its plural is akbād.

طحال (ṭiḥāl) is built on the native Arabic root ṭ-ḥ-l, "to hurt the spleen" (Lane). Lane gives ṭaḥala as to strike or hurt the ṭiḥāl.

Lesson 3 of 5.
معدة (miʿda) — the stomach.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Arabic for the liver, then the Arabic for the spleen.

You'll want to know: معدة.
معدة — miʿda — "the stomach".
عندي ألم في المعدة. ʿindī alam fī al-miʿda. I have a pain in my stomach.
Baṭn, belly, is the outside; miʿda is the organ.

The word, taken apart.
معدة (miʿda) is an old native Arabic word for the stomach, usually traced to the root m-ʿ-d; the origin of the sense is not settled.

Lesson 4 of 5.
أمعاء (amʿāʾ) — the intestines, the bowels.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Arabic for the spleen, then the Arabic for the stomach.

You'll want to know: أمعاء.
أمعاء — amʿāʾ — "the intestines, the bowels".
الأمعاء طويلة جدا. al-amʿāʾ ṭawīla jiddan. The intestines are very long.
It is a plural; the singular معى is rare in daily speech.

The word, taken apart.
أمعاء (amʿāʾ) is the plural of miʿan, a gut, and is built on the native Arabic root m-ʿ-y (Lane). The plural pattern afʿāl (as in akhbār) is common for short nouns.

Lesson 5 of 5.
نبض (nabḍ) — the pulse.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Arabic for the stomach, then the Arabic for the intestines.

You'll want to know: نبض.
نبض — nabḍ — "the pulse".
فحص الطبيب نبض المريض. faḥaṣa aṭ-ṭabīb nabḍ al-marīḍ. The doctor checked the patient's pulse.

The word, taken apart.
نبض (nabḍ) is built on the native Arabic root n-b-ḍ, "to beat, to throb" (Lane). Nabḍ is each beat of the blood.
