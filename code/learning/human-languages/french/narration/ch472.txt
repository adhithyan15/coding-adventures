French, chapter 472: Souvenirs et regrets.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
la nostalgie — nostalgia, longing for the past.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the French for to sob, then the French for to comfort.

You'll want to know: la nostalgie.
la nostalgie — "nostalgia, longing for the past".
Je pense à mon enfance avec nostalgie. I think of my childhood with nostalgia.
Avoir la nostalgie de — to miss, to long for.

The word, taken apart.
la nostalgie comes from New Latin nostalgia (EtymDB), coined from Greek nóstos "return home" and álgos "pain": it first named homesickness.

Lesson 2 of 5.

You'll want to know: regretter.
regretter — "to regret; to miss".
Je regrette mon ancien quartier. I miss my old neighbourhood.
Je regrette also means I am sorry: je regrette, c'est complet.

The word, taken apart.
regretter comes from Old French regreter "to lament the dead" (EtymDB), built from re- and an old verb for weeping, probably of Germanic origin.

Lesson 3 of 5.
la mélancolie — melancholy, gloom.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the French for nostalgia, then the French for to regret.

You'll want to know: la mélancolie.
la mélancolie — "melancholy, gloom".
L'automne me donne toujours un peu de mélancolie. Autumn always makes me a little melancholy.
The adjective is mélancolique.

The word, taken apart.
la mélancolie comes through Latin melancholia from Greek melankholía (EtymDB), built on mélas "black" and kholḗ "bile": old medicine blamed sadness on black bile.

Lesson 4 of 5.
émouvoir — to move, to touch (emotionally).

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the French for to regret, then the French for melancholy.

You'll want to know: émouvoir.
émouvoir — "to move, to touch (emotionally)".
Sa lettre m'a beaucoup ému. Her letter moved me a lot.
Past participle ému: j'étais ému — I was moved.

The word, taken apart.
émouvoir comes from Latin ēmovēre "to move out, to stir", built on movēre "to move" (Lewis & Short).

Lesson 5 of 5.
bouleverser — to deeply upset, to overwhelm; to turn upside down.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the French for melancholy, then the French for to move.

You'll want to know: bouleverser.
bouleverser — "to deeply upset, to overwhelm; to turn upside down".
Cette nouvelle a bouleversé toute la famille. This news deeply upset the whole family.
Also of plans or a room: il a bouleversé nos projets.

The word, taken apart.
bouleverser is built from boule "ball", from Latin bulla "a bubble", and verser "to tip over", from Latin versāre "to turn", built on vertere (Lewis & Short): to send rolling like a ball.
