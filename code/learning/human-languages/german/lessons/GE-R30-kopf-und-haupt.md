# Kopf und Haupt — two words for one head, and only one of them survived

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GE-SOUND-NASE-03, GE-SOUND-SEHEN-01] -->

[PAUSE 3s] The nose. Say it — long **a**, and the **s** buzzes like a *z*. Then
say the verb for what the eyes above it do.

## Across the family
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GE-LEX-HAUPT-02, GE-CULTURE-HEAD-CONTAINERS-05, GE-COMPOUND-KOPFSCHMERZEN-04] -->

German has two words for a head, and the one it uses every day is the one that
did not start out meaning a head at all.

| word | where it came from | where it is now |
|---|---|---|
| **Haupt** | the inherited Germanic head-word, cousin of English *head* | formal, poetic, and alive mainly in compounds |
| **Kopf** | a word for a **cup** or **bowl** | the ordinary, everyday head |

A container word pushed the inherited word aside — the same move that gave
French *tête* from Latin *testa*, a pot. Bodies get named after the things they
resemble, and the joke outlives the people who made it.

Which is why the pains go on **Kopf** and not on *Haupt*: *Kopfschmerzen* is
built from the everyday word, because that is the word you reach for when
something hurts.
