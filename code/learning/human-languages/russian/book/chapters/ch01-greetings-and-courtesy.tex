\chapter{Greetings and courtesy}
\label{ch:russian-greetings}

This chapter grows a complete courtesy exchange from six expressions. Cyrillic
arrives inside each word, starting with the four letters that most often fool a
Latin-alphabet reader. Companion lessons: \texttt{RU-C01-*.md}.

\section{\texorpdfstring{\ru{привет}}{privet} \emph{(priv\'et)} --- informal hello}

\begin{scriptstep}
Read left to right. In \ru{привет}, \ru{п} says $p$, \ru{р} says $r$,
\ru{и} says ``ee,'' \ru{в} says $v$, \ru{е} begins with a light ``ye,'' and
\ru{т} says $t$. Two shapes lie to a Latin-trained eye: \ru{р} looks like $p$
but says $r$; \ru{в} looks like $B$ but says $v$. Stress the final syllable:
\emph{pri-VYET}.
\end{scriptstep}

\begin{rootweb}
\emph{Priv\'et} contains the old \emph{vet-} family associated with speaking,
news, and counsel. The same family is visible in \ru{совет} \emph{sov\'et}, a
council or piece of advice --- the word behind the international name
\emph{Soviet}. A greeting and a council both begin with words exchanged.
\end{rootweb}

\begin{usage}
Use \ru{привет} with a friend, child, or someone you already address informally.
It is not the safest first word for a stranger in a formal setting.
\end{usage}

\begin{checkpoint}
Which two letters are false friends? Point to \ru{р} and say $r$; point to
\ru{в} and say $v$. Then read \ru{привет} once with final stress.
\end{checkpoint}

\section{\texorpdfstring{\ru{здравствуйте}}{zdravstvuyte}
  \emph{(zdr\'avstvuyte)} --- formal hello}

\begin{scriptstep}
This long word is learned as a sound shape before every letter is writable.
Aim first for \emph{ZDRAST-vuy-tye}: everyday speech compresses the central
cluster and the first written \ru{в} is often not heard. Stress stays near the
front. Recognizing a contraction is enough; perfect consonant gymnastics are not.
\end{scriptstep}

\begin{rootweb}
\ru{здравствуйте} is historically an instruction: ``be healthy.'' Its core is
\ru{здрав-}, the health family also visible in \ru{здоровье} ``health.'' The
ending \ru{-те} is plural and also polite, so the word carries respect in its
grammar as well as goodwill in its root.
\end{rootweb}

\begin{usage}
Use \ru{здравствуйте} with a stranger, an older person, a professional contact,
or a group. Contrast only the register: \ru{привет} informal;
\ru{здравствуйте} polite or plural.
\end{usage}

\begin{checkpoint}
What wish hides inside the formal greeting? ``Be healthy.'' Which ending helps
make it polite? \ru{-те}. Choose the formal greeting for a new teacher.
\end{checkpoint}

\section{\texorpdfstring{\ru{спасибо}}{spasibo} \emph{(spas\'ibo)} --- thank you}

\begin{scriptstep}
Reuse \ru{с} $s$, another false friend because it looks like Latin $C$.
The stress is in the middle: \emph{spa-SEE-ba}. The final written \ru{о} is
unstressed and reduces toward an $a$-like sound. Russian spelling preserves the
word while speech weakens the vowel.
\end{scriptstep}

\begin{rootweb}
\ru{спасибо} wore down from \ru{спаси Бог}: ``God save [you].'' English
\emph{goodbye} grew from ``God be with ye,'' and Spanish \emph{adi\'os} points
``to God.'' Different languages turned short blessings into ordinary social
words; modern \emph{spas\'ibo} is simply ``thank you.''
\end{rootweb}

\begin{usage}
Build a safe formal exchange: \ru{Здравствуйте!} --- \ru{Здравствуйте!} ---
\ru{Спасибо.} One greeting and one thanks are enough.
\end{usage}

\begin{checkpoint}
Where is the stress in \ru{спасибо}? On \emph{-si-}. What happens to final
unstressed \ru{о}? It sounds more $a$-like. Say \emph{spa-SEE-ba}.
\end{checkpoint}

\section{\texorpdfstring{\ru{да}}{da} \emph{(da)} --- yes}

\begin{scriptstep}
Two letters: \ru{д} $d$ and \ru{а} $a$ as in ``father.'' Neither is a false
friend. Read \ru{да} as one clear syllable.
\end{scriptstep}

\begin{usage}
\ru{Да} is a complete ``yes.'' Russian also uses it to keep a conversation
moving, rather like ``right'' or ``well,'' but affirmative \emph{da} is the only
job due now.
\end{usage}

\begin{checkpoint}
Read \ru{да}. Answer a harmless yes/no question with it once.
\end{checkpoint}

\section{\texorpdfstring{\ru{нет}}{net} \emph{(nyet)} --- no, literally not-is}

\begin{scriptstep}
\ru{н} is the third major false friend: it looks like Latin $H$ but says $n$.
At the start of this word \ru{е} carries a ``ye'' sound, then \ru{т} gives $t$:
\emph{nyet}.
\end{scriptstep}

\begin{rootweb}
\ru{нет} compresses an older combination of \ru{не} ``not'' and a form of
\ru{есть} ``is/exists'': roughly ``there is not.'' The negative \emph{ne} goes
back to Indo-European *ne, a deep relative of English \emph{no}, \emph{not},
\emph{never}, and \emph{nay}.
\end{rootweb}

\begin{usage}
Contrast complete answers only: \ru{да} --- yes; \ru{нет} --- no.
\end{usage}

\begin{checkpoint}
What sound does \ru{н} make? Say $n$, then answer ``no'' with \ru{нет}.
\end{checkpoint}

\section{\texorpdfstring{\ru{пожалуйста}}{pozhaluysta}
  \emph{(pozh\'alusta)} --- please and you are welcome}

\begin{scriptstep}
Meet \ru{ж}, the sound $zh$ in English ``measure.'' The careful spelling is
longer than ordinary speech: aim for \emph{pa-ZHAL-sta}. Stress \emph{-zhal-}
and allow the middle syllable to contract after you can recognize the full word.
\end{scriptstep}

\begin{rootweb}
The word grew from \ru{пожалуй}, a form connected with granting or doing a
favour, plus the old particle \ru{-ста}. That favour-history explains both
modern jobs: a request can begin ``please,'' and a received thanks can be
answered ``you are welcome.''
\end{rootweb}

\begin{usage}
Ask with \ru{пожалуйста}; answer \ru{спасибо} with \ru{пожалуйста}. Context
chooses between ``please'' and ``you are welcome.''
\end{usage}

\begin{checkpoint}
What new sound does \ru{ж} carry? $Zh$. Answer \ru{Спасибо} with
\ru{Пожалуйста}.
\end{checkpoint}

\section{Chapter 1 retrieval --- choose the register}

\begin{usage}
For a friend: \ru{Привет!} For a stranger: \ru{Здравствуйте!} Continue either
exchange with \ru{Спасибо.} --- \ru{Пожалуйста.} Then practise the answer pair
\ru{Да.} --- \ru{Нет.}
\end{usage}

\begin{checkpoint}
Without looking back, produce: informal hello, formal hello, thank you, you are
welcome, yes, no. Now read the six Cyrillic forms in the same order.
\end{checkpoint}
