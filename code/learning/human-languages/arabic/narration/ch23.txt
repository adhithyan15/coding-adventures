Arabic, chapter 23: You're Welcome.
2 lessons. You can do the first 1 of them in the car; after that you will want to have stopped.


Lesson 1 of 2.
عفوا (ʿafwan) — "you're welcome," from a root meaning "to erase".

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Your very first Arabic lesson already used this word once, in passing, as the reply to shukran. Here it gets its own full treatment — starting with a root that literally means "to erase."

The word, taken apart.
عفوا (ʿafwan), root ʿ-f-w (ع-ف-و): the root's core sense is "to erase, obliterate, remove" — and "to pardon, forgive" grows directly out of that, since forgiving something is, metaphorically, erasing it. The same root gives العفو (al-ʿAfuww, "the Supreme Pardoner"), one of the 99 names of Allah in Islamic tradition.

You'll want to know — Be honest: ʿafwan does more than one job.
ʿAfwan answers shukran as "you're welcome" — but depending on tone and context, the exact same word also does duty as "excuse me," "pardon," or "sorry." One root, one word, several closely related jobs — all growing from that same "erase/pardon" sense.

Grammar Lens: the same ending as shukran itself.
Look closely at the spelling: عفوا ends in the same tanwīn fatḥ ("-an") you already met in شكرا (shukran) — the indefinite accusative ending. Neither word is a full sentence with its own verb; both are fixed, idiomatic accusative forms functioning as a complete conversational turn on their own. The grammar you learned once, for shukran, already explains ʿafwan too.

Lesson 2 of 2.
Please, sorry, thank you — the courtesy kit retrieved whole — yes, please, sorry and thank you, the two letters that write the last of them, and the fact that three of the four bend to the person you are speaking to.

Before we start: this one needs your hands, so it is not a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, for letter shapes on the page. You can listen to everything else now — leave the sections called Script: the two letters inside شكرا and Writing: what changes when letters join until you have stopped, and I will say so again when we reach it.

Warm-up.
[pause 4 seconds]
Say yes, please, sorry and thank you in Arabic, in that order, before reading on. Four short words, each taught once, none asked for since.

You'll want to know: three of the four bend.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
word: please. to a man: من فضلك min faḍlak. to a woman: من فضلك min faḍlik.
word: sorry. to a man: آسف āsif. to a woman: آسفة āsifa.
word: yes. to a man: نعم naʿam. to a woman: نعم naʿam.
Naʿam is the clear standard yes, safe anywhere, and it has spoken neighbours you will also hear. Min faḍlik is the only one of the three whose bend is invisible: the written form does not change at all, because only the unwritten short vowel moves.
آسف is stranger than English sorry, because it is not a fixed word — it is a gendered adjective, from the verb asifa, to grieve or regret. So a woman apologising says a different word from a man. For a quick, ungendered excuse me, Arabic reaches for معذرة maʿdhira instead.

Script: the two letters inside شكرا.
ش shīn is not a new shape at all: it is س sīn with three dots on top — the same trick the boat-bowl plays, where ب takes one dot below, ت two above and ث three above. The dots do the work; the skeleton is shared.
The other new letter in the word is ك kāf.

Writing: what changes when letters join.
[once you have stopped driving — write: سلام right to left — sīn, lām, alif, mīm]
[once you have stopped driving — write: شكرا — shīn with its three dots, then kāf, rāʾ, alif]
What changes when a letter joins is the connector at each edge. What stays is the skeleton — which is why a letter you learned standing alone is still readable buried in the middle of a word.

Why it's said this way.
The reply to thanks is عفوا ʿafwan — it is nothing, you are pardoned — from a root meaning to forgive or waive. English answers gratitude by accepting it. Arabic answers by waving it away: you thank, and they erase the debt.

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 4 seconds]
Give the two forms of sorry and what makes them differ. (آسف and آسفة — a gendered adjective.) Give the reply to شكرا and its literal force. (عفوا — it is nothing, you are pardoned.) Say what ش is made of. (س plus three dots.) And say which courtesy word bends without the spelling changing. (من فضلك.)
