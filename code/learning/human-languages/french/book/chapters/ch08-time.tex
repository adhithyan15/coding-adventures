\chapter{Telling the Time}
\label{ch:time}

With your numbers in hand, you can tell the time. It takes one key word,
\emph{heure} (``hour''), plus two hours that refuse a number and take a name
instead: \emph{midi} and \emph{minuit}.
\emph{Practice lessons: \texttt{lessons/FR-C08-*.md}.}

\section{\emph{heure} --- the hour, and telling the time}
\label{lesson:heure}

\begin{sounds}
The \emph{h} of \emph{heure} is silent (as in English \emph{hour}): say
\emph{eur}.\\[3pt]
Because it starts with a vowel-sound, the number links onto it: \emph{deux
heures} $=$ \emph{deu-z-eur}, \emph{trois heures} $=$ \emph{troi-z-eur}.
\end{sounds}

\begin{cousinweb}
\textbf{heure} $\leftarrow$ Latin \emph{h\={o}ra} $\leftarrow$ Greek
\emph{h\={o}r\={a}}. The Greek word meant ``a period, a season, the right time''
(the goddesses of the seasons were the \emph{Horae}); Latin narrowed it to
``hour,'' and French wore \emph{h\={o}ra} down to \emph{heure}.\\[3pt]
English took the same Latin \emph{h\={o}ra} and made \textbf{hour} --- so
\emph{heure} and \emph{hour} are the same word, spelt apart. (The silent
\emph{h} in both is a fossil of the Latin spelling.)
\end{cousinweb}

\begin{grammarlens}
\textbf{\emph{il est \ldots{} heure(s)}.} To tell the clock time, French says
\emph{il est} (``it is'') $+$ the number $+$ \emph{heure(s)}. Literally ``it is
two \emph{hours}'' --- French counts the hours, where English says ``o'clock''
(itself a worn-down ``of the clock''). Add \emph{et quart} (``and a quarter''),
\emph{et demie} (``and a half''), \emph{moins le quart} (``quarter to'') later;
the core is \emph{il est \ldots{} heures}.
\end{grammarlens}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
French & English & note \\
\midrule
\emph{Il est une heure.} & It is one o'clock. & \emph{une heure} --- singular \\
\emph{Il est deux heures.} & It is two o'clock. & \emph{deux heures} --- plural \\
\emph{Il est dix heures.} & It is ten o'clock. & \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\section{\emph{midi} and \emph{minuit} --- noon and midnight}
\label{lesson:midi-minuit}

Two hours don't get a number --- they get a name. \emph{midi} (noon) and
\emph{minuit} (midnight) are the pivots of the day, and both are literally ``the
middle'' of the day and of the night.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
French & $\leftarrow$ Latin & literally \\
\midrule
\emph{midi} & \emph{medius di\={e}s} & ``the \textbf{mid}-\textbf{day}'' \\
\emph{minuit} & \emph{media nox} & ``the \textbf{mid}-\textbf{night}'' \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{cousinweb}
\textbf{midi} $=$ \emph{medius di\={e}s}, ``mid-day.'' You can still see both
halves: \emph{mi-} is ``middle'' (from \emph{medius}, cousin of English
\textbf{medium}, \textbf{mid}, \textbf{middle}), and \emph{-di} is the
\emph{di\={e}s} ``day'' you met in \emph{lundi} and \emph{mardi}. So \emph{midi}
is ``the day's middle.''\\[3pt]
\textbf{minuit} $=$ \emph{media nox}, ``mid-night.'' Here \emph{mi-} is again
``middle,'' and \emph{-nuit} is \emph{nox}/\emph{noct-}, ``night'' (cousin of
English \textbf{night}, and the \emph{noct-} of \textbf{nocturnal}). So
\emph{minuit} is ``the night's middle'' --- exactly like English
\textbf{mid-night}.
\end{cousinweb}

To use them, drop the \emph{heures} --- they \emph{are} the hour:
\emph{Il est midi.} ``It is noon.'' \emph{Il est minuit.} ``It is midnight.''

\begin{culture}
English \textbf{noon} comes from Latin \emph{n\={o}na h\={o}ra}, ``the
\emph{ninth} hour'' --- once mid-afternoon, it drifted earlier until it landed
on midday. French kept the tidier ``mid-day.''
\end{culture}
