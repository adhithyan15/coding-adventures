Telugu, chapter 62: Things People Make.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
దారం (dāraṁ) — thread.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: what did mirapa mean?

You'll want to know: దారం.
దారం (dāraṁ) — "thread".
దారం (dāraṁ) is thread — spun cotton, fine enough to sew with and strong enough to tie a knot that holds. It is the first thing in this chapter and the smallest.
The word may have come in from the north; the dictionaries do not settle it.
దారం (dāraṁ) does ceremonial work too. The thread tied round a wrist at a festival is a దారం (dāraṁ), and once it is on it stays there until it falls off by itself.
DEDR 3167 lists Telugu dāramu, "cord, twine", with Tamil tār, "cord", and Kannada dāra, "thread", and asks, with a query, whether it came from Indo-Aryan, comparing Prakrit dāra, "waist-band", and Sanskrit davara, "string" (CDIAL 6225).

What you've built.
The first thing a house makes for itself.

Lesson 2 of 5.
సూది (sūdi) — a needle.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: what did dāraṁ mean?

You'll want to know: సూది.
సూది (sūdi) — "a needle".
సూది (sūdi) is a needle. It arrived from Sanskrit sūcī, and Telugu wore the middle sound down to a d — a change the language makes often enough that the two shapes line up easily once you know to expect it.
A సూది (sūdi) is useless without a దారం (dāraṁ), and a దారం (dāraṁ) is only a tangle without a సూది (sūdi). That is about as neat a pair as this book has come across.
The same word does the వైద్యుడు's work: an injection is a సూది (sūdi), and somebody at a clinic who uses the word is not talking about mending a బట్ట.
CDIAL 13551 lists Sanskrit sūcī, "needle".

What you've built.
Two, and neither is much use without the other.

Lesson 3 of 5.
చీపురు (cīpuru) — a broom.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: what did sūdi mean?

You'll want to know: చీపురు.
చీపురు (cīpuru) — "a broom".
చీపురు (cīpuru) is the broom: a bundle of coconut-leaf ribs bound tight at one end, used bent at the waist rather than standing upright.
The word is inherited. Sweeping is the first work of the morning in a Telugu house, done before the ముగ్గు goes down at the doorstep.
A చీపురు (cīpuru) is treated with a little care because of that. It is not stood on its bristles, it is not stepped over, and it is not lent out lightly.

The word, taken apart.
చీపురు (cīpuru) is native Dravidian. DEDR 2599 lists the Telugu word for "a broom" with Tamil cī, "to sweep", and Kannada cīpari, "broom".

What you've built.
Three, and this one starts the day.

Lesson 4 of 5.
గొడుగు (goḍugu) — an umbrella.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: what did cīpuru mean?

You'll want to know: గొడుగు.
గొడుగు (goḍugu) — "an umbrella".
గొడుగు (goḍugu) is an umbrella, and in Telugu it answers the sun as much as the rain — which is the commoner use of it on a hot morning.
The word is inherited. Long before cloth and wire there was a గొడుగు (goḍugu) of palm leaf, and the ceremonial one held over a deity or over a bride is still made and named the same way.
It settles two things already met: గాలి will turn it inside out and మంచు will not, so a గొడుగు (goḍugu) goes out in one kind of weather and stays at home in the other.

The word, taken apart.
గొడుగు (goḍugu) is native Dravidian, one word with Tamil kuṭai, Kannada koḍe and Malayalam kuṭa, "umbrella" (DEDR 1663). DEDR compares Sanskrit utkūṭa, "umbrella", a word known only from lexicons.

What you've built.
Four.

Lesson 5 of 5.
అద్దం (addaṁ) — a mirror.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: what did goḍugu mean?

You'll want to know: అద్దం.
అద్దం (addaṁ) — "a mirror".
అద్దం (addaṁ) is a mirror. Of the five things here it is the only one a household buys rather than makes, and the only one whose name is probably borrowed — usually traced, through Prakrit addāa, to Sanskrit ādarśa, "what shows back".
The other four come out of whatever grows nearby: a దారం (dāraṁ) out of cotton, a చీపురు (cīpuru) out of leaf ribs, a గొడుగు (goḍugu) out of palm and cane, and a సూది (sūdi) out of a scrap of metal from the smith at the edge of the గ్రామం. An అద్దం (addaṁ) needs a factory.
Asking for one is where దయచేసి earns its keep. అద్దం (addaṁ) ఇవ్వండి on its own lands like an instruction; with దయచేసి in front it lands like a request.
DEDR 147 lists Telugu addamu, "mirror", notes that Tamil attam is a loan from Telugu, and compares Prakrit addāa, "mirror"; CDIAL 1143 lists Sanskrit ādarśa, "mirror".

What you've built.
Five: దారం (dāraṁ), సూది (sūdi), చీపురు (cīpuru), గొడుగు (goḍugu), అద్దం (addaṁ). Enough to ask for what you need with దయచేసి in front of it.
