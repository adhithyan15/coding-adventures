Lesson 1 of 8.
ع (ʿayn) — the eye that became an O.

Before we start: this one needs your hands, so it is not a driving lesson. You will want a pen and something to write on and your eyes, for letter shapes on the page. You can listen to everything else now — leave the sections called Script — Draw it, Script — A third dots-family, Script — The surprise: ʿayn becomes O, Script — Where you've heard it and Guided Practice until you have stopped, and I will say so again when we reach it.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
This is the letter people think of when they think "Arabic." ع (ʿayn) writes a sound made deep in the throat — one English simply does not have. And its history holds the best surprise in the whole alphabet: this consonant is the ancestor of our vowel O.

Script — Draw it.
Break it apart — an open curve, then a bowl:
the open-mouth curve on top — like a small c opening to the left.
the bowl below, when ʿayn stands alone or ends a word — a scoop underneath.
Joined in the middle of a word it flattens into a little wedge; standing alone it shows the full curve-over-bowl. It is a connector, so it changes coat by position like sīn and lām.

Script — A third dots-family.
You know the bowl family (ب/ن/ت/ث) from Lesson 4 and the hook-and-tail family (ح/خ/ج) from Lesson 7. ʿayn opens a third: its skeleton is shared with غ (ghayn), which is the same shape plus one dot above. Same trick, third time — by now you should expect it.

Script — The surprise: ʿayn becomes O.
ʿayn comes from Phoenician ʿayin, meaning "eye" — and the oldest forms were drawn as one. When the Greeks borrowed the Phoenician alphabet, they had no throat-sound like ʿ. So they did what borrowers do with a sign they can't use: they repurposed it — and turned the eye-shaped letter into the vowel omicron, our O.
So the round O you write is a Phoenician eye, and Arabic's ʿayn is the same sign that kept its original consonant job. (Compare the thread so far: ʾālep becomes A, bēt becomes B, mem becomes M, kaph becomes K, rēsh becomes R — and now ʿayin becomes O.)

Script — Where you've heard it.
You have been saying this sound since Chapter 1: السلام عليكم (as-salāmu ʿalaykum) — the ʿ in ʿalaykum is this letter. And the farewell you'll write two lessons from now, مع السلامة (as-salāma) (maʿa s-salāma), opens with it.

Lesson 2 of 8.
ه and ة — the loop, and the ending that means "feminine".

Before we start: this one needs your hands, so it is not a driving lesson. You will want a pen and something to write on and your eyes, for letter shapes on the page. You can listen to everything else now — leave the sections called Script — ه (hāʾ) — the loop that never looks the same, Script — ة (tāʾ marbūṭa) — the "tied tāʾ" and Guided Practice until you have stopped, and I will say so again when we reach it.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Two related shapes. ه (hāʾ) is a plain "h" — and the most shape-shifting letter you've met, wearing four genuinely different coats. Then ة, a letter built out of it, whose whole job is to say one grammatical thing: this word is feminine.

Script — ه (hāʾ) — the loop that never looks the same.
Break it apart — there is really just one piece:
the loop — a closed round body.
But that loop changes shape a lot by position more than any letter so far: standing alone it's a neat circle-ish loop; at the start it opens out; in the middle it becomes a small figure-of-eight pinched in the centre; at the end it trails. Same letter, four faces — the four-coats rule from Lesson 2 taken to its extreme.
From Phoenician hē — the ancestor of Greek epsilon and Latin E. (Another one for the family tree: ʾālep becomes A, bēt becomes B, hē becomes E, mem becomes M, ʿayin becomes O.)

Script — ة (tāʾ marbūṭa) — the "tied tāʾ".
Now look carefully at this:
ه = hāʾ's loop, no dots.
ة = the same loop + tāʾ's two dots above.
Its name says exactly what it is: tāʾ marbūṭa, "the tied-up tāʾ" — a ت (tāʾ, Lesson 4) whose ends have been tied into a loop. It appears only at the end of a word, and it is the standard mark of a feminine noun or adjective.
You already know its partner: back in Lesson 4 you learned ت with two dots above. Put those same two dots on a hāʾ loop and you get the feminine ending. Nothing new to memorise — just two known pieces combined.
Where you've seen it: the farewell مع السلامة (as-salāma) (maʿa s-salāma) ends in ة — salāma is feminine. That's the next lesson.

Lesson 3 of 8.
مع السلامة (as-salāma) (maʿa s-salāma) — the goodbye, and a word you already know.

Before we start: this one needs your hands, so it is not a driving lesson. You will want a pen and something to write on, your eyes, for letter shapes on the page and your eyes, because the lesson points at something written down. You can listen to everything else now — leave the sections called Script — Part 1 — مع (maʿa, "with"), Script — Part 2 — السلامة (as-salāma, "the safety"), Script — The root s-l-m, one more time, Script — Assemble it — right to left and Guided Practice until you have stopped, and I will say so again when we reach it.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
The closing move. مع السلامة (as-salāma) (maʿa s-salāma) is how Arabic says goodbye — literally "with safety," go in peace. And the heart of it is a word you wrote all the way back in Lesson 3: سلام. Everything since has been leading here.

Script — Part 1 — مع (maʿa, "with").
Two letters, both known:
م (mīm, Lesson 3) — round head, joining left into…
ع (ʿayn, Lesson 10) — the open curve over its bowl, closing the word.
becomes مع = maʿa, "with."

Script — Part 2 — السلامة (as-salāma, "the safety").
Look at what this is made of:
ال — the al- ("the"), alif + lām, both from Lessons 1–2. You've read it since Chapter 1 in الخير and السلام عليكم.
سلام — the exact word you assembled in Lesson 3: sīn, lām, alif, mīm.
ة — the tāʾ marbūṭa from Lesson 11, marking salāma as feminine.
So السلامة (as-salāma) = al- + salām + -a. You are not learning a new word; you are adding two edges to one you already own.

Script — The root s-l-m, one more time.
salām, salāma, islām, muslim — all from the Semitic root s–l–m, "peace, safety, wholeness." The greeting you learned first (السلام عليكم, "peace be upon you") and the farewell you learn last (مع السلامة (as-salāma) (maʿa s-salāma), "with safety") come from the same three consonants. Arabic opens and closes a conversation with one idea.

Script — Assemble it — right to left.
م then ع (ʿayn) becomes مع (maʿa).
lift; ا + ل (the al-), the lām joining into…
س, ل, ا — and remember the لا ligature, and that alif breaks…
م, then ة to close.
becomes مع السلامة (as-salāma) (maʿa s-salāma).

Lesson 4 of 8.
مع (maʿa) — "with".

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Two letters, both already yours, and a word that will carry the whole chapter.

You'll want to know — The letters.
(Skim if you read Arabic.) م (mīm) from Lesson 3 of the writing set, and ع (ʿayn) from Lesson 10 — the throat sound whose Phoenician ancestor meant "eye," and which the Greeks recycled into the vowel O.
Right to left: م, ع becomes maʿa. The ʿayn is not a vowel; it is a constriction deep in the throat. Take your time with it.

You'll want to know — The word.
مع (maʿa) = "with, together with."
It is a preposition, and unlike الـ (al-) or بـ (bi-) from Chapter 3, it is a free-standing word — it does not glue onto the next one. Compare:
[a table of 4 rows, read aloud]
الـ al- "the". attaches?: yes.
بـ bi- "in/with". attaches?: yes.
لـ li- "to". attaches?: yes.
مع maʿa "with". attaches?: no.
So Arabic has two words touching on "with": the one-letter بـ (bi-, "in/by means of," as in bi-khayr) and the full word مع (maʿa, "accompanied by"). Bi-khayr is "in goodness"; maʿa is standing next to something.

Why it's said this way — The Semitic cousin.
maʿa from Semitic ʿimma, and its Hebrew cousin is עִם (ʿim), "with." The same pairing you met with salām / shalom in Chapter 1 and ism / shem in Chapter 2. Three chapters, three cousins — the family resemblance is systematic, not a coincidence.

[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: the contrast — "bi-khayr in goodness, maʿa with"]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: the cousin — "maʿa, Hebrew ʿim"]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[once you have stopped driving — write: مع (maʿa) — mīm joining into ʿayn]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
What does مع mean? ("With.") Does it attach to the next word, like al- and bi-? (No — it stands alone.) What is the difference between bi- and maʿa? (Bi- = "in/by means of"; maʿa = "accompanied by".) What is its Hebrew cousin? (ʿim.) Next: the word it goes with — and you already know most of it.


Lesson 5 of 8.
السلامة (as-salāma) — "the safety".

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
This looks like a long new word. It isn't. It is one word you learned in Chapter 1 with a piece added at each end.

You'll want to know — Taking it apart.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
piece: الـ. what it is: al-, "the". met in: Ch. 1.
piece: سلام. what it is: salām, "peace". met in: Ch. 1 — the very first word.
piece: ة. what it is: tāʾ marbūṭa, feminine ending. met in: writing Lesson 11.
becomes السلامة (as-salāma) = al- + salām + -a = "the safety."
Every letter is one you have already written by hand.

You'll want to know — The pattern that changed the meaning.
Arabic doesn't just add endings — it pours a root into a shape. Here the root s–l–m goes into the pattern faʿāla, which turns a thing into the state of being that thing:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
salām means peace.
salāma means safety, soundness, wellbeing — the state of being at peace.
Same three consonants, one new shape, a new but related word. This is the engine Chapter 1 promised, working in front of you.

You'll want to know — Two small rules, both already learned.
1. The sun letter. S (س) is a sun letter, so the l of al- assimilates to it: written الس, but said as-salāma, not al-salāma. The same thing happens in السلام عليكم — the greeting you have said since Chapter 1.
2. The feminine ة. Tāʾ marbūṭa marks salāma as feminine. Arabic abstract nouns very often are.

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
What three pieces make السلامة? (al- + salām + ة.) What did the faʿāla pattern do to the meaning? (Turned "peace" into "the state of being at peace" — safety.) Why is it as-salāma and not al-salāma? (س is a sun letter; the l assimilates.) What does the ة tell you? (The word is feminine.) Next: put maʿa in front of it.


Lesson 6 of 8.
مع السلامة (as-salāma) (maʿa s-salāma) — "goodbye".

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, because the lesson points at something written down. You can listen to all of it now and come back to the parts that need looking at once you have stopped.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Put the last two lessons together and the farewell falls out. You have already hand-written this phrase; now it means something.

You'll want to know — The assembly.
مع (maʿa, "with") + السلامة (as-salāma, "the safety") becomes مع السلامة (maʿa s-salāma) — "with safety."
Said as one run: maʿa s-salāma — the a of maʿa swallows the al-'s vowel, and the sun-letter rule doubles the s.
It is not "goodbye" in the English sense of a plain sign-off. It is a wish: go in safety.

You'll want to know — The circle closes.
Look at what Chapter 1 opened with and what Chapter 4 closes with:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
first greeting. phrase: السلام عليكم as-salāmu ʿalaykum. root: s–l–m.
last farewell. phrase: مع السلامة (as-salāma) maʿa s-salāma. root: s–l–m.
The same three consonants. Arabic begins and ends a conversation with one idea — peace, safety, wholeness. The root engine you met in the very first lesson has been running the whole time; here it simply comes back round.
The family, one more time: salām (peace), salāma (safety), islām (submission, i.e. entering peace), muslim (one who does so).

You'll want to know — English does the same trick.
Goodbye looks like a single word. It is worn down from God be with ye. So the English farewell is also built on "with" — you are being commended to something protective as you go.
[a table of 4 rows, read aloud]
language: Arabic. farewell: maʿa s-salāma. literally: "with safety".
language: English. farewell: goodbye. literally: "God be with ye".
language: Spanish. farewell: adiós. literally: "to God".
language: French. farewell: adieu. literally: "to God".
Four languages, one instinct: as someone leaves, hand them over to safekeeping.

Lesson 7 of 8.
إلى اللقاء (ilā l-liqāʾ) — "see you".

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
The other everyday farewell — and the one with a surprise waiting inside Spanish.

You'll want to know — A note on the letters.
(Skim if you read Arabic.) Two pieces here are beyond what the writing track has covered, so read them for now and draw them later:
ق (qāf) — a k made far back against the soft palate, deeper than ك (kāf). It is not in the letter set you have been writing from.
ى in إلى — this is alif maqṣūra, "shortened alif": it wears yāʾ's skeleton with no dots and is pronounced as a long ā. You already know from writing Lessons 4–5 that dots are what separate letters sharing a body; this is that same principle, taken one step further.
The rest — ا ل ء — you have written.

You'll want to know — The two pieces.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
إلى ilā means "to, until".
اللقاء al-liqāʾ means "the meeting".
becomes إلى اللقاء (ilā l-liqāʾ) = "until the meeting."
لقاء (liqāʾ) comes from the root l–q–y, "to meet." The final ء is the hamza from writing Lesson 6 — a glottal stop, a real consonant here, not decoration.
Note ل is a sun letter too, so al-liqāʾ doubles the l, just as as-salāma doubled the s.

Why it's said this way — The Spanish surprise.
Spanish says goodbye like this:
hasta luego — "until later" hasta mañana — "until tomorrow".
Same shape as ilā l-liqāʾ: "until" + a future point. But it goes further — Spanish hasta is itself an Arabic loanword, from ḥattā ("until"). Of the roughly four thousand Arabic words Spanish took during the eight centuries of al-Andalus, most are nouns; hasta is one of the rare borrowed function words.
So Spanish did not merely copy the pattern — it borrowed the very word that builds it.
[a table of 4 rows, read aloud]
language: Arabic. farewell: ilā l-liqāʾ. shape: until + the meeting.
language: Spanish. farewell: hasta luego. shape: until ( from ḥattā) + later.
language: French. farewell: à bientôt. shape: to + soon.
language: Italian. farewell: a presto. shape: to + soon.
