## Grammar Lens: three words for money on a page
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C456-CUENTA-02, ES-LEX-C456-CUENTA-04] -->

| what it names | the word | where you see it |
|---|---|---|
| what the seller asks | el precio | a label |
| what it comes to for you | el coste | a quote |
| what has been added | el cargo | a bill |

The order is the order of a transaction: you read a *precio*, you work out a
*coste*, and afterwards a *cargo* appears. Any of the three translated as
"price" will read as slightly wrong to a Spanish speaker in two of the three
places.

## Grammar Lens: four of the five were relatives
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C456-CUENTA-01, ES-LEX-C456-CUENTA-03, ES-LEX-C456-CUENTA-05] -->

| new word | what it was built on |
|---|---|
| **la ensalada** | *la sal* — the salted thing |
| **el cargo** | *el carrito* — a load on a cart |
| **el coste** | *costar*, and *estar* underneath it |
| **la agencia** | *la agenda* — things to be done |

*La agencia* is the neat one. Your **agenda** is a Latin plural meaning "the
things that must be done", and an **agencia** is a firm that does them for
you. Diary and travel agent, one verb.

Even **aceptar** has one, though it takes longer to spot: its *capere*, to
take, is the *capere* inside **el principio**, where *princeps* is the one who
**takes first place**. Every word in the chapter had a relative waiting.
