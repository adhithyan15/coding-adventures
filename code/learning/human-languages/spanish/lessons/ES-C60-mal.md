# mal — bien finally gets its opposite

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-BIEN, ES-MORPH-BUENO-BUENA] -->

[PAUSE 2s] *Bien* has been with you since chapter one, and so has *bueno*.

[PAUSE 1s] Both have an opposite, and the pair works exactly the same way.

## The word, taken apart: mal
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-MAL, ES-LEX-MALO, ES-ETYMON-MAL-MALUS]; assesses=[ES-LEX-BIEN] -->

***Malo*** is the adjective — *bad*. ***Mal*** is what is left of it when it
goes in front of a masculine noun, and it is also the adverb — *badly*.

| | |
|---|---|
| *bien* / ***mal*** | well / badly |
| *bueno* / ***malo*** | good / bad |

> *Estoy* ***mal****.* — I'm not well.

[PAUSE 2s] Both come straight from Latin: *bene* and ***malus***, which you can
still hear in English *malady* and *malice*.

## Why it's said this way
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-GRAMMAR-APOCOPE-PATTERN]; assesses=[ES-ETYMON-MUY-MULTUM, ES-LEX-MUY] -->

Now look at what has happened twice in three chapters.

| long form | short form | short one appears |
|---|---|---|
| *mucho* | ***muy*** | before an adjective or adverb |
| *malo* | ***mal*** | before a masculine noun, and as the adverb |

[PAUSE 2s] This is not two exceptions. It is one habit of the language: Spanish
**bites the ending off a word that is leaning on another word**, and keeps it
whole when it stands on its own.

[PAUSE 2s] Once you have heard it twice you will start noticing it everywhere,
and it stops being something to memorise. A short form is a word in a hurry to
get to the next one.
