## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-C345-REF-01]; assesses=[] -->

**mismo** — "same," "very," "self."

Latin had **ipse**, *self, the very one*. Spoken Latin decided that was not
emphatic enough, and started stacking.

First **met**, an emphasising particle, was stuck on the front: *egomet*, *I
myself*. Then the superlative ending *-issimus* was piled on the back. The
result was something like **metipsimus** — roughly *the very most self-est
one*, a word made almost entirely of insistence.

That collapsed into Spanish **mismo**, French *même* and Italian *medesimo*.
All three languages built their ordinary word for *same* out of somebody
thumping a fist on the bar.

English kept *ipse* undisguised in a few borrowed phrases. **Ipso facto** is
*by that very fact*. **Solipsism** is *self-alone-ism*, the belief that only
oneself exists. And **ipsissima verba** are *the very words themselves*.

Spanish uses *mismo* both ways: **el mismo libro** is *the same book*, and
**yo mismo** is *I myself*.
