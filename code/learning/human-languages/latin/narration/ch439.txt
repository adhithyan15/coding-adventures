Latin, chapter 439: To put in, To step out, To haul ashore, To launch, Navigable.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
appellō, appellere — to put in (to land), to bring a ship to shore.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to set sail, then the Latin for a ferryman.

You'll want to know: appellō, appellere.
appellō, appellere — "to put in (to land), to bring a ship to shore".
Nautae nāvem ad lītus appellunt. The sailors bring the ship in to the shore.
appellō, appellere, appulī, appulsum, third conjugation. Do not mix it up with appellō, appellāre 'to call', a different verb.

The word, taken apart.
appellō, appellere is built from ad- 'to' and pellō 'to drive, to push' (EtymDB; Lewis & Short): to drive a ship to land.

Lesson 2 of 5.
ēgredior, ēgredī — to step out, to go out; to land, to disembark.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a ferryman, then the Latin for to put in.

You'll want to know: ēgredior, ēgredī.
ēgredior, ēgredī — "to step out, to go out; to land, to disembark".
Viātōrēs ē nāve ēgrediuntur et ad caupōnam ambulant. The travellers get off the ship and walk to the inn.
Deponent: ēgredior, ēgredī, ēgressus sum. Ē nāve ēgredī, to disembark. English egress comes from it.

The word, taken apart.
ēgredior is built from ē(x)- 'out' and gradior 'to step' (EtymDB; Lewis & Short). English egress comes from its participle ēgressus.

Lesson 3 of 5.
subdūcō, subdūcere — to haul ashore, to draw up (a ship); to take away.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to put in, then the Latin for to step out.

You'll want to know: subdūcō, subdūcere.
subdūcō, subdūcere — "to haul ashore, to draw up (a ship); to take away".
Hieme nautae nāvēs in lītus subdūcunt. In winter the sailors haul the ships up on the shore.
subdūcō, subdūcere, subdūxī, subductum. Ancient ships were pulled up on the beach for the winter.

The word, taken apart.
subdūcō is built from sub- 'from under, up' and dūcō 'to lead, to draw' (EtymDB; Lewis & Short). English subduction comes from the noun subductiō.

Lesson 4 of 5.
dēdūcō, dēdūcere — to launch (a ship); to lead away, to escort.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to step out, then the Latin for to haul ashore.

You'll want to know: dēdūcō, dēdūcere.
dēdūcō, dēdūcere — "to launch (a ship); to lead away, to escort".
Vēre nautae nāvēs in mare dēdūcunt. In spring the sailors launch the ships into the sea.
dēdūcō, dēdūcere, dēdūxī, dēductum. The opposite of subdūcō. English deduce and deduct come from it.

The word, taken apart.
dēdūcō is built from dē- 'down, away' and dūcō 'to lead' (EtymDB; Lewis & Short): to draw a ship down into the water. English deduce and deduct come from it.

Lesson 5 of 5.
nāvigābilis, nāvigābile — navigable.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to haul ashore, then the Latin for to launch.

You'll want to know: nāvigābilis, nāvigābile.
nāvigābilis, nāvigābile — "navigable".
Flūmen magnum et altum est, itaque nāvigābile. The river is big and deep, and so it is navigable.
An adjective of two endings, from nāvigō 'to sail' with -bilis 'that can be'. English navigable comes from it.

The word, taken apart.
nāvigābilis is built on nāvigō 'to sail' with the suffix -bilis 'that can be' (Lewis & Short). English navigable comes from it.
