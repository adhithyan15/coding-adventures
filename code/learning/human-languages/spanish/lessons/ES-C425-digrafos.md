# los dígrafos — two letters, one sound, and a change made in 1994

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-ORTH-C425-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say *chico*, *llave* and *perro*. Each of those has two identical or
paired letters doing a single job. Name how many sounds the doubled part makes.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-ORTH-C425-04]; assesses=[] -->

**un dígrafo** — "a digraph": **two letters that spell one sound.**

Greek *di-* two + *graphē* writing. Spanish has five:

| digraph | example | one sound |
|---|---|---|
| ch | chico | as in English *church* |
| ll | llave | a *y* sound for most speakers |
| rr | perro | the long rolled r |
| gu | guerra | a hard **g**, with the **u** silent |
| qu | queso | a **k**, with the **u** silent |

The last two are the ones that catch a reader out. In *guerra* and *queso* the
**u** is **not pronounced**. It is there to keep the **g** and **c** hard in
front of **e** and **i** — which is exactly why *pingüino* needs the two dots to
cancel the silence.

## Grammar Lens: the 1994 change, and why a dictionary moved
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-ORTH-C425-04, ES-ORTH-C425-01] -->

**Ch** and **ll** used to be letters of the alphabet in their own right, with
their own names (*che*, *elle*) and their own sections in a dictionary. *Chico*
came after every word beginning with **c**, not inside them.

In 1994 they stopped being separate letters. A dictionary printed before then
and one printed after put the same word in a different place:

| word | before 1994 | after |
|---|---|---|
| chico | its own **ch** section | under **c** |
| llave | its own **ll** section | under **l** |

They are still digraphs and still spell one sound. What changed is only how they
are **filed**. If you use an older Spanish dictionary and cannot find a word,
this is usually why.
