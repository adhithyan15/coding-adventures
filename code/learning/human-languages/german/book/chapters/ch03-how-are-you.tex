\chapter{How Are You?}
\label{ch:how-are-you}

Having given your name, you ask after the other person --- and German asks it
with a verb of \emph{motion}: \emph{wie geht es dir?}, ``how goes it to you?''
Around that question sit the two courtesies that hold every conversation
together, \emph{danke} and \emph{bitte}, each of which turns out to be an
English word in disguise. \emph{Practice lessons: \texttt{lessons/GE-C03-*.md}.}

\section{\emph{danke} --- ``thank you,'' which is really ``I think of you''}
\label{lesson:danke}

\begin{sounds}
\emph{DAHN-kuh}: a pure open \emph{ah}, the final \emph{-e} sinking to a soft
\emph{uh}. Stress the front: \emph{DAHN-kuh}.
\end{sounds}

\begin{cousinweb}
\textbf{danke} $\leftarrow$ \textbf{denken}, ``to think.'' The old idea: to
thank someone is to \emph{keep them in mind} --- ``I think of you,'' ``you are
in my thoughts.'' German \emph{Dank} (``thanks'') and \emph{Gedanke} (``a
thought'') are the same root wearing two coats.\\[3pt]
And here is the payoff for an English speaker: \textbf{English ``thank'' and
``think'' are the same word too.} Old English \emph{\th{}ancian} (``thank'')
and \emph{\th{}encan} (``think'') split from one root --- to thank was to
\emph{hold in thought}. So \emph{danke} isn't foreign at all; it is English
\textbf{thank} with the \textbf{think} still visible inside it.
\end{cousinweb}

Three different pictures of gratitude, worth holding side by side:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
Language & ``Thanks'' & Literal idea \\
\midrule
German & \emph{danke} & ``I \textbf{think} of you'' \\
French & \emph{merci} & a \textbf{reward} / \textbf{mercy} \\
Spanish & \emph{gracias} & \textbf{grace} / \textbf{favour} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{grammarlens}
\textbf{Dressing it up.} \emph{danke sch\"{o}n} --- ``thank you \emph{nicely}''
(\emph{sch\"{o}n} $=$ beautiful, nice) $\to$ ``thank you very much.''
\emph{danke sehr} --- ``thanks \emph{very}.'' \emph{vielen Dank} --- ``many
thanks'' (\emph{viel}, ``much,'' comes later).
\end{grammarlens}

\section{\emph{bitte} --- the Swiss-army word: ``please'' \emph{and} ``you're welcome''}
\label{lesson:bitte}

\begin{sounds}
\emph{BIT-tuh}: a short crisp \emph{i}, a clear double-\emph{t}, a soft
\emph{-uh} at the end.
\end{sounds}

\begin{cousinweb}
\textbf{bitte} is the plea-form of \textbf{bitten}, ``to ask, to request, to
pray.'' When you say \emph{bitte} you are, at root, \emph{making a request} ---
``I ask (this of you).''\\[3pt]
That verb \emph{bitten} is a close cousin of two English words. \textbf{bid} ---
as in ``I \emph{bid} you welcome,'' ``do as you're \emph{bidden}'' --- to ask or
command. And \textbf{bead} --- astonishingly, yes: a \emph{bead} was originally
a \textbf{prayer}. People counted their prayers (Old English \emph{gebed}) on a
string, and the name of the prayer slid onto the little ball you counted it
with. So a rosary \emph{bead} is a frozen \emph{request} --- exactly what
\emph{bitte} still is.
\end{cousinweb}

If German gives you one word to overwork, it is \emph{bitte}. It is ``please.''
It is also ``you're welcome,'' and ``here you go,'' and even ``pardon?'' One
word, and it all comes straight from \emph{asking}:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
You say \emph{bitte} when\ldots & It means & Why (it is all ``I ask'') \\
\midrule
making a request & \textbf{please} & ``I ask you\ldots'' \\
answering \emph{danke} & \textbf{you're welcome} & ``I ask nothing back'' \\
handing something over & \textbf{here you go} & ``I offer, at your request'' \\
you didn't hear & \textbf{pardon?} & ``I ask you to repeat'' \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{culture}
For this chapter the key pairing is the courtesy loop: \emph{Danke.} ---
\emph{Bitte.} (``Thanks.'' --- ``You're welcome.'') It is the German twin of
Spanish \emph{de nada} and French \emph{de rien}, and it closes every small
favour.
\end{culture}

\section{\emph{gehen} --- ``to go,'' and it is just English \emph{go}}
\label{lesson:gehen}

\begin{sounds}
In \emph{gehen} the \emph{h} merely \textbf{lengthens} the vowel (it is not a
breathy English \emph{h}): \emph{GAY-en}. The form you need most is
\emph{geht} $=$ \emph{gayt}.
\end{sounds}

\begin{cousinweb}
\textbf{gehen} (``to go'') and English \textbf{go} descend from the \emph{same}
Germanic verb (\emph{*g\=an} / \emph{gehen}) --- no Latin detour, no false
friend, a straight cognate. When you say \emph{geht}, you are saying
\emph{goes}.
\end{cousinweb}

Spanish asks ``how are you?'' with \emph{estar} (``to stand''). French and
German both ask it with \textbf{``to go''} --- \emph{how does it go?} German
states its wellbeing as \emph{motion}, exactly like French \emph{\c{c}a va}:

\begin{center}\itshape
Es geht mir gut. $=$ ``It goes for-me well.'' $\to$ ``I'm doing well.''
\end{center}

\begin{morphologybox}
\textbf{es} $=$ ``it'' (cognate of English \emph{it}).\\[3pt]
\textbf{geht} $=$ ``goes.''\\[3pt]
\textbf{mir} $=$ ``to me, for me'' --- the \emph{dative}, the ``to-whom'' form;
its partner is \emph{dir}, ``to you.''\\[3pt]
\textbf{gut} $=$ ``well'' (Chapter~\ref{ch:greetings} --- cognate of English
\emph{good}).
\end{morphologybox}

So German literally says \textbf{``it goes well \emph{to me}.''} Wellbeing is
something that \emph{goes}, and it goes \emph{to} a person.

\begin{grammarlens}
\textbf{The dative, gently.} German marks \emph{the person something happens
to} with a special form: \emph{ich} (``I'') $\to$ \emph{mir} (``to me'');
\emph{du} (``you'') $\to$ \emph{dir} (``to you''). ``How are you?'' will use
\emph{dir} or \emph{Ihnen} --- ``how does it go \emph{to you}?'' For now, just
recognize \emph{mir} $=$ ``to me.''
\end{grammarlens}

\section{\emph{Wie geht es dir?} --- ``how are you?''}
\label{lesson:wie-geht-es}

\begin{sounds}
German \emph{w} says \emph{v}, so \emph{wie} is \emph{vee}; \emph{ie} is a long
\emph{ee}. The whole question: \emph{vee gayt es deer}.
\end{sounds}

Every piece is already yours: \textbf{wie} (``how''), the \textbf{du} /
\textbf{Sie} choice, and \textbf{gehen} / \textbf{geht} (``to go'').
\emph{wie} is used for manner and degree; \emph{du} goes to a friend, and
capital-S \emph{Sie} to a stranger or an elder (it is literally ``they,''
borrowed as respect). Snap them together:

\begin{center}\itshape
Wie geht es dir? $=$ ``How are you?'' (informal)\\
literally: ``How goes it to you?''
\end{center}

\begin{morphologybox}
\textbf{wie} $=$ ``how'' $\cdot$ \textbf{geht} $=$ ``goes'' $\cdot$ \textbf{es}
$=$ ``it'' $\cdot$ \textbf{dir} $=$ ``to you'' --- the dative of \emph{du}, the
\emph{mir} $\to$ \emph{dir} pair from the last lesson.
\end{morphologybox}

Two registers, and only the ``you'' changes --- the same move as the
\emph{du}/\emph{Sie} split:

\noindent
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.27\linewidth}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}X@{}}
\toprule
Register & Question \\
\midrule
informal (friend, family) & \emph{Wie geht es dir?} (spoken \emph{Wie geht's
dir?} / just \emph{Wie geht's?}) \\
formal (stranger, elder) & \emph{Wie geht es Ihnen?} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}

\emph{es} and \emph{dir} often crush together in speech into \emph{geht's} ---
\emph{Wie geht's?} is the everyday form, and you can answer it with the
\emph{gut} you already have: \emph{Danke, gut!} (``Thanks, well!'').

\begin{grammarlens}
\textbf{Same question, three metaphors.} German \emph{gehen} (go): ``how
\emph{goes} it to you?'' French \emph{aller} (go): ``how does it \emph{go}?''
Spanish \emph{estar} (stand): ``how are you \emph{standing}?'' Two of the three
put wellbeing in motion; Spanish puts it on its feet.
\end{grammarlens}

\section{\emph{es geht} --- the German shrug: ``it goes''}
\label{lesson:es-geht}

Someone asks \emph{Wie geht's?} You are neither great nor terrible. German's
answer is beautifully understated: \textbf{es geht} --- just ``it goes.'' Not
\emph{well}, not \emph{badly} --- it simply \emph{goes}. That bareness
\emph{is} the shrug.

\begin{grammarlens}
In the last lesson you added \emph{gut} to make it positive: \emph{Es geht mir
gut}, ``it goes well for me.'' Say it with \textbf{nothing added} ---
\emph{es geht} --- and the very absence of a ``well'' or ``badly'' makes it
mean ``eh, so-so, it's going, I'm getting by.'' The understatement carries the
whole meaning.
\end{grammarlens}

Your German answer ladder for \emph{Wie geht's?}:

\noindent
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.29\linewidth}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}X@{}}
\toprule
Answer & Sense \\
\midrule
\emph{Sehr gut!} / \emph{Gut, danke} & very well / well, thanks \\
\emph{Es geht} & so-so, okay-ish \\
\emph{So lala} & so-so (a sing-song ``meh''; \emph{lala} is just filler) \\
\emph{Nicht so gut} & not so well \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}

\begin{culture}
The cross-track shrug collection, now four deep: German \textbf{es geht} (``it
goes''), French \textbf{comme ci, comme \c{c}a} (``like this, like that''),
Spanish \textbf{m\'{a}s o menos} (``more or less'') or \textbf{regular},
Italian \textbf{cos\`{\i} cos\`{\i}} (``so so'').
\end{culture}

\section{The whole exchange}
\label{lesson:ch3-practice}

No new words --- this chapter's atoms (\emph{danke}, \emph{bitte},
\emph{gehen}, \emph{wie geht's}, \emph{es geht}) assemble into a real exchange,
formal and informal, picking up right where the introduction of
Chapter~\ref{ch:introductions} left off.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Formal} (a stranger, an elder):

\begin{center}\itshape
--- Guten Tag. Wie geht es Ihnen?\\
--- Danke, gut. Und Ihnen?\\
--- Es geht.\\
--- [after a small favour] --- Danke! --- Bitte.
\end{center}

\noindent\textbf{Informal} (a friend):

\begin{center}\itshape
--- Hallo! Wie geht's?\\
--- Gut, danke, und dir?\\
--- So lala.
\end{center}

\begin{grammarlens}
The glue is \emph{und dir?} / \emph{und Ihnen?} --- ``and you?'' (\emph{und}
$=$ ``and,'' cognate of English \textbf{and}), the German twin of Spanish
\emph{\textquestiondown y t\'{u}?} and French \emph{et toi?}. Note that it
takes the \textbf{dative} (\emph{dir} / \emph{Ihnen}), because it is short for
``how goes it \emph{to you}?''
\end{grammarlens}

\noindent
\begin{tabular}{@{}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.30\textwidth}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.63\textwidth}@{}}
\toprule
Built this chapter & \\
\midrule
\emph{danke} & thanks ($\leftarrow$ \emph{denken}, ``to think''; English
\emph{thank} $=$ \emph{think}) \\
\emph{bitte} & please / you're welcome ($\leftarrow$ \emph{bitten}, ``to
ask''; English \emph{bid}, \emph{bead}) \\
\emph{gehen} / \emph{geht} & ``to go,'' the state-verb ($=$ English \emph{go})
\\
\emph{Wie geht es dir?}\slash{} \emph{Ihnen?} & how are you --- informal / formal \\
\emph{gut}\slash{} \emph{es geht}\slash{} \emph{so lala}\slash{} \emph{nicht so gut} & the answer
ladder \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}

Which verb carries German's ``how are you?'', and what English word is it?
\emph{gehen} $=$ \emph{go}. Which two of our three tracks use ``to go,'' and
which uses ``to stand''? Go: German and French; stand: Spanish. Next chapter:
farewells --- \emph{auf Wiedersehen}, \emph{tsch\"{u}ss}, \emph{bis bald}.
