# nāvis, nāvis — a ship

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[LA-LEX-C285-ITER-FACIO-02, LA-LEX-C100-VERBA6-02, LA-LEX-C73-HOMINES2-04] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say "I am making a journey". (*Iter faciō.*) Then "to sail", and
"a sailor". (*Nāvigāre. Nauta.*)

[PAUSE 1s] A long Roman journey often went by sea. The word for what the
sailor sails has been hiding inside *nāvigāre* all along.

## You'll want to know: nāvis, nāvis
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[LA-LEX-C286-NAVIS-01]; assesses=[LA-LEX-C153-QUAL153-04, LA-LEX-C156-ACT156-05] -->

**nāvis, nāvis** — "a ship". It is feminine, and its accusative is
**nāvem**.

> **In nāvem ascendō.** — I go on board the ship.
> **Nāvis solvit.** — The ship sets sail.

[PAUSE 2s] You own **ascendō**, "to climb, to go up", and Romans went *up*
into a ship. And you own **solvō**, "to untie": a ship that *solvit* has
cast off its ropes from the shore.

## The word, taken apart: nāvis
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[LA-LEX-C45-WORLD-05] -->

**nāvis** is inherited, usually traced to Proto-Indo-European *\*néh₂us*,
"boat" (EtymDB 2.1). Greek *naus*, "ship", is a cousin (Lewis & Short set
them side by side). From the Latin come English *naval*, *navy* and the
*nave* of a church, and *nāvigō* is *nāvis* with *agō*, "to drive"
(Lewis & Short): to drive a ship.
