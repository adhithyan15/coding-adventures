# ero contento — how it felt

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[IT-LEX-ERA-01, IT-GRAMMAR-SCENA-EVENTO-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say "I was tired, but I was happy". (*Ero stanco, ma ero contento*,
or *ero stanca, ma ero contenta*.)

[PAUSE 1s] You said that sentence in chapter two hundred and sixty-three. This
lesson is about why it worked.

## Grammar Lens: a feeling is scene
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[IT-GRAMMAR-SENTIMENTO-PASSATO-01]; assesses=[IT-LEX-C43-EMOZIONI-04, IT-LEX-C43-EMOZIONI-05, IT-LEX-C218-QUAL218-03] -->

You already own the feelings: **contento**, **felice**, **triste**,
**stanco**, **nervoso**. Describing an experience is putting them in the past,
and the past they want is the imperfetto:

> *Prima dell'esame **ero nervoso**. Poi **ero felice**.*
> — Before the exam I was nervous. Then I was happy.

A feeling has no edge. It was going on, like the weather, so in a story it is
usually scene: **ero**. *Sono stato felice* exists too, for a feeling summed
up as one finished stretch: *sono stato felice a Roma*, the whole stay.

And the adjective agrees with whoever felt it. *Contento* is a regular
four-ending adjective: **contento**, **contenta**, **contenti**, **contente**.
*Felice* and *triste* end in *-e* and have only two: **felice** for one,
**felici** for more, man or woman.

> *Eravamo contenti.* — We were happy.
> *Mia madre era felice.* — My mother was happy.

## Grammar Lens: contento di
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[IT-GRAMMAR-CONTENTO-DI-02]; assesses=[IT-LEX-C77-AGG6-05, IT-LEX-C92-VIAGGIO92-05] -->

To say **what** you were happy about, add **di**:

> *Ero contento **del** viaggio.* — I was pleased with the journey.
> *Sono felice **di** essere qui.* — I'm happy to be here.

*di* + *il* is **del**, the same welding as *a* + *il* in *al*. Before an
infinitive, *di* stays as it is.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[] -->

**contento** comes from Latin *contentus*, "satisfied", the participle of
*continēre*, "to hold together, to contain", itself *con-* + *tenēre*, "to
hold". Lewis & Short, under *contineo*, tie *contentus* to the verb's sense
"to hold back, to keep in check": to be content is to hold yourself in, wanting
nothing further. English kept both senses apart: the **content** of a
box, and a **content** person. EtymDB 2.1 links the Italian word to
*contentus*, though it also mixes in a different Latin word, *contemptus*,
"scorn", which is not its source.
