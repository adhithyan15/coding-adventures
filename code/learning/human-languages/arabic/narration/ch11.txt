Arabic, chapter 11: Head and Hand.
3 lessons. All 3 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 3.
رأس (raʾs) — the head.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
One more ancient Semitic root — and a word still hard at work in modern political vocabulary, the same way Latin's caput is in English.

You'll want to know — The word.
رأس (raʾs) = "head" — an ancient Semitic root, cousin of Hebrew ראש (rosh), the very word inside Rosh Hashanah, "head of the year."

Why it's said this way — From "head" to "president".
Arabic builds رئيس (raʾīs), "president, chief, head [of an organization]," directly on this same root — literally "the one who is the head." That's exactly the move English makes with Latin caput: captain ("head" of a unit), capital ("head" city). Different language families, the same very human idea — the person or place "at the head" is the one in charge.

[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: "raʾīs" — president, literally "the head [one]"]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: the Hebrew cousin — "raʾs / rosh"]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
What does رأس mean, and what's its Hebrew cousin? ("Head" — cousin of rosh, as in Rosh Hashanah.) What word is built on it for "president," and what does it literally mean? (رئيس raʾīs, "the head [one]" — the same "head = leader" idea as English captain/capital from Latin caput.)


Lesson 2 of 3.
يد (yad) — the hand.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Some words wear their age lightly — worn down, reshaped, disguised. This one hasn't changed much at all.

You'll want to know — The word.
يد (yad) = "hand" — a short, ancient Semitic root. Its Hebrew cousin is spelled and pronounced almost identically: יד (yad). Where raʾs/rosh show a family resemblance, yad/yad is closer to twins — one of the more strikingly unchanged shared words across the whole Semitic family.

Lesson 3 of 3.
How are you, cold — the wellbeing exchange run cold — a question that asks after your state rather than after you, a root that means to turn, and two answers where English has one.

Warm-up.
[pause 4 seconds]
Ask after somebody and answer them back, both parts, without reading on. If the question came out as a memorised noise rather than three pieces you can name, this page is for you.

The two words.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
word: كيف kayfa. letters, right to left: kāf, yāʾ, fāʾ. what it is: how — a question word, partner to ما.
word: حال ḥāl. letters, right to left: ḥāʾ, alif, lām. what it is: state, condition — root ḥ–w–l, to turn.
The ح is the deep throat ḥ, not an English h. And حال is the whole point of the question: English asks after you, Arabic asks after your state — the thing that turns and changes, which is exactly what its root means.

Grammar Lens: the same suffix, one lesson on.
كيف حالك؟ bolts three pieces you already own together: how + state + your. The suffix behaves identically to the one that gave you اسمي, and it splits the same way.
[a table of 1 row, read aloud]
to a man: كيف حالك؟ kayfa ḥāluka. to a woman: كيف حالك؟ kayfa ḥāluki.
Literally: how — your-state. No is, no are. The same zero copula that ran through the name exchange runs through this one.

How to answer: two replies where English has one.
بخير bi-khayr is بـ (in) plus خير (khayr, good) — literally in goodness. You had met khayr long before you were told: it sits inside the morning greeting صباح الخير, morning of the good.
الحمد لله al-ḥamdu lillāh comes back more often than bi-khayr does. Every letter in it is one you can write — alif, lām, ḥāʾ, mīm, dāl, hāʾ — and it says the praise is to God, from the root ḥ–m–d, to praise.

The exchange.
[your turn — say: ask a man — kayfa ḥāluka?]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: ask a woman — kayfa ḥāluki?]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: the plain answer — bi-khayr, shukran]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: the commoner answer — al-ḥamdu lillāh]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 4 seconds]
