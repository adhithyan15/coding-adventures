Spanish, chapter 429: The Instituto, And What A Mark Is Called.
7 lessons. You can do the first 2 of them in the car; after that you will want to have stopped.


Lesson 1 of 7.
el instituto — the building between the two you already know.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
You can say la escuela and la universidad. A Spanish fifteen-year-old goes to neither. Where do they go?

You'll want to know: el instituto.
el instituto — the secondary school, for students of about twelve to eighteen.
On a sign it is often shortened to IES — Instituto de Educación Secundaria.

The word, taken apart.
Latin instituere — in- plus statuere, "to set up, to stand a thing upright." An institutum was something that had been set up: a custom, an arrangement, a foundation. A Spanish instituto is a thing that was established, and the school sense is one narrow use of a very wide word.
Statuere is behind a crowd of words you can already half-read: estatua, estado, estatura, constituir. Every one of them is about standing.

Grammar Lens: the false friend, and the ladder it breaks.
English institute names a research body — the Institute of Physics. Spanish instituto can mean that too, but its everyday sense is the school, and that is the one a reading paper uses.
The three buildings in order:
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
age: about 6 to 12. building: la escuela.
age: about 12 to 18. building: el instituto.
age: after 18. building: la universidad.
Without the middle rung the ladder has a gap in exactly the years an exam candidate is most likely to be asked about.
The stress is ins-ti-TU-to, on the second-last syllable, and no accent is written because a word ending in a vowel is stressed there by default.

Lesson 2 of 7.
la nota — a mark, in the oldest sense of the word.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
You can say el examen. Now try to say what you got for it, and notice you have the event and not the result.

You'll want to know: la nota.
la nota — the mark, the grade.
¿Qué nota tienes? — "What mark did you get?" In Spain the answer is a number out of ten, and five is where passing begins.

The word, taken apart.
Latin nota meant a mark scratched onto something — a sign cut into wax, wood or skin so it could be told apart later.
Every modern sense grew out of that one scratch:
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
word: nota. the mark in it: the mark a teacher puts on your work.
word: notar. the mark in it: to notice — to catch the mark.
word: notable. the mark in it: worth marking.
English note, notice, notable and notorious are the same word arriving by a different road.
So the grade sense is not a metaphor. It is the literal one, and a written note — a mark made on paper to be read later — is the extension.

Grammar Lens: one word, two senses, and how to tell them apart.
You have met nota before, on a message left for somebody. Both senses are live, and what sorts them is the company the word keeps:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
what stands nearby: examen, clase, a number. which sense: the mark.
what stands nearby: dejar, escribir, a person. which sense: the message.
Sacar buena nota is the set phrase for doing well — literally "to take out a good mark," the way you take out anything you have earned.

Lesson 3 of 7.
la calificación — the word the paper uses when the person would say nota.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, because the lesson points at something written down. You can listen to all of it now and come back to the parts that need looking at once you have stopped.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say nota. Now imagine it printed at the top of an official column on a school document, and expect a longer word.

You'll want to know: la calificación.
la calificación — the grade, in its formal dress.
Spoken between students it is nota. Printed on a report, a transcript or a form, it is calificación, and the two name the same thing.

The word, taken apart.
Medieval Latin qualificare, built from qualis ("of what kind") plus facere ("to make"). To calificar is to say what kind a thing is.
That is a startlingly honest description of what a grade does. A number out of ten is a claim about the kind of work in front of the reader, which is why the formal word for it is built from the question of what kind?
English took the same compound as qualify, and kept the other half of the sense: to qualify a statement is to say what kind of statement it is.

Grammar Lens: where the accent sits, and why.
Calificación ends in -n, which by default pulls the stress to the second-last syllable — ca-li-fi-CA-cion. The stress is on the last one, so an accent has to be written: -ción.
Every Spanish word ending in -ción works this way, and you have met dozens:
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
word: calificación. stressed syllable: -ción.
word: educación. stressed syllable: -ción.
word: información. stressed syllable: -ción.
In the plural the accent disappears — calificaciones — because adding a syllable puts the stress where the default would have put it anyway.

Lesson 4 of 7.
aprobar — to pass, and who is really doing the approving.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, because the lesson points at something written down. You can listen to all of it now and come back to the parts that need looking at once you have stopped.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
You can name the exam and the mark. Say what you did with the exam when the mark was a five or better.

You'll want to know: aprobar.
aprobar — to pass, as in to pass an exam.
He aprobado el examen. — "I have passed the exam."
The mark that does it is a five out of ten and upward.

The word, taken apart.
Latin approbare — ad- ("towards") plus probare, "to test something and find it good."
Look at the direction that gives you. English says I passed the exam, with the student doing the acting and the exam standing still. The Latin behind aprobar says something closer to the examiner tested the work and found it good — the judgement travels the other way.
Probare is a generous root, and you can read its family off the page:
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
word: probar. what it tests: to try, to taste, to test.
word: prueba. what it tests: a test, a piece of evidence.
word: aprobar. what it tests: to test and find good.
English probe, proof, prove and approve all come from the same verb.

Grammar Lens: the vowel that breaks in the middle.
Aprobar is one of the verbs whose o splits into ue when the stress lands on it:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
form: yo apruebo. stem: stressed.
form: nosotros aprobamos. stem: unstressed.
You have seen this before in poder and contar. The rule has not changed: the stem breaks where the stress falls and stays whole where it does not.

Lesson 5 of 7.
suspender — to fail, and the picture the word is holding.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
You can say aprobar. Guess its opposite, and then read on to see what the Spanish word is actually picturing.

You'll want to know: suspender.
suspender — to fail, as in to fail an exam or a subject.
He suspendido el examen. — "I have failed the exam." A mark below five does it.

The word, taken apart.
Latin suspendere — sub- ("beneath") plus pendere, "to hang." To suspend a thing is to leave it hanging.
That is not a dead metaphor here. A failed subject in Spain is not finished: it hangs over the student until a resit in a later term. There is even a word for one — una asignatura pendiente, "a hanging subject" — which people also use for anything in life they have been putting off.
Pendere built a shelf of words that all hang:
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
word: pendiente. what hangs: a slope, an earring, a thing outstanding.
word: depender. what hangs: to hang from something else.
word: pensar. what hangs: to weigh a thing in the mind.
English pendant, pending, suspense and depend hang from the same nail.

Grammar Lens: the English false friend, and the Spanish one.
English suspend keeps only the harsher senses — a suspended player, a suspended sentence. The school sense does not survive the crossing, so an English speaker reading he suspendido will hear something far more dramatic than what happened.
The pair, side by side:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
mark: five or above. verb: aprobar.
mark: below five. verb: suspender.
Both verbs take the exam as their object, so the exam is what is passed or failed, rather than the student.

Lesson 7 of 7.
Synthesis — reading a report card.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, because the lesson points at something written down. You can listen to all of it now and come back to the parts that need looking at once you have stopped.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say the two words for a mark. One of them is about to appear as a column heading.

Reading: el boletín de calificaciones.
IES Ejemplo Boletín de calificaciones.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
clase: Matemáticas. calificación: 7.
clase: Historia. calificación: 4.
clase: Inglés. calificación: 9.
Three rows, and everything in them is now readable. IES is the school. Calificación heads the column because this is a printed document. The numbers are out of ten.
A real letterhead carries the centre's own name after those three letters — usually a poet, a scientist or a place. Ejemplo stands in for it here, so nothing on this page points at a school somebody actually attends. What you read off a real document is the same three things: the IES, the heading, and the column.

How to answer.
Two of the three rows passed and one did not. Say which verb goes with each:
He aprobado Matemáticas e Inglés. He suspendido Historia.
[pause 4 seconds]
The history row is the one that hangs. The student will carry it into the next term as una asignatura pendiente, and the family will ask about it in exactly those words.

The exchange.
— ¿Qué notas has sacado? — He aprobado casi todo. Historia no.
The question uses notas because it is spoken. The answer names the one that did not pass, which is what the question is really asking about.
