Lesson 1 of 6.
关机 (guānjī) — to switch off (a phone or a computer).

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Mandarin for straight on, then the Mandarin for route.

You'll want to know: 关机.
关机 — guānjī — "to switch off (a phone or a computer)".
我的手机关机了 wǒ de shǒujī guānjī le — My phone is switched off.
The new character, in pieces: 关 (guān) — six strokes: two short strokes leaning toward each other, then 天 underneath. The next lesson puts it in your hand.

The word, taken apart.
关机 (guānjī) is built from 关 (guān) 'to shut' and 机 'machine', both inherited from Middle Chinese, kwaen and kj+j (Guangyun, in Baxter's transcription). The dictionary glosses the first as the bar that shuts a door, and explains the second as the catch that sets off a crossbow.

Lesson 2 of 6.
关 (guān) — two short strokes over 天.

Before we start: this one needs your hands, so it is not a driving lesson. You will want a pen and something to write on and your eyes, for letter shapes on the page. Script — two short strokes over 天.
关 (guān).
Two pieces, top before bottom.
The top piece, two strokes. A dot on the left, falling to the lower right, then a short stroke on the right, falling to the lower left: the two lean toward each other.
The bottom piece, 天, four strokes: a bar, a longer bar, a long stroke falling to the left, and a long stroke falling to the right.
2 + 4 = six.

[once you have stopped driving — this part needs your eyes or your hands; if you are driving, skip ahead to the next part]
Guided Practice.
[once you have stopped driving — write: 关 (guān), all six, with the model in front of you]
[once you have stopped driving — cover: write it again from memory, and count the pieces, not the strokes]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
How many strokes in 关 (guān)? (Six.) Which character is the bottom piece? (天.) Which way do the two top strokes lean? (Toward each other.)

Lesson 4 of 6.
liúyán — to leave a message.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Mandarin for to switch off, then the Mandarin for to turn a phone on.

You'll want to know: liúyán.
liúyán — "to leave a message".
tā bú zài nǐ kěyǐ liúyán — He isn't in, but you can leave a message.
Its characters come later; for now it is a word for the ear.

The word, taken apart.
liúyán is built from liú 'to leave behind' and yán 'words', both inherited from Middle Chinese, ljuw and ngjon (Guangyun, in Baxter's transcription), which glosses the first 'to stay' and 'to stop', and the second 'speech'.

Lesson 5 of 6.
zhànxiàn — engaged; busy (of a phone line).

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Mandarin for to turn a phone on, then the Mandarin for to leave a message.

You'll want to know: zhànxiàn.
zhànxiàn — "engaged; busy (of a phone line)".
tā de diànhuà yìzhí zhànxiàn — His line has been engaged the whole time.
Its characters come later; for now it is a word for the ear.

The word, taken apart.
zhànxiàn is built from zhàn 'to take up' and xiàn 'a line'. The second is inherited from Middle Chinese sjenH (Guangyun, in Baxter's transcription), glossed 'thread'; the dictionary lists the first only with another sense, so how it came to mean taking up is not settled.
