# er hat — "he has"

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GE-GRAMMAR-HABEN-DU-HAST-01, GE-LEX-ER-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] One more break, and then the verb behaves for good. The machine says
*-t* on *hab-*.

## You'll want to know: er hat
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[GE-GRAMMAR-HABEN-ER-HAT-01]; assesses=[GE-GRAMMAR-THIRD-PERSON-T-03, GE-LEX-SIE-SHE-02] -->

> **er hat** — "he has." Also *sie hat*, "she has," and *es hat*, "it has."

The rule predicts *hab-t*. German says **hat**: the same *b* gone, the same
vowel shortened. One syllable, rhyming with English *hut* rather than *hat* —
the German *a* sits further back than the English one.

One form covers *er*, *sie* and *es*, exactly as the third-person *-t* always
does. So the two breaks in this verb are neighbours: *du* **hast**, *er*
**hat**, and every other form is the machine.
