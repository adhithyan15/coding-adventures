Hindi, chapter 92: Whose, And What A Postposition Does To It.
8 lessons. You can do the first 6 of them in the car; after that you will want to have stopped.


Lesson 1 of 8.
uskā — one word for his, her and its.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
You have had merā, merī, mere since the second chapter. Say all three. You have been able to say whose something is for exactly one person: your own.

You'll want to know: uskā.
uskā — "his," "her," or "its."
Hindi does not choose between those three, and the reason is the point of the lesson: the possessive agrees with the thing owned, not with the owner.

Grammar Lens: English agrees with the owner, Hindi with the thing.
This is the single biggest difference between the two systems, and it runs the opposite way from what an English speaker expects:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
English his / her. what decides the form: the owner's gender.
Hindi uskā / uskī. what decides the form: the owned thing's gender.
So uskā before a masculine noun and uskī before a feminine one — and whether the owner is a man or a woman changes nothing at all. A sentence with uskī kitāb tells you the book is feminine and tells you nothing about whose it is.
This is not a new rule. It is the -आ agreement you already have, doing its ordinary job: uskā ends in -आ, so it bends like every other -आ word in the book.

[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: uskā — his, her, or its]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: the form before a feminine noun (uskī)]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: merā and uskā one after the other, and hear the same ending]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: what uskī tells you about the owner (nothing)]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Does uskā mean his or her? (Either — Hindi does not distinguish.) What decides between uskā and uskī? (The gender of the thing owned.)


Lesson 2 of 8.
हमारा (hamārā) — our, and the places Hindi prefers it to "my".

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say merā and uskā. Both end the same way and bend the same way, so the next word brings no new grammar — only a new owner.

You'll want to know: हमारा.
हमारा (hamārā) — "our."
Built on हम (ham), "we," which you have met. Before a feminine noun it is hamārī; before a plural or an oblique it is hamāre, exactly as merā behaves.

Grammar Lens: where Hindi says "our" and English says "my".
The grammar is easy; the usage is the thing worth carrying:
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
talking about: the house you grew up in. English habit: my house. Hindi habit: hamārā ghar.
talking about: your family. English habit: my family. Hindi habit: hamārā parivār.
talking about: your country. English habit: my country. Hindi habit: hamārā deś.
A Hindi speaker describing their family will very often say hamārā parivār where an English speaker says my family, because the thing is understood as belonging to the household rather than to the speaker. Neither is wrong in either language — but the preference is real, and a listening paper will play you the Hindi one.

[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
What pronoun is hamārā built on? (Ham, we.) Which of uskā and hamārā tells you something about the owner? (Hamārā — it says the owner is a group including the speaker.)


Lesson 3 of 8.
तुम्हारा (tumhārā) — your, at the middle level of three.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say hamārā. You have had āpkā for your since early on. This lesson gives you the other one, and says when each is right.

You'll want to know: तुम्हारा.
तुम्हारा (tumhārā) — "your," said to someone you are familiar with.
Built on तुम (tum), the middle of Hindi's three yous.

Grammar Lens: three levels, and the possessive that goes with each.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
pronoun: आप āp. possessive: āpkā. who: a stranger, an elder, anyone respected.
pronoun: तुम tum. possessive: tumhārā. who: a friend, a sibling, a younger colleague.
pronoun: तू tū. possessive: terā. who: intimate, or rude — rarely the right choice for a learner.
The safe default for a learner is आप, and the reason is asymmetric: using āp with a close friend sounds a little formal, while using tum with a stranger or an elder sounds rude. One error is a small stiffness; the other is an offence.
But tumhārā is not optional to know. A listening paper is full of friends talking to each other, and it is the form they use.

Lesson 4 of 8.
अपना (apnā) — the word that takes over when the owner is the subject.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say merā and tumhārā. Now try to say "I do my work" in Hindi using merā, and read on to find out why that sentence is wrong.

You'll want to know: अपना.
अपना (apnā) — "one's own."
When the owner of a thing is the subject of the same sentence, Hindi replaces the ordinary possessive with apnā. This is a requirement rather than a preference:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
wrong, maiṅ merā kām kartā hūṅ.
right, maiṅ apnā kām kartā hūṅ.
Both would be "I do my work." The first is what an English speaker builds, and a Hindi ear hears it as a mistake straight away.

Grammar Lens: one word covering every person.
The saving is large, because apnā does not care who the subject is:
[a table of 4 rows, read aloud]
subject: I. English: my own. Hindi: apnā.
subject: you. English: your own. Hindi: apnā.
subject: he or she. English: their own. Hindi: apnā.
subject: we. English: our own. Hindi: apnā.
Four English possessives, one Hindi word. It still agrees with the thing owned in the ordinary way — apnā kām, apnī kitāb, apne dost — because it ends in -आ like all the others.
And it removes a real ambiguity. Vah uskī kitāb paṛhtā hai means he reads somebody else's book; vah apnī kitāb paṛhtā hai means he reads his own. English he reads his book cannot tell those apart without more words.

[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: the form before a plural or an oblique (apne)]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
When does apnā replace the ordinary possessive? (When the owner is the subject of the sentence.) Whose book is uskī kitāb when the subject is he? (Somebody else's.)


Lesson 5 of 8.
मेरे पास (mere pās) — Hindi has no word for "have".

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say merā, merī, mere. You have had all three since the second chapter. This lesson shows you that the third one has a second job nobody mentioned.

You'll want to know: मेरे पास.
मेरे पास (mere pās) — "I have." Literally: near me.
पास (pās) means "near, at hand." Hindi has no verb meaning to have, so possession is built out of location:
मेरे पास एक किताब है। mere pās ek kitāb hai — "Near me there is a book" = I have a book.
The thing owned is the subject of that sentence, which is why hai agrees with kitāb and not with the speaker.

Grammar Lens: mere is not the plural here.
This is the part that repays attention. You learned mere as the plural of merā. It is also the oblique — the form a word takes in front of a postposition — and the two happen to look identical:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
phrase: mere dost. why mere: plural — my friends.
phrase: mere pās. why mere: oblique — pās is a postposition.
So mere pās is not "my nears." It is merā bending because something stands after it. Every -आ possessive does this: uskā becomes uske pās, hamārā becomes hamāre pās, apnā becomes apne pās.
That single habit — -आ becomes -ए in front of a postposition — is worth more than the phrase itself, because it governs every postposition in the language.

Lesson 6 of 8.
मेरे लिए (mere liye) — the same bend, in front of a different postposition.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say mere pās. Say why it is mere and not merā. The word in this lesson works the same way, which is the whole point of putting them together.

You'll want to know: मेरे लिए.
मेरे लिए (mere liye) — "for me." mere sāth — "with me."
Three phrases, one shape. The possessive goes oblique and the postposition follows:
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
mere pās means near me — I have.
mere liye means for me.
mere sāth means with me.

Grammar Lens: the pattern generalises to every person.
Once the bend is automatic, the whole grid follows without memorising a single new form:
[a table of 4 rows, read aloud]
owner: I. oblique: mere. with liye: mere liye.
owner: he, she. oblique: uske. with liye: uske liye.
owner: we. oblique: hamāre. with liye: hamāre liye.
owner: the subject's own. oblique: apne. with liye: apne liye.
The last row is the one both practice papers reach for. Phrases like apne dost ke sāth — "with one's own friend" — put three of this chapter's ideas in four words: the reflexive possessive, the oblique bend, and a two-word postposition.

[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]

Lesson 7 of 8.
pā̃ch mālik, ek niyam — five owners, and one bend that covers them all.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, because the lesson points at something written down. You can listen to all of it now and come back to the parts that need looking at once you have stopped.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Name as many possessives as you can before reading the table. Aim for five.

Grammar Lens: the whole grid.
Every one of these ends in -आ, so every one bends the same way:
[a table of 6 rows, read aloud]
owner: my. masculine: merā. feminine: merī.
owner: your (respectful). masculine: āpkā. feminine: āpkī.
owner: your (familiar). masculine: tumhārā. feminine: tumhārī.
owner: his, her, its. masculine: uskā. feminine: uskī.
owner: our. masculine: hamārā. feminine: hamārī.
owner: the subject's own. masculine: apnā. feminine: apnī.
Six forms in the first column and not one of them is irregular. The agreement is with the thing owned throughout, which is why nothing in the middle column tells you anything about the owner.

Grammar Lens: the bend, and the two jobs of -ए.
Put a postposition after any of them and the -आ becomes -ए:
[a table of 4 rows, read aloud]
direct: merā. before a postposition: mere pās.
direct: uskā. before a postposition: uske liye.
direct: hamārā. before a postposition: hamāre ghar meṅ.
direct: apnā. before a postposition: apne dost ke sāth.
The form -ए therefore has two jobs, and the sentence tells you which:
mere dost — no postposition follows, so this is the plural: my friends.
mere pās — a postposition follows, so this is the oblique: near me.
Nothing in the spelling distinguishes them. What comes next does.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: all six possessives in the masculine, then all six in the feminine]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: the one that replaces the others when the owner is the subject (apnā)]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: mere pās, uske pās, hamāre pās in a run]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: which job -ए is doing in mere dost, and which in mere pās]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
What does a possessive agree with — the owner or the thing owned? (The thing owned.) What are the two jobs of the -ए ending? (Plural, and oblique before a postposition.)


Lesson 8 of 8.
आपके पास क्या है? (āpke pās kyā hai?) — asking what somebody has, without a verb for having.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say mere pās ek kitāb hai. Now turn it into a question addressed to somebody you would call āp, and notice that āpkā will have to bend.

Grammar Lens: the question bends too.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
question. sentence: आपके पास क्या है? (āpke pās kyā hai?)
answer. sentence: मेरे पास (mere pās) एक किताब है।.
Āpkā becomes āpke for the same reason merā becomes mere: पास stands after it. The question and the answer are the identical construction with the owner swapped.
There is no verb for have anywhere in either line. है is the ordinary copula, and it is agreeing with kitāb.

Reading.
Read aloud. Every word has been taught:
— आपके पास क्या है? (āpke pās kyā hai?) — मेरे पास (mere pās) एक किताब है। — तुम्हारे पास क्या है? — मेरे पास (mere pās) एक कलम है। — हमारे घर में क्या है? — हमारे घर में सब है।.
Six lines, three different owners, and the same bend in every one of them: āpke, tumhāre, hamāre — every possessive ending in -ए because a postposition stands after it.

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Which word in mere pās ek kitāb hai means have? (None — the construction is near me there is.) Why is it āpke pās rather than āpkā pās? (Pās is a postposition, so the possessive goes oblique.)
