# estar en paro — the halt, rather than the missing job

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-TRABAJO] -->

[PAUSE 2s] You can say *el trabajo*. Try to say that somebody has none, and
notice English reaches for a word built on *employ* that Spanish does not use
in conversation.

## You'll want to know: estar en paro
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-C430-VIDA-01]; assesses=[] -->

**estar en paro** — to be unemployed, to be out of work.

*Mi hermano está en paro.* — "My brother is out of work."

**El paro** on its own is both the state and the benefit paid during it.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C430-VIDA-01] -->

**El paro** is built straight off **parar**, "to stop" — so it is a **stoppage**.
Spanish names the situation after **the halt**, where English names it after the
missing employment.

The verb has a startling history. Latin **parare** meant **to make ready** — the
root inside *preparar* and *reparar*. The sense travelled in steps: *to get
ready* became *to stand ready*, and *to stand ready* became **to stand still**.

| word | where on the journey |
|---|---|
| pre**para**r | to make ready beforehand |
| **para**r | to stand still |
| el **paro** | the standing still itself |

So *estar en paro* is, underneath, **being in the halt**.

## Grammar Lens: which verb, and which country
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C430-VIDA-01, ES-LEX-TRABAJO] -->

It takes **estar** rather than **ser**, because it names a situation rather than
a person, and situations change.

| where | the everyday word |
|---|---|
| Spain | el paro |
| much of Latin America | el desempleo |

**Desempleo** is the formal word everywhere, and the one a newspaper prints. A
person in Spain talking about their own life says **paro**.

The pair a form will ask you to choose between is short: *tengo trabajo* against
*estoy en paro*.
