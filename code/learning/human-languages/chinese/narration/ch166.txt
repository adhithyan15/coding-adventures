Lesson 1 of 6.
开水 (kāishuǐ) — boiled water; boiling water.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Mandarin for a skirt, then the Mandarin for a scarf.

You'll want to know: 开水.
开水 — kāishuǐ — "boiled water; boiling water".
中午我们吃米饭喝开水 zhōngwǔ wǒmen chī mǐfàn hē kāishuǐ — At midday we have rice and drink boiled water.

The word, taken apart.
开水 (kāishuǐ) is built from 开 'to open', here 'to come to the boil', and 水 'water', both inherited from Middle Chinese, khoj and sywijX (Guangyun, in Baxter's transcription); the boiling sense is not given there.

Lesson 2 of 6.
下午茶 (xiàwǔchá) — afternoon tea (a light meal).

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Mandarin for a scarf, then the Mandarin for boiled water.

You'll want to know: 下午茶.
下午茶 — xiàwǔchá — "afternoon tea (a light meal)".
我们三点吃下午茶 wǒmen sān diǎn chī xiàwǔchá — We have afternoon tea at three.
The new character, in pieces: 茶 (chá) — nine strokes in three pieces: the grass piece of 菜 on top, the two strokes of 人 under it, and at the foot a horizontal, a vertical with a hook, and two short strokes on either side of it. The next lesson puts it in your hand.

The word, taken apart.
下午茶 (xiàwǔchá) is built from 下午 'afternoon' and 茶 (chá) 'tea'. All three parts are inherited from Middle Chinese, haeX, nguX and drae (Guangyun, in Baxter's transcription); the dictionary marks the tea character as a popular form. Tea with something to eat, as a light meal of its own.

Lesson 3 of 6.
茶 (chá) — grass, a roof, a small tree.

Before we start: this one needs your hands, so it is not a driving lesson. You will want a pen and something to write on and your eyes, for letter shapes on the page. [once you have stopped driving — read: each one aloud, with nothing said first — cài, rén]

[once you have stopped driving — this part needs your eyes or your hands; if you are driving, skip ahead to the next part]
Script — grass, a roof, a small tree.
茶 (chá).
Three pieces, top to bottom. Count the pieces, not the strokes.
The grass piece, three strokes, as in 菜: a horizontal, then two short verticals through it.
Then 人, two strokes, spread wide like a roof.
At the foot, four strokes: a horizontal, a vertical with a hook, then a short stroke falling to the left and a dot falling to the right, one on each side.
3 + 2 + 4 = nine.

[once you have stopped driving — this part needs your eyes or your hands; if you are driving, skip ahead to the next part]
Guided Practice.
[once you have stopped driving — write: 茶 (chá), all nine, with the model in front of you]
[once you have stopped driving — cover: write it again from memory, and count the pieces, not the strokes]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
How many strokes in 茶 (chá)? (Nine.) What sits on top? (The grass piece of 菜.) What comes under it? (人, spread like a roof.)

Lesson 4 of 6.

You'll want to know: hóngchá.
hóngchá — "black tea".
nǐ yào hóngchá háishi lǜchá — Black tea or green tea?
Its characters come later; for now it is a word for the ear.
English calls this tea black; Mandarin calls it red.

The word, taken apart.
hóngchá is built from hóng 'red' and 茶 (chá) 'tea', both inherited from Middle Chinese, huwng and drae (Guangyun, in Baxter's transcription).

Lesson 6 of 6.
pàochá — to make tea (to steep the leaves).

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Mandarin for black tea, then the Mandarin for tea leaves.

You'll want to know: pàochá.
pàochá — "to make tea (to steep the leaves)".
wǒ yòng kāishuǐ pàochá — I make the tea with boiling water.
Its characters come later; for now it is a word for the ear.

The word, taken apart.
pàochá is built from pào 'to soak' and 茶 (chá) 'tea', both inherited from Middle Chinese, phaew and drae (Guangyun, in Baxter's transcription). The dictionary glosses the first 'bubbles on water'; soaking is not given there, and phaew would regularly give a first tone, so how pào came about is not settled.
