Italian, chapter 474: Di che umore sei?.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
l'umore — mood.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Italian for sly, then the Italian for to admire.

You'll want to know: l'umore.
l'umore — "mood".
Oggi sono di buon umore. Today I am in a good mood.
Di buon umore — in a good mood; di cattivo umore — in a bad mood.

The word, taken apart.
umore is a learned borrowing from Latin ūmor "moisture, fluid" (EtymDB; Lewis & Short): old medicine linked a person's mood to the fluids of the body.

What you've built.

Lesson 3 of 5.
lunatico — moody.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Italian for mood, then the Italian for calm.

You'll want to know: lunatico.
lunatico — "moody".
Mio fratello è lunatico: un giorno ride, un giorno no. My brother is moody: one day he laughs, the next he does not.
Not like English lunatic: it means moody, full of ups and downs.

The word, taken apart.
lunatico is a learned borrowing from Late Latin lūnāticus "moonstruck" (EtymDB), built on lūna "the moon": moods were thought to follow its phases.

Lesson 4 of 5.
innervosirsi — to get irritated, to get nervous.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Italian for calm, then the Italian for moody.

You'll want to know: innervosirsi.
innervosirsi — "to get irritated, to get nervous".
Mi innervosisco quando il treno è in ritardo. I get irritated when the train is late.
Built on nervoso. An -isc- verb with essere: mi sono innervosito.

The word, taken apart.
innervosirsi is built on nervoso "nervous", from Latin nervōsus "sinewy, vigorous" (Lewis & Short), from nervus "a sinew".

Lesson 5 of 5.
sbuffare — to huff, to puff with impatience.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Italian for moody, then the Italian for to get irritated.

You'll want to know: sbuffare.
sbuffare — "to huff, to puff with impatience".
Il ragazzo sbuffa ogni volta che deve fare i compiti. The boy huffs every time he has to do his homework.
A puff of air through the lips; lo sbuffo is the puff.

The word, taken apart.
sbuffare is built from s- and buffo "a puff of air". The origin of buffo is not settled; it is usually taken to imitate the sound of blowing.
