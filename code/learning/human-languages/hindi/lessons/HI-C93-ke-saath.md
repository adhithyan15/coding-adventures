# के साथ — with, in two words

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[HI-LEX-C84-POSS-01, HI-CONCEPT-C39-DOST-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say *his* and *her* in Hindi, and notice that both were the same
word wearing different endings. Now try to say **with my friend**, and notice
that English needs exactly one word to do it.

## You'll want to know: के साथ
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[HI-LEX-C93-REL-01]; assesses=[] -->

**मैं दोस्त के साथ हूँ।** — I am with a friend.

English spends one word on this. Hindi spends **two**, and only the second one
is new.

## The two words
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[HI-LEX-C93-REL-01, HI-LEX-C84-POSS-01] -->

Take the phrase apart and something familiar falls out:

| piece | what it is |
|---|---|
| दोस्त | the noun |
| के | **उसका's ending**, standing on its own |
| साथ | the new word — *company* |

So **दोस्त के साथ** is, piece by piece, *the friend's company*. Hindi is not
reaching for a preposition at all. It is building a possessive and then naming
the relation as a thing.

That is why the first word is **के** and never **का**: a possessive agrees with
what follows it, and **साथ** is masculine and standing in front of a further
word, so it takes the oblique ending you met on nouns one chapter ago.

## Grammar Lens: a shape, not a word
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[HI-LEX-C93-REL-01, HI-LEX-C84-POSS-04] -->

Write the shape down once, because the rest of this chapter is the same shape
six more times:

| slot | contents |
|---|---|
| 1 | the noun |
| 2 | **के** |
| 3 | the relation word |

**अपने दोस्त के साथ** — *with my own friend* — puts **अपना** in slot 1's
ordinary spot, bent to **अपने** because a postposition is coming. Everything in
front of a postposition bends. That has now been true of possessives, of
pronouns and of nouns, and it is true here.
