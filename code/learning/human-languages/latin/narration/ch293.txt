Latin, chapter 293: Paulātim.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
paulātim — little by little.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for in an instant, then the Latin for immediately.

You'll want to know: paulātim.
paulātim — "little by little".
Paulātim aqua in fossā crēscēbat. Little by little the water in the ditch was rising.
Built on paulum, 'a little'. Caesar writes of people who paulātim get used to something.

The word, taken apart.
paulātim is built on paulum, "a little" (Lewis & Short), with the adverb ending -ātim that is also seen in gradātim: one little bit after another.

Lesson 2 of 5.
sēnsim — gradually, gently.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for immediately, then the Latin for little by little.

You'll want to know: sēnsim.
sēnsim — "gradually, gently".
Senex sēnsim ad forum ambulābat. The old man was walking slowly to the forum.
So gentle that each step is felt; the opposite of repente.

The word, taken apart.
sēnsim is built on sentiō, "to perceive" (Lewis & Short): first "perceptibly", then "gently, little by little", as opposed to what comes suddenly.

Lesson 3 of 5.
gradātim — step by step.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for little by little, then the Latin for gradually.

You'll want to know: gradātim.
gradātim — "step by step".
Gradātim ad summum montem ascendimus. Step by step we climbed to the top of the mountain.
From gradus, 'a step' (taught). Cicero uses it of an argument that moves one point at a time.

The word, taken apart.
gradātim is built on gradus, "step" (EtymDB), with the adverb ending -ātim. Gradus is inherited and goes back to a root meaning "to walk, to step".

Lesson 4 of 5.
paulisper — for a little while.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for gradually, then the Latin for step by step.

You'll want to know: paulisper.
paulisper — "for a little while".
Paulisper in tabernā mānsimus; deinde discessimus. We stayed in the shop for a little while; then we left.
It says how long, briefly. In Plautus a slave begs his master to wait paulisper.

The word, taken apart.
paulisper is built on paulum, "a little", the neuter of paulus, "small", and per, "through" (EtymDB, Lewis & Short): through a small stretch of time.

Lesson 5 of 5.
aliquamdiū — for some time.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for step by step, then the Latin for for a little while.

You'll want to know: aliquamdiū.
aliquamdiū — "for some time".
Aliquamdiū pugnātum est; tum hostēs fūgērunt. There was fighting for some time; then the enemy fled.
aliquam + diū (taught): for a fair while, longer than paulisper. Caesar uses it of battles.

The word, taken apart.
aliquamdiū joins a form of aliquis, "some", with diū, "for a long time" (Lewis & Short): for some length of time.
