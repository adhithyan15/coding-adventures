Italian, chapter 9: Months and Seasons.
2 lessons. Both can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 2.
i mesi — Rome's own months.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Sitting next to Rome, Italian kept the month names closest to Latin of all the sisters — the same procession of gods and Caesars, barely worn. And two things you know pay off: marzo is the war-god of martedì, and settembre– dicembre are the Latin numbers 7–10.

The months, taken apart.
[a table of 12 rows, read aloud]
Italian from Latin: gennaio from Januarius. who / what: Janus, god of beginnings. Spanish twin: enero.
Italian from Latin: febbraio from Februarius. who / what: the Februa purification. Spanish twin: febrero.
Italian from Latin: marzo from Martius. who / what: Mars — the god of martedì! Spanish twin: marzo.
Italian from Latin: aprile from Aprilis. who / what: aperire "to open". Spanish twin: abril.
Italian from Latin: maggio from Maius. who / what: Maia, growth-goddess. Spanish twin: mayo.
Italian from Latin: giugno from Junius. who / what: Juno, queen of gods. Spanish twin: junio.
Italian from Latin: luglio from Julius. who / what: Julius Caesar. Spanish twin: julio.
Italian from Latin: agosto from Augustus. who / what: emperor Augustus. Spanish twin: agosto.
Italian from Latin: settembre from september. who / what: "7th" (septem). Spanish twin: septiembre.
Italian from Latin: ottobre from october. who / what: "8th" (octo). Spanish twin: octubre.
Italian from Latin: novembre from november. who / what: "9th" (novem). Spanish twin: noviembre.
Italian from Latin: dicembre from december. who / what: "10th" (decem). Spanish twin: diciembre.
The Italian tells: gennaio kept the -aio of Januarius, and settembre/ottobre keep the doubled/assimilated look (ottobre, like otto). And two payoffs land:
marzo = Mars = martedì. The month and Tuesday honour the same war-god — Martius and Martedì. One unlocks the other.
settembre…dicembre = 7…10. Still the Latin numbers, because the Roman year began in March; then luglio (Julius) and agosto (Augustus) were inserted and shifted the count.

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Which god links marzo and martedì? (Mars.) Why is dicembre Latin for "ten"? (The Roman year began in March.) Which two months honour real people? (Luglio — Julius; agosto — Augustus.) Next: the seasons.


Lesson 2 of 2.
le stagioni — the four seasons.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
The word for "season," stagione, is itself a small lesson: it's Latin statiō, a "standing" — a fixed station of the year (cousin of English station, stage). And the loveliest of the four, primavera, means the "first green."

The four seasons, taken apart.
[a table of 4 rows, read aloud]
Italian: la primavera (spring). from Latin: prīma vēra. literally: "first spring / first green" (prima "first" + vera from ver "spring").
Italian: l'estate (summer). from Latin: aestas / aestātem. literally: "the heat, the hot season".
Italian: l'autunno (autumn). from Latin: autumnus. literally: "autumn" (twin of English autumn).
Italian: l'inverno (winter). from Latin: hibernum. literally: "the wintry season" (cousin of hibernate).
primavera = prima + vera: the "first spring." Latin vēr was the word for spring; Italian (and Spanish, and Portuguese) built "prima-vera," the year's first season — the same idea French once had in primevère.
estate from aestas, "heat." autunno from autumnus, the same word English borrowed. inverno from hibernum "wintry" — English hibernate is "to spend the inverno."

Guided Practice.

[your turn — say: "la primavera, l'estate, l'autunno, l'inverno"]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: "prima + vera becomes primavera (first green)"; "inverno hibernate"]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: "stagione from statiō — a 'standing' of the year, like station"]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Name the four Italian seasons. (Primavera, estate, autunno, inverno.) What does primavera literally mean? ("First spring / first green.") What Latin word is stagione from, and its English cousin? (Statiō "a standing"; station.) Next chapter builds on this toward family and food.
