You'll want to know: qīzi.
qīzi — "wife".
tā de qīzi shì yīshēng — His wife is a doctor.
Its characters come later; for now it is a word for the ear.

The word, taken apart.
qīzi is built from qī and 子, both inherited from Middle Chinese, tshej and tsiX (Guangyun, in Baxter's transcription), which glosses the first 'equal'. In the word the second loses its tone.

Lesson 2 of 6.
老公 (lǎogōng) — hubby; husband (informal).

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Mandarin for something on your mind, then the Mandarin for a wife.

You'll want to know: 老公.
老公 — lǎogōng — "hubby; husband (informal)".
我老公先回家了 wǒ lǎogōng xiān huí jiā le — My husband has gone home ahead of me.
The new character, in pieces: 老 (lǎo) — six strokes: a short bar, a vertical and a long bar, then a long stroke falling to the left; under them a short stroke falling to the left and a vertical that bends right and hooks up, the right half of 比. The next lesson puts it in your hand.

The word, taken apart.
老公 (lǎogōng) is built from 老 (lǎo) 'old' and 公, both inherited from Middle Chinese, lawX and kuwng (Guangyun, in Baxter's transcription), which glosses the first 'aged' and the second, among other senses, 'a father' and 'an official'. How the pair came to mean a husband is not settled.

Lesson 3 of 6.

Script — a cross and a long fall, over the right half of 比.
老 (lǎo).
Two pieces, top before bottom.
The top piece, four strokes. A short bar. A vertical down through it. A long bar under them. Then a long stroke from the upper right falling all the way to the lower left.
The bottom piece, two strokes, the right half of 比: a short stroke falling to the left, then a vertical that bends to the right along the foot and hooks up.
4 + 2 = six.

[once you have stopped driving — this part needs your eyes or your hands; if you are driving, skip ahead to the next part]
Guided Practice.

[once you have stopped driving — cover: write it again from memory, and count the pieces, not the strokes]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
How many strokes in 老 (lǎo)? (Six.) Which stroke runs across the whole character? (The long fall to the left.) Where have you made the last two strokes? (On the right of 比.)

Lesson 4 of 6.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]

You'll want to know: 外公.
外公 — wàigōng — "grandpa on the mother's side".
外公很热心 wàigōng hěn rèxīn — My grandpa is very warm-hearted.

The word, taken apart.
外公 (wàigōng) is built from 外 'outside' and 公 'a father', both inherited from Middle Chinese, ngwajH and kuwng (Guangyun, in Baxter's transcription), which glosses the second, among other senses, 'a father'.

The word, taken apart.
shūshu is built from shū, said twice, the second time with no tone. It is inherited from Middle Chinese syuwk (Guangyun, in Baxter's transcription), glossed 'a father's youngest brother'.

Lesson 6 of 6.
āyí — auntie; mother's sister.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Mandarin for grandpa on the mother's side, then the Mandarin for an uncle.

You'll want to know: āyí.
āyí — "auntie; mother's sister".
nàge āyí shì wǒ māma de péngyou — That lady is a friend of my mum's.
Its characters come later; for now it is a word for the ear.

The word, taken apart.
āyí is built from ā, a friendly prefix, and yí 'a mother's sister', both inherited from Middle Chinese, 'a and yij (Guangyun, in Baxter's transcription), which glosses the second 'a mother's sister'. The dictionary gives the first only as 'a bend' and 'a hill', so its use before names is not settled.
