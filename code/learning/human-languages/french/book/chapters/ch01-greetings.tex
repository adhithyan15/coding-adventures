\chapter{Greetings}
\label{ch:greetings}
\hlchaptermodality{1}

The French greetings, built from their pieces --- a word for ``good,'' words
for ``day,'' ``evening,'' ``night,'' and the gender machine that decides their
endings. French and Spanish are both daughters of Latin; where a French word
and its Spanish cousin split from one Latin root, both appear here, because the
\emph{difference} between them is often the lesson --- and the text supplies
the Spanish word whether or not you have met it before.

\section{\emph{salut} --- ``hi,'' a wish for your health}
\label{lesson:salut}

\begin{sounds}
Final \textbf{t} is silent: \emph{salut} = \emph{sa-LU}. The \textbf{u} is the
front-rounded French \emph{u}.
\end{sounds}

\begin{cousinweb}
\textbf{salut} (informal ``hi/bye'') $\leftarrow$ Latin \emph{salus, salutem}
(``health, safety''). So \emph{salut} literally wishes \emph{health} --- like
old English ``hail.'' Cousins: \textbf{salut}e, \textbf{salut}ation,
\textbf{salut}ary, \textbf{salu}brious; and via \emph{salvus} (``safe''),
\emph{save}, \emph{salvation}.
\end{cousinweb}

\begin{culture}
Strictly \textbf{informal}, and doubles as ``bye.'' For respect or a first
meeting you reach for \emph{bonjour} --- just as Spanish, the other Latin
daughter, reaches past its own casual \emph{hola} for the fuller \emph{buenos
d\'{i}as}.
\end{culture}

\section{Writing runway I --- notice and trace \emph{salut}}
\label{lesson:salut-observe}
\label{lesson:FR-W01-salut-observe}

No new French yet. Keep \textbf{salut} visible and point to its five written
letters from left to right: \textbf{s-a-l-u-t}. Your voice finishes on
\textbf{u}; the final \textbf{t} stays visible and silent.

Slowly finger-trace \textbf{salut} once, then trace it once in the air. Say
\emph{sa-LU} only after your finger reaches the silent \textbf{t}. This is
noticing, not a penmanship test; use the lower-case forms you normally write.

% canonical-insertion: FR-W01-salut-guided-copy
\section{Writing runway II --- one supported copy}
\label{lesson:salut-guided-copy}
\label{lesson:FR-W01-salut-guided-copy}

Keep \textbf{salut} visible and copy it once. Look back after every letter if
you want. Touch \textbf{s-a-l-u-t} in your copy, then say \emph{sa-LU} without
adding a final \emph{t} sound. Compare only the order and presence of the five
letters. Repair one letter if needed, then stop.

% canonical-insertion: FR-W01-salut-delayed-copy
\section{Writing runway III --- look, hide, write, compare}
\label{lesson:salut-delayed-copy}
\label{lesson:FR-W01-salut-delayed-copy}

Look at \textbf{salut} for five seconds and touch the silent final \textbf{t}.
Then:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item cover the model;
  \item wait five seconds;
  \item write the word once from memory;
  \item uncover \textbf{salut}, compare, and repair only the place that differs.
\end{enumerate}

A repair is part of learning, not a failure. Stop after one memory attempt.

\section{Writing runway IV --- one heard-word dictation}
\label{lesson:salut-dictation}
\label{lesson:FR-W01-salut-dictation}

Cover the answer at the end of this paragraph. Ask someone to say \emph{sa-LU},
or use the narrated edition and listen without looking ahead. Write the familiar
casual greeting from sound alone. Then uncover and compare: \textbf{salut}.
Check its five letters and silent final \textbf{t}; repair one place if needed.

This completes the first trace-to-dictation runway. It is not yet a DILF mock:
later lessons must grow this one known word into contact details, numbers,
forms, and short practical messages.

\section{\emph{bien} --- ``well''}
\label{lesson:bien}

\begin{cousinweb}
\textbf{bien} (``well'') $\leftarrow$ Latin \emph{bene} $\to$ \textbf{bene}fit,
\textbf{bene}volent, \textbf{bene}diction. Spanish, from the same \emph{bene},
kept the very same spelling, \emph{bien} --- the two Romance languages
inherited the adverb almost unchanged.
\end{cousinweb}

A complete one-word answer: \emph{Comment \c{c}a va?} $\to$ \emph{Bien.};
intensified, \emph{tr\`{e}s bien}. The adverb (``well''); the \emph{adjective}
``good'' is a different word, \emph{bon}, next.

\section{\emph{bon} / \emph{bonne} --- ``good''}
\label{lesson:bon}

\begin{sounds}
\textbf{bon} is nasal \emph{b\~{o}} (silent \emph{n}); but \textbf{bonne}
(feminine) is \emph{bun} --- the doubled \emph{nn} cancels the nasal, so the
two \emph{sound} different despite looking alike.
\end{sounds}

\begin{cousinweb}
\textbf{bon / bonne} $\leftarrow$ Latin \emph{bonus / bona} $\to$
\textbf{bon}us, \textbf{bon}anza, \textbf{boun}ty (via \emph{bont\'{e}}),
\emph{bonbon}. Spanish, from the same \emph{bonus / bona}, made \emph{bueno /
buena} --- where French kept the \emph{o} short, Spanish broke it into a
diphthong (\emph{o} $\to$ \emph{ue}).
\end{cousinweb}

\begin{grammarlens}
\emph{bon} \textbf{agrees} with its noun in gender and number ---
\emph{bon} (m.), \emph{bonne} (f.), \emph{bons} (m.pl.), \emph{bonnes} (f.pl.).
This gender-agreement of adjectives is Latin's legacy across the Romance
languages (Spanish runs the same machine on \emph{bueno / buena}). You'll
watch it: \emph{bon jour} (m.) but \emph{bonne nuit} (f.).
\end{grammarlens}

\section{\emph{le} / \emph{la} / \emph{les} --- ``the,'' and the gender it carries}
\label{lesson:le-la}

\begin{cousinweb}
\textbf{le} $\leftarrow$ \emph{ille}, \textbf{la} $\leftarrow$ \emph{illa},
\textbf{les} $\leftarrow$ \emph{illos} --- Latin demonstratives (``that
one/those'') worn down into ``the.'' Spanish, from the very same
\emph{ille / illa / illos}, has \emph{el / la / los}; and those same Latin
demonstratives also gave French \textbf{il} / \textbf{elle} (``he/she'').
(English ``the'' is Germanic --- unrelated.)
\end{cousinweb}

\begin{grammarlens}
Every French noun is masculine or feminine --- Latin's three genders (masculine,
feminine, neuter) collapsed to two, the neuter folding mostly into the
masculine. Learn each noun \emph{with} its article. French has one convenience:
in the plural, both genders share a single article, \emph{les}.
\end{grammarlens}

\section{\emph{jour} --- ``day,'' and the road to English \emph{journey}}
\label{lesson:jour}

\begin{sounds}
\textbf{j} = \emph{zh} (as in \emph{measure}); \textbf{ou} = \emph{oo}. So
\emph{jour} = \emph{zhoor}.
\end{sounds}

\begin{cousinweb}
\textbf{le jour} (masc.) $\leftarrow$ Latin \emph{diurnum} (``daytime''), from
\emph{dies} (``day''). That detour through \emph{diurnum} is why \emph{jour}
looks nothing like Spanish \emph{d\'{i}a} (which took \emph{dies} directly).
Cousins, mostly via French: \textbf{jour}nal, \textbf{jour}ney (a \emph{day's}
travel --- \emph{journ\'{e}e}), so\textbf{journ}, ad\textbf{journ}
(\emph{\`{a} jour}); and via \emph{dies}: \textbf{di}urnal, \textbf{di}ary.\\
\emph{So English \emph{journey} and Spanish \emph{d\'{i}a} are cousins},
meeting at Latin \emph{dies} --- one through French, one direct.
\end{cousinweb}

\begin{grammarlens}
\emph{jour} is \textbf{masculine} (\emph{le jour}). Plural adds a silent
\emph{-s} (\emph{les jours}) --- heard only in \emph{les}.
\end{grammarlens}

\section{\emph{bonjour} --- assembled, and \emph{not} plural}
\label{lesson:bonjour}

\emph{bon} (``good'') $+$ \emph{jour} (``day'') $=$ ``good day,'' one word,
\emph{b\~{o}-zhoor}; \emph{bon} is masculine because \emph{jour} is.

\begin{culture}
The contrast to bank: Spanish says \emph{buenos d\'{i}as} --- \textbf{plural},
the fossil of a blessing. French says \emph{bonjour} --- \textbf{singular},
just ``good day.'' Same two blocks, same roots; Spanish pluralized the
blessing, French didn't. And culturally \emph{bonjour} is near-obligatory ---
opening a shop or a conversation \emph{without} one reads as rude.
\end{culture}

\section{\emph{soir} --- ``evening,'' the same ``late'' Spanish used}
\label{lesson:soir}

\begin{cousinweb}
\textbf{le soir} (masc., \emph{swahr}) $\leftarrow$ Latin \emph{s\={e}rum}
(``the \textbf{late} hour''), from \emph{s\={e}rus} (``late''). Cousin:
\emph{soir\'{e}e}. Spanish, from the same instinct, named its ``afternoon''
\emph{tarde} $\leftarrow$ \emph{tardus}, ``\textbf{late}'' --- two languages,
two ``late'' words, the same idea of naming the later day after its lateness.
\end{cousinweb}

\begin{grammarlens}
\emph{le soir} is \textbf{masculine}; plural \emph{les soirs} (the \emph{-s}
silent, heard only in \emph{les}).
\end{grammarlens}

\section{\emph{bonsoir} --- good evening}
\label{lesson:bonsoir}

\emph{bon} $+$ \emph{soir} (masc.) $=$ ``good evening,'' \emph{b\~{o}-swahr};
\emph{bon} stays masculine. The evening swap for \emph{bonjour}, and --- like
Spanish \emph{buenas noches} --- usable both arriving and leaving.

\section[\emph{nuit}]{\emph{nuit} --- ``night,'' and a sound-change mirroring Spanish}
\label{lesson:nuit}

\begin{cousinweb}
\textbf{la nuit} (fem., \emph{nwee}) $\leftarrow$ Latin \emph{noctem}
(\emph{noct-}) $\to$ \textbf{noct}urnal, equi\textbf{nox}. The change is a
\emph{rule}, side by side with Spanish: Latin \emph{-ct-} $\to$ Spanish
\emph{-ch-}, French \emph{-it-}.
\end{cousinweb}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}llll@{}}
\toprule
Latin & Spanish & French & English \\
\midrule
\emph{noctem} & noche & nuit & night \\
\emph{lactem} & leche & lait & (lact-) \\
\emph{factum} & hecho & fait & fact \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{grammarlens}
\emph{nuit} is \textbf{feminine} (\emph{la nuit}, from feminine Latin
\emph{nox}) --- which is why ``good night'' is \emph{bon\textbf{ne} nuit}.
Plural \emph{les nuits}.
\end{grammarlens}

\section{\emph{bonne nuit} --- good night, and the feminine \emph{bon}}
\label{lesson:bonne-nuit}

\emph{bonne} (fem.) $+$ \emph{nuit} (fem.) $=$ ``good night'' (\emph{bun
nwee}). At last \emph{bon} in its feminine coat --- and the whole agreement
rule in one view:

\begin{center}
\emph{bon\textbf{jour}} (\emph{jour} m.\ $\to$ \emph{bon}) \quad
\emph{bon\textbf{soir}} (\emph{soir} m.\ $\to$ \emph{bon}) \quad
\emph{bon\textbf{ne} nuit} (\emph{nuit} f.\ $\to$ \emph{bonne})
\end{center}

\begin{culture}
Unlike \emph{bonsoir} (an evening greeting), \emph{bonne nuit} is a
\textbf{farewell for bed}. French keeps them separate where Spanish overloads
\emph{buenas noches} for both. Next chapter: introducing yourself, and
French's own \emph{tu} vs.\ \emph{vous}.
\end{culture}

\section{Practice --- choose, say, and write}
\label{lesson:greetings-practice}

Greet a friend casually, then a shopkeeper politely: \emph{salut} /
\emph{bonjour}. Choose the greeting for daytime, evening, and bedtime:
\emph{bonjour}, \emph{bonsoir}, \emph{bonne nuit}. Say why the adjective is
\emph{bon}, \emph{bon}, then \emph{bonne}.

Finally, cover every printed \emph{salut} on these pages. Write the casual
greeting once from memory, uncover it, and check the silent final \textbf{t}.
This closes the chapter's first trace-to-dictation runway without adding a new
word.
