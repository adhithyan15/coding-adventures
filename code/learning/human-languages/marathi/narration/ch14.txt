Lesson 1 of 6.
मित्र (mitra) — a friend, and a very old bond.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, for letter shapes on the page. You can listen to everything else now — leave the section called The letters in this word until you have stopped, and I will say so again when we reach it.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
You can ask for bhākarī now. This chapter turns from what you'd request to who you'd share it with.

You'll want to know: मित्र.
मित्र — mitra — friend (masculine).

The letters in this word.
Two things worth pausing on. ि, the short i vowel sign, is new — written before the consonant it belongs to but said after it, so मि is read mi, not im. And त्र is त joined to र by a halant, the same conjunct mechanism as punhā's न्हा, just a different pair of letters. Mi-tra.

The word, taken apart — a friend, and a covenant before that.
मित्र is a learned borrowing straight from Sanskrit मित्र mitra — taken up whole, not worn down by centuries of sound change the way most everyday words are. Its root is Indo-European mey-, "to bind," and the same word named a god: Avestan Mithra and Old Persian Mithra were divine witnesses to oaths — the bond itself, personified, before the word settled into ordinary Sanskrit as "friend."

Grammar Lens: taken up whole, not worn down.
Sanskrit-descended Marathi words come in two kinds: a tatsama, borrowed directly with its Sanskrit shape mostly intact — मित्र (mitra) is one — and a tadbhava, one that passed through centuries of Prakrit sound change and came out reshaped. भाकरी was neither: it never touched Sanskrit at all. This chapter will show both a tatsama and a tadbhava for family words, so you can hear the difference.

Lesson 3 of 6.
कुटुंब (kutumb) (kuṭumb) — family, and a gender Hindi disagrees with.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, for letter shapes on the page. You can listen to everything else now — leave the section called The letters in this word until you have stopped, and I will say so again when we reach it.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
मित्र (mitra) was taken up whole from Sanskrit, not worn down. This next word was too — and it names the group your friend belongs to as well.

You'll want to know: कुटुंब.
कुटुंब (kutumb) — kuṭumb — family (neuter).

The letters in this word.
One new mark: ु, the short u vowel sign, a small hook under the consonant — the short partner of dūdh's long ू. ट you have informally from marāṭhī; ं the familiar anusvāra. Ku-ṭu-mb.

The word, taken apart — borrowed whole, origin disputed.
कुटुंब (kutumb) is a tatsama, a learned borrowing from Sanskrit कुटुम्ब kuṭumba, "household, family" — like मित्र (mitra), taken up whole rather than worn down by sound change. Unlike मित्र (mitra), its story does not reach a secure Indo-European root: a Dravidian origin for Sanskrit's own kuṭumba has been proposed, but it is not settled, so this lesson names the dispute rather than inventing a resolution.

Grammar Lens: a gender Hindi disagrees with.
The very same Sanskrit word carries two different genders in its two daughters: Marathi's कुटुंब (kutumb) (kuṭumb) is neuter, Hindi's कुटुंब (kutumb) (kuṭumb) is masculine. Both borrowed the identical tatsama; each language then had to assign it a gender inside its own system, and they assigned it differently. A word's gender is not carried in with its meaning — it is decided fresh by whichever language receives it.

Lesson 5 of 6.
भाऊ (bhāū) — brother, and this time the resemblance is real.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, for letter shapes on the page. You can listen to everything else now — leave the section called The letters in this word until you have stopped, and I will say so again when we reach it.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
मित्र (mitra) and कुटुंब (kutumb) (kuṭumb) were both taken up whole from Sanskrit. This next word was not — it was worn down, letter by letter, over centuries.

You'll want to know: भाऊ.
भाऊ — bhāū — brother (masculine).

The letters in this word.
One new letter: ऊ, the independent long ū, used here because a Marathi word cannot stack one vowel mark on top of another — after आ's ा, the second vowel needs its own full letter. भ you have from bheṭū. Bhā-ū.

The word, taken apart — worn down, but the family resemblance survives.
भाऊ (bhāū) is a tadbhava — not borrowed whole like मित्र (mitra) or कुटुंब (kutumb) (kuṭumb), but the ordinary Prakrit-worn descendant of Sanskrit भ्रातृ bhrātṛ, three syllables ground down to two over a thousand years of speech. That root is Indo-European bʰréh₂tēr, and here the family resemblance to English is not a false friend — it is the real thing: brother, Latin frāter, and Sanskrit's own bhrātṛ are all the same word, worn down four different ways by four different languages.

Lesson 6 of 6.
बहीण (bahīṇ) — sister, deliberately not English's cousin.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, for letter shapes on the page. You can listen to everything else now — leave the section called The letters in this word until you have stopped, and I will say so again when we reach it.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Three words so far: mitra, kuṭumb, bhāū. Say them, and say which one is a genuine cousin of an English word. (Bhāū — brother.) Its pair is not so simple.

You'll want to know: बहीण.
बहीण — bahīṇ — sister (feminine).

The letters in this word.
Nothing new. ब from baraṃ, ह from namaskār, ी the familiar long ī, ण from every -णे verb. Ba-hī-ṇ.

The word, taken apart — not sister's cousin.
बहीण (bahīṇ) is a tadbhava, worn down like भाऊ (bhāū): Old Marathi bahiṇī, from Maharastri Prakrit bhaiṇī, from Sanskrit भगिनी bhaginī. There the resemblance to brother's pair ends. Bhaginī is traditionally connected to भज् bhaj-, "to share, to apportion" — a sister as one who shares in the household. That root has nothing to do with Indo-European swésōr, the ancestor of English sister. Two words that translate each other perfectly are, underneath, unrelated.

Grammar Lens: the chapter's whole shape, in two pairs.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
word: मित्र, कुटुंब (kutumb) (mitra, kuṭumb). kind: tatsama. route: taken up whole from Sanskrit.
word: भाऊ, बहीण (bhāū, bahīṇ). kind: tadbhava. route: worn down by Prakrit sound change.
Notice, too, how each pair looks: मित्र (mitra) and कुटुंब (kutumb) (kuṭumb) carry conjuncts and marks a learner has to stop for; भाऊ (bhāū) and बहीण (bahīṇ) do not — plain consonants and vowels, the shape sound change tends to leave behind. And of the tadbhava pair, only भाऊ (bhāū) is a secure English cousin; बहीण (bahīṇ) is not.
