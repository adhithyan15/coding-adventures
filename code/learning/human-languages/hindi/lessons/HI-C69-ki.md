# कि (*ki*) — "that"

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[HI-CONCEPT-C34-SOCHNA-01, HI-GRAMMAR-VERB-FINAL-01] -->

[PAUSE 3s] Retrieve the two items before this one by name: **लेकिन**, which sets
two clauses against each other, and the rule that the verb comes last. Then say
**मैं सोचता हूँ** — *I think*. You have had that verb for a long time and have
never once been able to say WHAT you think.

## Grammar Lens: कि opens the room the thought goes into
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[HI-JOIN-KI-01]; assesses=[HI-CONCEPT-C34-SOCHNA-01, HI-CONCEPT-C37-CHAI-01, HI-CONCEPT-C03-THIK-01] -->

> **मैं सोचता हूँ कि चाय ठीक है.**
> — *maiṁ sochtā hūṁ ki chāy ṭhīk hai.* —
> **I think that the tea is fine.**

Word for word: *I think **that** tea fine is*. Everything after **कि** is an
ordinary complete Hindi sentence — **चाय ठीक है** — with its verb in the
ordinary last place, doing the job of an object.

That is the trick worth seeing, and it is why the last lesson came first: you do
not need any new grammar to report a thought. You need one word and a sentence
you could already say. **Every sentence in this book just became something you
can think, know or say *about*.**

**लेकिन** joins two halves as EQUALS. **कि** does something different: it puts
one clause INSIDE the other, as the second verb's object. Hindi keeps the main
clause first — **सोचता हूँ** comes before what is thought, never after — so the
word order is the one you would say anyway.

**कि** is not an inherited word. It is Persian **که** (*ke*), taken whole, and
it pushed aside the older Sanskrit-derived form in exactly this slot. Marathi
imported the same word as **की**. Two languages, one borrowing, the same job.
