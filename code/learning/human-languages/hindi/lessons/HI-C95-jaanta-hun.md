# मैं जानता हूँ — the half that was missing

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[HI-CONCEPT-C07-NAHIN-01, HI-CONCEPT-C05-MAINHINDIBOLTAHUN-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say *I do not know* — you have had that sentence since the chapter
on **नहीं**. Now try to say the opposite of it.

## You'll want to know: जानता
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[HI-LEX-C95-KNOW-01]; assesses=[] -->

**मैं जानता हूँ।** — I know.

Nothing about that is new except the permission.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[HI-LEX-C95-KNOW-01, HI-SCRIPT-RECOG-132] -->

You have been saying **मैं नहीं जानता** for a very long time. The negative
lesson used it as its example sentence, and everything in it was explained
except the last word.

| what you had | what you have now |
|---|---|
| मैं नहीं जानता | मैं जानता हूँ |

Take **नहीं** out and one thing has to come back: **हूँ**. A Hindi present
needs its *to be* on the end, and the negative sentence dropped it — which is
ordinary and which is why the affirmative does not look like the negative with
a word removed.

The verb is **जानना**, and its **ज** is the letter drawn two chapters before
this one. It was on the page long before that, inside **जी** and **आज**.

## Grammar Lens: a word carried and never handed over
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[HI-LEX-C95-KNOW-01, HI-CONCEPT-C07-NAHIN-01] -->

This is worth naming because it is a real gap and not a quirk of your memory.
The negative lesson's **headword** names *jāntā* and glosses it as the
masculine present of *to know* — but everything that lesson actually teaches is
about **नहीं**: its Sanskrit root, its Indo-European cousins, its two jobs.

**The word rode along in an example and was never handed over.** You could deny
knowing something and not claim it, which is a strange shape for a vocabulary
to have.
