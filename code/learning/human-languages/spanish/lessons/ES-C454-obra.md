# la obra — the works

## You'll want to know: la obra
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-C454-CORTE-01]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C449-TALLER-01] -->

**la obra** — the works: building work, and the site it happens on.

> Calle cortada por **obras**.

*Están de obras* — they have the builders in. *La obra empieza en marzo* —
work starts in March. *Un obrero* is a manual worker, and *la mano de obra* is
labour.

It also means a work of art or a play — *una obra de teatro* — which is the
same idea: a thing somebody made.

## Grammar Lens: obras in the plural is the roadworks
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C454-CORTE-01, ES-LEX-C346-WHERE-14] -->

Singular and plural point at different things here:

| Spanish | what it names |
|---|---|
| *la obra* | the project, or the artwork |
| *las obras* | the works going on, the disruption |

Every barrier and diversion sign in Spain says **OBRAS**, plural. A Spaniard
saying *hay obras en mi calle* is complaining about noise and dust, not about
a sculpture.

Keep *estar de obras* as a phrase: it describes a building in the state of
being worked on, and *estar* is right because it is a condition that will
pass.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C454-CORTE-01] -->

Latin **opera**, works — the plural of **opus**, a work, taken by Spanish as
a singular feminine noun.

The same *opus* is inside **la oficina**, which is *opus* plus *facere*: the
place where work gets done. So a builder's site and an office are the same
Latin word, arriving by different routes and landing far apart.

*Opera* the music is the identical word again, borrowed through Italian: *the
works*. And English **operate**, **operation** and **opus** are all here too.
One Latin noun for work, and it has ended up meaning a hole in the road, a
room with desks, and a night at the theatre.
