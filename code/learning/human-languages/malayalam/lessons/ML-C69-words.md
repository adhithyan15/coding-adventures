# (reading) — six words, and the mark that ends them

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ML-LEX-C41-DEIXIS-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Every word below you have already met. Not one is new.

[PAUSE 1s] What is new is that nobody says them to you first.

## Reading
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ML-SKILL-READ-WORDS]; assesses=[ML-LEX-C41-DEIXIS-02, ML-LEX-AANU-01] -->

> ഇത്
> അത്
> ആണ്
> ഉണ്ട്
> പൂവ്
> പേര്

[PAUSE 3s] Read down the list once, without stopping.

[PAUSE 3s] Again — and this time look at how every one of them ends.

## You'll want to know
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ML-CONCEPT-C32-UNDU-01, ML-LEX-C52-WELCOME-04, ML-LEX-PERU-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] All six end in the same small hook, the chandrakkala, and it is the
most common mark on any Malayalam page.

[PAUSE 2s] It does two different jobs, and where it sits tells you which. Inside
a word it stacks two consonants into a single shape — that is the mark you
learned when you first wrote the greeting.

[PAUSE 2s] At the END of a word it does something else entirely: it is a faint
half-u, breathed rather than said. **പൂവ്** does not stop at the v;
there is a breath after it, and that breath is what the hook is for.

[PAUSE 2s] So a Malayalam word can end in a mark that is not silence, and a
reader who treats it as silence will sound wrong on five words out of six.
