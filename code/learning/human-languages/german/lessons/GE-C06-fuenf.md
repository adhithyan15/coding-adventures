# fünf — "five," and the law you can hear

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GE-LEX-VIER-01, GE-GRAMMAR-SOUND-LAW-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] The fifth number closes the first hand — and it is the clearest
example of a sound law anywhere in the language.

## You'll want to know: fünf
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[GE-LEX-FUENF-01]; assesses=[GE-LEX-VIER-01] -->

> **fünf** — "five."

## Sounds you'll need
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[GE-SOUND-FUENF-UE-01]; assesses=[GE-GRAMMAR-UMLAUT-FRONTING-01, GE-LEX-FUENF-01] -->

- `umlaut-ue` — **ü** is an *ee* said with your lips rounded for an *oo*. Say
  *ee*, hold the tongue exactly where it is, and push your lips forward.

So *fünf* is roughly *fewnf*, in one syllable. This is the umlaut doing what you
learned it does: it takes the back vowel **u** and drags it to the front of the
mouth.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[GE-ETYMON-FUENF-FIVE-02]; assesses=[GE-LEX-FUENF-01, GE-GRAMMAR-SOUND-LAW-01] -->

**fünf** is English **five**, and the two of them are the sound law from the
last lesson caught in the act.

The older word for five began with a **p**. Latin kept it — *quinque* still has
one in the middle — and Germanic turned every one of those **p**s into an **f**.
So English got *five* and German got *fünf*, both starting with the letter that
was once a *p*:

> old **p** → Germanic **f**: **f**ive / *f*ünf, against Latin's *quinque*

Say *fünf* and *five* one after the other. They are the same word, and the whole
distance between them and the Latin one is that single turn.
