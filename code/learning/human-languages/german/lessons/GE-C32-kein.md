# kein — "no," and the two words hiding inside it

## You'll want to know: kein
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[GE-LEX-KEIN-01]; assesses=[GE-LEX-DER-DIE-DAS-02, GE-LEX-EIN-EINE-01, GE-LEX-BROT-01] -->

> **kein** — "no," "not a," in front of a *der*-word or a *das*-word.

Said *kine*, rhyming with English *mine*: the **ei** is the *eye* sound you have
had since **eins**, and the final **n** is plain.

Where you would say **ein**, say **kein** to cancel it:

> **ein Hund** → **kein Hund** — "a dog" → "no dog"

> **ein Brot** → **kein Brot** — "a loaf" → "no loaf"

Nothing else in the phrase moves. The **k** goes on the front of the word you
already have, and the noun stands exactly as it stood.

## The word, taken apart: not + one
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[GE-ETYMON-KEIN-NICHT-EIN-02]; assesses=[GE-LEX-KEIN-01, GE-LEX-EINS-01, GE-ETYMON-NICHT-NAUGHT-02] -->

**kein** is Old High German ***nihein***, which is **ni** ("not") plus **ein**
("one"). *Not one.* The *ni-* wore down to nothing over a thousand years and
left the *k* that had crept in beside it, and the modern word no longer looks
like its own parts.

English did the identical thing with its own two words, and English still shows
the seam:

| the pieces | what they made |
|---|---|
| *ne* + *ān* ("not" + "one") | **nān** → **none**, **no** |
| *ni* + *ein* ("not" + "one") | **nihein** → **kein** |

So *"kein Brot"* and *"no bread"* are the same sentence, built twice out of the
same two ideas by two languages doing it separately.
