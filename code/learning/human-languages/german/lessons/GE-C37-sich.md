# sich — the verb bent back on itself

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GE-LEX-MICH-01, GE-LEX-DICH-01, GE-LEX-UNS-01, GE-LEX-EUCH-01, GE-LEX-FREUT-MICH-02] -->

[PAUSE 2s] You have said **freut mich** since the second chapter without asking
what the *mich* is doing there. This lesson answers it.

## Grammar Lens: when the doer and the done-to are the same
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[GE-GRAMMAR-REFLEXIV-01]; assesses=[GE-LEX-MICH-01, GE-LEX-DICH-01, GE-LEX-UNS-01, GE-LEX-EUCH-01] -->

> When the person doing something is also the person it happens to, German puts
> the done-to pronoun in anyway.

> **Ich wasche mich.** — "I wash." Literally *I wash me*.

English drops the pronoun in most of these — *I wash*, *I shave*, *I dress* —
and German never does. Every done-to pronoun you own is already the right one:
*mich*, *dich*, *uns*, *euch*. Nothing new is needed for four of the six.

## You'll want to know: sich
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[GE-LEX-SICH-01]; assesses=[GE-GRAMMAR-REFLEXIV-01, GE-LEX-ER-01, GE-LEX-SIE-SHE-02, GE-LEX-IHN-01] -->

> **sich** — the one shape German uses for *himself*, *herself*, *itself*,
> *themselves* and formal *yourself*.

Said *zikh*, with a *z* at the front and the soft ich-sound at the back.

> **Er wäscht sich.** · **Sie wäscht sich.** · **Sie waschen sich.**

Where English needs four different words with *-self* welded on, German needs
one, and it never changes.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GE-LEX-SICH-01, GE-GRAMMAR-REFLEXIV-01, GE-LEX-FREUT-MICH-02] -->

**sich** is Latin **sē** and French **se**, all descended from Proto-Indo-European
***\*swe-***, "one's own". English lost the pronoun and kept the root inside
words built on it: **self**, **suicide**, and — by a long road — **sibling**, a
person of one's own kin.

Which finally explains **freut mich**: *freuen* is one of these verbs, so *es
freut mich* is "it gladdens me", and the greeting you have said a hundred times
was a reflexive construction all along.
