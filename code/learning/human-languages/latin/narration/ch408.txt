Latin, chapter 408: Mistakes and the Cane.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
mendum, mendī — a mistake, a blemish (in writing).

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for ignorance, then the Latin for to imitate.

You'll want to know: mendum, mendī.
mendum, mendī — "a mistake, a blemish (in writing)".
In hōc librō multa menda sunt. There are many mistakes in this book.
mendum, mendī n. Ēmendō, to correct, is to take the mistakes out. English amend and emend.

The word, taken apart.
mendum is inherited from a Proto-Indo-European root meaning "a fault, a flaw" (EtymDB), and ēmendō "to correct" is built on it (Lewis & Short). English amend and emend come from ēmendō.

Lesson 2 of 5.
vitium, vitiī — a fault, a defect; a vice.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to imitate, then the Latin for a mistake.

You'll want to know: vitium, vitiī.
vitium, vitiī — "a fault, a defect; a vice".
Nēmō sine vitiīs nāscitur. No one is born without faults.
vitium, vitiī n. A flaw in a thing, a fault in a person. English vice and vicious.

The word, taken apart.
The origin of vitium is not settled: Lewis & Short tie it to the root of vieō "to twist", so first a twist, a flaw, while EtymDB offers a different Proto-Indo-European source. English vice and vicious come from it.

Lesson 3 of 5.
ferula, ferulae — a cane (for punishing pupils); a fennel stalk.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a mistake, then the Latin for a fault.

You'll want to know: ferula, ferulae.
ferula, ferulae — "a cane (for punishing pupils); a fennel stalk".
Magister ferulam in manū tenēbat. The teacher held the cane in his hand.
ferula, ferulae f.: the stalk of the giant fennel, light and stinging, the schoolmaster's cane. English ferule.

The word, taken apart.
The origin of ferula is not settled: EtymDB derives it from feriō "to strike", but Lewis & Short give no origin for it. English ferule comes from it.

Lesson 4 of 5.
verber, verberis — a lash, a blow; (plural) a beating.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a fault, then the Latin for a cane.

You'll want to know: verber, verberis.
verber, verberis — "a lash, a blow; (plural) a beating".
Puer verbera timet. The boy fears a beating.
verber, verberis n., mostly plural: verbera, blows, a flogging. English reverberate comes from its verb.

The word, taken apart.
verber is inherited from a Proto-Indo-European root meaning "to turn, to bend" (EtymDB), which also gives English warp. English reverberate comes from its verb verberō "to beat".

Lesson 5 of 5.
vāpulō, vāpulāre — to be beaten, to get a thrashing.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a cane, then the Latin for a lash.

You'll want to know: vāpulō, vāpulāre.
vāpulō, vāpulāre — "to be beaten, to get a thrashing".
Puer piger vāpulāvit. The lazy boy got a beating.
Active in form but passive in meaning: vāpulō means 'I am beaten'. Its perfect is vāpulāvī. Vāpulābis!, you'll get a thrashing, is a threat in comedy.

The word, taken apart.
The origin of vāpulō is not settled: Lewis & Short suggest, with a "perhaps", a root meaning to wriggle, and EtymDB gives a Proto-Indo-European root without a meaning.
