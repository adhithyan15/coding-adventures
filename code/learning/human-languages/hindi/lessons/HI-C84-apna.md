# अपना — the word that takes over when the owner is the subject

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[HI-CONCEPT-C02-MERAA-01, HI-LEX-C84-POSS-03] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say *merā* and *tumhārā*. Now try to say "I do my work" in Hindi
using *merā*, and read on to find out why that sentence is wrong.

## You'll want to know: अपना
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[HI-LEX-C84-POSS-04]; assesses=[] -->

**अपना** (*apnā*) — "one's own."

When the owner of a thing is **the subject of the same sentence**, Hindi
replaces the ordinary possessive with *apnā*. This is a requirement rather than
a preference:

| | |
|---|---|
| wrong | *maiṅ **merā** kām kartā hūṅ* |
| right | *maiṅ **apnā** kām kartā hūṅ* |

Both would be "I do my work." The first is what an English speaker builds, and
a Hindi ear hears it as a mistake straight away.

## Grammar Lens: one word covering every person
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[HI-LEX-C84-POSS-04, HI-LEX-C84-POSS-01, HI-LEX-C84-POSS-03] -->

The saving is large, because *apnā* does not care who the subject is:

| subject | English | Hindi |
|---|---|---|
| I | my own | **apnā** |
| you | your own | **apnā** |
| he or she | their own | **apnā** |
| we | our own | **apnā** |

Four English possessives, one Hindi word. It still agrees with the thing owned
in the ordinary way — *apnā kām*, *apnī kitāb*, *apne dost* — because it ends in
**-आ** like all the others.

And it removes a real ambiguity. *Vah **uskī** kitāb paṛhtā hai* means he reads
**somebody else's** book; *vah **apnī** kitāb paṛhtā hai* means he reads **his
own**. English *he reads his book* cannot tell those apart without more words.
