# comí — one regular -er singular past

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-COMER, ES-GRAMMAR-ER-PRESENT-SINGULAR, ES-SOUND-WRITTEN-ACCENT] -->

[PAUSE 2s] You know present **como, comes, come** and the singular **-ar** past.
To make a completed past from **comer**, keep **com-** and learn three forms:
**comí, comiste, comió**.

## Grammar Lens: present beside completed past
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-GRAMMAR-ER-PRETERITE-SINGULAR]; assesses=[ES-LEX-COMER, ES-GRAMMAR-ER-PRESENT-SINGULAR] -->

| person | present | completed past |
|---|---|---|
| yo | **como** | **comí** |
| tú | **comes** | **comiste** |
| él / ella / usted | **come** | **comió** |

Say **comí · comiste · comió**. Keep the limit visible: these are the same three
familiar singular persons. Plural forms wait.

## Grammar Lens: final stress in two forms
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-ORTHOGRAPHY-ER-IR-PRETERITE-FINAL-STRESS]; assesses=[ES-GRAMMAR-ER-PRETERITE-SINGULAR, ES-SOUND-WRITTEN-ACCENT] -->

**Comí** and **comió** carry written accents because their stress falls on the
final vowel. **Comiste** follows ordinary next-to-last stress, so it needs no
mark. The three forms by themselves make complete utterances:

- **Comí.** — “I ate.”
- **¿Comiste?** — “Did you eat?”
- **Comió.** — “He, she, or you-formal ate.”

## The endings, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-HISTORY-ER-IR-PRETERITE-LATIN-PERFECT]; assesses=[ES-ETYMON-COMEDERE, ES-GRAMMAR-ER-PRETERITE-SINGULAR, ES-ORTHOGRAPHY-ER-IR-PRETERITE-FINAL-STRESS] -->

Spanish **comer** continues Latin **comedere**. Its regular past endings also
continue Latin perfect material, including forms with **-ī, -istī, -it**.
Sound change and analogy reshaped that material across Romance into Spanish
**-í, -iste, -ió**. The resemblance is real, but it is not one tidy chain in
which every modern ending merely drops a letter.
