# হৃদয় — heart, and the face read whole

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[BN-SCRIPT-RI-MATRA-01, BN-SCRIPT-DA-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Write **হৃ**. Then write **দ**.

## Script
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[BN-SCRIPT-HRIDOY-READ-01]; assesses=[BN-SCRIPT-HA-01, BN-SCRIPT-RI-MATRA-01, BN-SCRIPT-DA-01, BN-SCRIPT-YA-01, BN-SCRIPT-NUKTA-01] -->

> **হ** + **ৃ** + **দ** + **য়** → **হৃদয়**

- **হৃ** — the breath with the hidden hook under it, *hri*
- **দ** — the dental *d*, carrying its inherent **ô**
- **য়** — the dotted glide, closing the word

**hridoy** — *heart.* Four pieces, and three of the four are things this book
bought for other words entirely: the **দ** came from milk, the **য়** came from
the verb of being, and only the hook was bought for this word alone.

That is the ordinary economy of a syllabary, and it is why a reader's progress
accelerates. The first word cost nine lessons. This one cost one.

The spelling is also a small piece of evidence. **হৃদয়** is a **tatsama** — a
word Bengali took out of Sanskrit whole rather than wearing it down over
centuries — and you can see that in the letters. A worn-down word would have lost
this hook long ago, the way **ভাই** lost the one its Sanskrit ancestor had.
Spelling is where a language keeps its receipts.

Now the whole chapter, off the page:

> **চোখ** · **নাক** · **মুখ** · **হৃদয়**

Four words, thirteen pieces, one new sign between them.
