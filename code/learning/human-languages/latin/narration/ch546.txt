Latin, chapter 546: At the theatre.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
cavea, caveae — the seats of a theatre; a cage.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a band of musicians, then the Latin for harmony.

You'll want to know: cavea, caveae.
cavea, caveae — "the seats of a theatre; a cage".
Tōta cavea plaudit. The whole audience applauds.
Feminine, first declension, from cavus — hollow. The hollow bowl of seats, and so the audience; also a cage or den. English cage.

The word, taken apart.
cavea is built on cavus "hollow" (Lewis & Short). English cage comes from it through Old French.

Lesson 2 of 5.

You'll want to know: pulpitum, pulpitī.
pulpitum, pulpitī — "a stage, platform".
Histriō in pulpitum ascendit. The actor climbs onto the stage.
Neuter, second declension. A wooden platform for actors or speakers. English pulpit.

The word, taken apart.
pulpitum is a Latin word whose origin is not settled; Lewis & Short gives none. English pulpit comes from it.

Lesson 3 of 5.
aulaeum, aulaeī — a theatre curtain.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for the seats of a theatre, then the Latin for a stage.

You'll want to know: aulaeum, aulaeī.
aulaeum, aulaeī — "a theatre curtain".
Aulaeum premitur, et fābula incipit. The curtain is lowered, and the play begins.
Neuter, second declension. Roman curtains worked upside down: they sank into a slot at the start of a play and rose at the end.

The word, taken apart.
aulaeum was borrowed from Greek aulaia "curtain, hanging" (Lewis & Short; EtymDB).

Lesson 4 of 5.
prologus, prologī — a prologue; its speaker.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a stage, then the Latin for a theatre curtain.

You'll want to know: prologus, prologī.
prologus, prologī — "a prologue; its speaker".
Prologus argūmentum fābulae nārrat. The prologue tells the plot of the play.
Masculine, second declension, from Greek. Both the speech before a comedy and the actor who gave it. English prologue.

The word, taken apart.
prologus was borrowed from Greek prologos, built on pro "before" and logos "speech" (EtymDB; Lewis & Short). English prologue comes from it.

Lesson 5 of 5.
pantomīmus, pantomīmī — a mime dancer.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a theatre curtain, then the Latin for a prologue.

You'll want to know: pantomīmus, pantomīmī.
pantomīmus, pantomīmī — "a mime dancer".
Pantomīmus sine verbīs fābulam saltat. The mime dancer dances a story without words.
Masculine, second declension, from Greek. A solo dancer who told myths with gestures; the best were stars.

The word, taken apart.
pantomīmus was borrowed from Greek pantomimos, built on pan "all" and mimeisthai "to imitate" (EtymDB; Lewis & Short). English pantomime comes from it.
