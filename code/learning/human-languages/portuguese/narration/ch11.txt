Lesson 1 of 2.
o pão — bread, worn down to a hum.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
o pão ("bread") — and Portuguese does to it exactly what it did to "father." Just as pater eroded to the one-syllable pai, Latin pānis wore down to the single nasal syllable pão ("powng"). Same word as French pain and Italian pane, taken furthest.

Taken apart.
o pão ("bread") from Latin pānis. The intervocalic -n- dissolved and left its nasality behind on the vowel: pānis becomes pãe becomes pão, the tilde marking the hum — the identical process behind pai ( from pater) and mãe ( from māter) from the family chapter. Masculine: o pão; the plural is the tricky pães.

The word, taken apart: The "bread" family — still there.
Even worn to a hum, the root keeps its English relatives: companion / company (com- + pānis, "one you share bread with"), pantry, pannier. So pão and companion are the near-vanished and the well-preserved ends of one Latin word.

Lesson 2 of 2.
a água, o vinho — water and wine.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Two drinks, two more sound-stories. Portuguese kept água close to Latin aqua (unlike French, which dissolved it to eau), and it stamps vinho with its signature spelling — the -nh-.

The word, taken apart: a água (water) — Latin aqua, kept.
a água ("water") from Latin aqua. Portuguese held its body — aqua becomes água — where French wore it down to a single vowel (eau). The accent on á marks the stress. It's feminine: a água. English kept the loud original in aquatic, aquarium, aqueduct.

The word, taken apart: o vinho (wine) — Latin vīnum, with -nh-.
o vinho ("wine") from Latin vīnum. The -nh- is Portuguese's palatal ny sound — the same sound as Spanish ñ (viño becomes vinho), French/Italian gn, and the nh you already met in amanhã ("tomorrow"). One sound, four spellings across the sisters. The word still gives English wine, vine, vinegar, vintage. Masculine: o vinho.
