Portuguese, chapter 265: Estava Contente --- How It Felt, and What I Hope.
3 lessons. All 3 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 3.
estava contente — how it felt.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say the last line of the journey you read: "I was pleased, and so was my friend". (Eu estava contente, e o meu amigo também.)
[pause 1 second]
That sentence worked for a reason. This lesson is about why.

Grammar Lens: a feeling is scene, and it takes estar.
You already own the feelings: contente, feliz, triste, nervoso, preocupado. A feeling of the moment takes estar, and in a story the past it wants is the imperfeito, estava:
Primeiro estava nervoso. Depois do exame, estava contente. — At first I was nervous. After the exam, I was pleased.
A feeling has no edge. It was going on, like the weather, so in a story it is scene. Set it against ser, which chapter sixteen kept for what something is by nature: ela é feliz is a happy person; ela estava feliz is how she was that day.
And the adjective agrees with whoever felt it. Nervoso has four endings: nervoso, nervosa, nervosos, nervosas. Contente, feliz and triste have one form for a man or a woman, and add -s or -es for more: contentes, felizes, tristes.
Estávamos contentes. — We were pleased. A minha mãe estava preocupada. — My mother was worried.

Grammar Lens: contente com.
To say what you were pleased with, add com, "with":
Estava contente com a viagem. — I was pleased with the journey. Estou contente com o meu português. — I'm pleased with my Portuguese.
Preocupado com works the same way: estava preocupado com o exame, worried about the exam. Portuguese says "with" where English says "about".

The word, taken apart.
contente comes from Latin contentus, "satisfied", the participle of continēre, "to hold together", itself con- plus tenēre, "to hold" (Lewis & Short, under contineo); EtymDB 2.1 traces it only as far as Old Portuguese contente. To be content is to hold yourself in, wanting nothing further. That tenēre is the one inside ter and tinha, and English kept both senses of the word: the content of a box, and a content person.

Lesson 2 of 3.
espero voltar — saying what you are reaching for.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say "I hope so". (Espero que sim.) Then "I was pleased". (Estava contente.)
[pause 1 second]
An experience has a past and a future. Last lesson was how it felt; this one is what you want next.

You'll want to know: espero voltar.
espero + infinitive — "I hope to".
Espero voltar a Portugal. — I hope to come back to Portugal. Espero aprender bem português. — I hope to learn Portuguese well.
You have esperar, which waits and hopes; espero is its "I" form, and an infinitive straight after it settles the meaning: hope. Nothing goes in between. Spanish says the same, espero volver; Italian needs a small word, spero di; Portuguese does not.
When the person who hopes is not the one who will do it, the frame is the one you already have, espero que…, and the verb after it bends into a form a later chapter builds. For now, keep to hopes about yourself.

Grammar Lens: o meu sonho é.
An ambition is bigger than a hope, and it has its own frame:
O meu sonho é viver em Lisboa. — My dream is to live in Lisbon. O meu sonho é viajar muito. — My dream is to travel a lot.
o sonho, "the dream", you have. The article stays in front of meu, as it does all through European Portuguese; in Brazil it is often dropped, meu sonho é. After é the infinitive follows bare: the dream is the doing.
Three strengths, then, for the future: gostaria de viajar, I'd like to; espero viajar, I hope to; o meu sonho é viajar, it is my dream. Only the first needs a de.

The phrase, taken apart.
esperar is inherited from Latin spērāre, "to hope, to expect" (EtymDB 2.1; Lewis & Short, under spero). English desperate is the same verb with dē- in front, "hope taken away", and a esperança, "hope", is its noun. o sonho is inherited from Latin somnium, "a dream" (EtymDB 2.1), which is built on somnus, "sleep": a dream is what sleep brings.

Lesson 3 of 3.
Review — how it felt, and what comes next.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say "I hope to travel". (Espero viajar.)

What you've built.
An experience told well has two ends. The feeling looks back, with estava; the hope looks forward, with espero or o meu sonho é.
A viagem era longa. Perdi o comboio, por isso estava zangado. Mas no fim estava contente com a viagem. Espero voltar.
[pause 2 seconds]
Follow the feelings. Estava zangado came from an event, perdi, and por isso joined them; estava contente closed the story; espero voltar opened the next one.
