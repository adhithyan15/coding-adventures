# el partido — a side split off

## You'll want to know: el partido
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-C446-MOVER-05]; assesses=[ES-LEX-JUGAR-07] -->

**el partido** — the match, the game.

*El partido del domingo* — Sunday's match. *¿Viste el partido?* — did you see
the match? *Jugar un partido* — to play a game.

It is also **the political party**: *el partido socialista*. And *sacar partido
de algo* is to make the most of something.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C446-MOVER-05, ES-LEX-C345-REF-03] -->

**partido** is the past participle of **partir**, to divide — and *partir* is
**la parte**, which you have.

So *partido* means **divided**, and everything it names is something a whole was
split into.

| what it names | what was divided |
|---|---|
| el partido (match) | the players, into two sides |
| el partido (party) | the electorate, into factions |
| sacar partido | the advantage, cut out of a situation |

One word, no coincidence. A football match and a political party are both *a
side that a whole was split into*, and English needs two unrelated words —
*match*, from an old sense of *equal*, and *party*, which is the same Latin
*pars* arriving through French.

## Why it's said this way
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C446-MOVER-05, ES-LEX-C446-MOVER-04] -->

**Partir** has a second life worth having, because it looks like a contradiction.

*Partir* also means **to leave, to set off** — *el tren parte a las ocho*. How
does *divide* become *depart*?

Because leaving is a division: you separate yourself from the place and the
people. English does exactly the same thing and hides it in the Latin —
**depart** is *de-* plus the same *partire*, and **a parting** is both a
splitting and a goodbye.

So a train that **parte** is cutting itself away from the platform, and a train
that is **directo** does not divide the journey at all. The two words in this
chapter that describe a train describe opposite things about it.
