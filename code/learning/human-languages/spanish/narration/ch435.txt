Spanish, chapter 435: Los Papeles Del Trabajo.
7 lessons. All 7 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 7.
el currículum — a word that stayed Latin.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say el trabajo. Say el papel. A job advert asks you to send one particular piece of paper. Name it.

You'll want to know: el currículum.
el currículum — the CV, the résumé.
The full form is currículum vítae, and in speech people usually say only el currículum, or shorten it further to el currí.

The word, taken apart.
Latin curriculum meant a running, a lap of a track — from currere, to run. Currículum vítae is the running of a life.
Spanish kept the Latin -um ending instead of reshaping it, which marks the word as a formal borrowing rather than an inherited one:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
inherited from Latin: la puerta. borrowed from Latin: el currículum.
inherited from Latin: el agua. borrowed from Latin: el álbum.
An inherited word has been worn down by centuries of speech; a borrowed one was lifted from a book and still looks like it. That ending is the giveaway, and it tells you the register before you know the meaning: a word ending -um in Spanish is almost always formal, technical or administrative.
The same currere is under el curso and la carrera, both of which are also runnings.

Lesson 2 of 7.
la documentación — the papers, as one thing.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say el currículum. Say el papel. The garage asked you to bring something more than one sheet. Name it.

You'll want to know: la documentación.
la documentación — the paperwork, the documents, your papers.
Singular in form and plural in meaning: one word for the whole set. Traiga la documentación asks for all of it, not one sheet.

The word, taken apart.
documentar plus -ción, the ending that turns an action into a thing:
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
verb: documentar. the thing: la documentación.
verb: reparar. the thing: la reparación.
verb: informar. the thing: la información.
Fourth ending in five chapters, and the most productive of the lot. Almost any Spanish verb in -ar can take it, and English usually has the twin in -tion — so -ción words are the cheapest reading you will ever get.
One thing to watch: they are always feminine, whatever the verb, and the plural drops the accent — la reparación, las reparaciones.

Lesson 3 of 7.
la experiencia — the word you can already read.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say la documentación. Say la empresa. An advert wants one more thing from you before the papers. Say it and check your guess below.

You'll want to know: la experiencia.
la experiencia — experience.
experiencia previa is the set phrase every job advert uses: se valora experiencia previa, imprescindible experiencia previa.

The word, taken apart.
You did not need this lesson to read the word, and that is worth noticing rather than passing over.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
la experiencia means experience.
la diferencia means difference.
la paciencia means patience.
-encia and English -ence are the same ending, inherited by both from Latin. Hundreds of words pair this way, and the pairing almost never misleads.
So the real content of this lesson is a licence: when a long Spanish noun ends in -ción, -encia, -idad or -mente, try the English twin first. You will be right far more often than wrong, and the cost of being wrong is a moment.
That is not true of short words, where the false friends live — embarazada does not mean embarrassed, and éxito is not exit. The long formal endings are the safe ones.

Lesson 4 of 7.
previo — before this one.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say la experiencia. The advert wants a particular kind of it — the kind you got somewhere else, earlier. Name that kind.

You'll want to know: previo.
previo / previa — previous, prior, earlier.
experiencia previa — previous experience. sin previo aviso — without prior warning, which is the other place you will meet it.

The word, taken apart.
pre- before, plus -vio from via, way. What lies on the way before.
The position is worth watching, because Spanish adjectives usually follow the noun and this one does both:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
phrase: experiencia previa. where it sits: after.
phrase: sin previo aviso. where it sits: before.
Sin previo aviso is a fixed phrase and keeps the older order. Fixed phrases preserve arrangements that ordinary speech has left behind, which is why they so often look like exceptions — they are the older rule, kept whole.

Lesson 5 of 7.
jubilado — the participle that became a person.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say el pedido. Say el trabajo. You learned that a participle can harden into a noun. Here it hardens into a person.

You'll want to know: jubilado.
jubilado / jubilada — retired, and also a retired person.
jubilarse — to retire. Se jubila en marzo — she retires in March.

The word, taken apart.
Same move as el pedido, one chapter apart:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
verb: pedir. participle: pedido. used as: a thing.
verb: jubilar. participle: jubilado. used as: a person.
A Spanish participle can stand as an adjective or a noun with no further change, which is why un jubilado needs no word for person in it.
And the root is not what you would expect. Latin iubilare meant to shout for joy — it is the same word under jubilee and jubilant. Retiring in Spanish is, etymologically, letting out a cheer.
Whether anybody feels that on the day is another matter, but the word was built by people who thought the end of work was worth shouting about.

Lesson 6 of 7.
(currículum, documentación, experiencia, previo, jubilado) — from cold.

Warm-up.
[pause 3 seconds]
Close the lessons before this one. Say the CV, the paperwork and retired.

Grammar Lens: the endings that read themselves.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
ending: -ción. what it does: action becomes a thing. example: la documentación.
ending: -encia. what it does: pairs with English -ence. example: la experiencia.
ending: participle. what it does: becomes a thing or a person. example: jubilado.
The participle row is the one to keep: el pedido in the first chapter of this run was a thing, jubilado here is a person, and neither needed an extra word to become one.
Five chapters have now handed you -dor, -ería, -ero, -ción and -encia. Do not read that as a rule that every word yields — el currículum in this very chapter kept a Latin ending and yields nothing. The habit is to look, not to expect.

What you've built.
You could say you had a job, and nothing about getting one. El trabajo, la empresa and trabajar were yours; the CV you send, the papers you bring, the experience an advert demands and the word for somebody who has stopped working were not.
A job advert is four lines long and uses all five. That is the shape of A2 reading: short, ordinary, administrative, and impossible without exactly these nouns.
