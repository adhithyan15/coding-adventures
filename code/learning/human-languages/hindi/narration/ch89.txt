Hindi, chapter 89: School: The Borrowed Word and the Built One.
5 lessons. You can do the first 4 of them in the car; after that you will want to have stopped.


Lesson 1 of 5.
स्कूल (skūl) — the building the teacher and the student were always in.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say शिक्षक and chātra. You have had both of them for a long time. Now say the place they meet — and notice that you cannot yet.

You'll want to know: स्कूल.
स्कूल (skūl) — "school". Masculine.
Write it: स + ् + क gives the conjunct स्क, then ू above the ल... no: the ू belongs to क, so it is स्कू and then ल. Three pieces, one of them a conjunct you have seen before.

The word, taken apart.
This one is borrowed straight from English, and the English word has a joke inside it that Hindi inherited without asking.
English school comes from Latin schola, from Greek skholḗ — which meant leisure. Free time. The hours you did not have to work.
The Greeks thought study was what a person did with their leisure, so the word for time off slid across to mean the place you spend it learning. Every language that borrowed it borrowed the joke: Spanish escuela, German Schule, Russian shkola, and now Hindi स्कूल (skūl).
So when you say skūl, you are saying a Greek word for "spare time" that has travelled through Latin, English and into Devanagari without anyone noticing what it used to mean.

Lesson 2 of 5.
विद्यालय (vidyālay) — an abode of knowledge.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
You have स्कूल (skūl), borrowed whole. Hindi also has its own word, built out of two pieces you can take apart. Guess which register it belongs to before reading on — the sign above the gate, or the sentence a child says?

You'll want to know: विद्यालय.
विद्यालय (vidyālay) — "school", masculine, and noticeably more formal than स्कूल. It is what you read on the gate, on a form, in a newspaper. A child telling you where they are going says skūl.

The word, taken apart.
Two Sanskrit pieces, joined the way Sanskrit joins everything:
विद्या (vidyā) — "knowledge" — plus आलय (ālaya) — "abode". An abode of knowledge.
आलय is productive on its own: himālaya is हिम ("snow") + आलय — the abode of snow. You already know that mountain's name; now you know it is a compound, and the second half is sitting in this word too.
And विद्या rests on the root विद् (vid), "to know" — which is one of the furthest-travelled roots in any language on earth:
Sanskrit वेद (veda) — knowledge, the name of the oldest texts.
Latin vidēre "to see", giving video, vision, evidence.
English wit, wisdom, and the -wise in likewise.
So vidyālay and the English word wit are built on the same ancient syllable. The borrowed स्कूल (skūl) carried a Greek word for leisure into Hindi; the native विद्यालय was here all along, and its root reaches English by a different road entirely.

Lesson 3 of 5.
स्कूल (skūl) or विद्यालय (vidyālay) — the same building, two registers.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Both words mean school. Say each one, and decide which you would write on an official form.

Grammar Lens: borrowed whole, or built from parts.
This is the third time this pattern has turned up, and it is worth naming:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
meaning: a meal. borrowed whole: नाश्ता. built from parts: दोपहर का खाना.
meaning: a school. borrowed whole: स्कूल (skūl). built from parts: विद्यालय (vidyālay).
Hindi keeps both kinds and uses them at different heights. The borrowed word is what people say; the built word is what institutions write. Neither is more correct, and a learner who only has one of them will sound wrong in half the situations they meet.
Note which way round it falls here: the borrowed word is the casual one. That is not a rule — it is what happened to these two.

Lesson 4 of 5.
शिक्षक स्कूल में हैं (śikṣak skūl meṁ haiṁ) — the building, with people in it.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
A place you can name is not yet a place you can put somebody in. Say where the teacher is, out loud, before reading on.

Reading.
Every word here is one you have met:
शिक्षक स्कूल में हैं (śikṣak skūl meṁ haiṁ)। chātra भी स्कूल में हैं। विद्यालय (vidyālay) बड़ा है।.
Three short lines, and the third one changes register on purpose: the sign over the door calls the building विद्यालय (vidyālay), even though the two sentences before it called the same building स्कूल (skūl).

Grammar Lens: why the reading paper cares.
This matters beyond the vocabulary. An exam reading paper is built out of signs, forms and notices — exactly the places where the formal word appears — while the listening paper is built out of people talking, where the casual word does.
A candidate who knows only स्कूल (skūl) can follow the conversation and stall on the sign. A candidate who knows only विद्यालय (vidyālay) does the reverse.

Lesson 5 of 5.
(नाम, कैसे, फिर) (nām, kaise, phir) — the opening of the book, eighty chapters later.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, because the lesson points at something written down. You can listen to all of it now and come back to the parts that need looking at once you have stopped.

Warm-up.
[pause 3 seconds]
Ask a stranger their name, respectfully. Then say the word for again.

You'll want to know: why this page gives you no cues.
The opening chapters built the four moves that open and close a meeting. Every page since leaned on them; none asked you to produce them cold. An examiner will, so this page names a move and waits.

The exchange — four of them, by what each one does.
[a table of 4 rows, read aloud]
the move: open. what you produce: the greeting, and the answer to a thank-you.
the move: trade names. what you produce: the question, your answer, the pleased-to-meet-you.
the move: ask after them. what you produce: the how-are-you, and a reply.
the move: close. what you produce: one of three farewells, chosen by formality.
Cover the right-hand column; the left one is the whole cue.

Grammar Lens: कैसा, कैसी, कैसे — the one that bends.
कैसा masculine singular, कैसी feminine, कैसे for a respectful आप or a masculine plural.
That last cell is why आप कैसे हैं? takes कैसे for one person: आप counts as plural whoever it points at, and adjective and verb follow it.

You'll want to know: आपका स्वागत है, and where ख़ुशी belongs.
One phrase, two moments: it answers a thank-you (you're welcome) and greets an arrival (welcome), with no change to the Hindi. आपसे मिलकर ख़ुशी हुई is narrower — the first minute, nowhere else.

Why it's said this way — three farewells, three distances.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
what you say: अलविदा. how it lands: final, weighty. who it is for: a long parting.
what you say: फिर मिलेंगे. how it lands: warm, ordinary. who it is for: almost anyone.
what you say: चलता हूँ / चलती हूँ. how it lands: casual, spoken. who it is for: people you know.
फिर on its own means again or then, and it turns the middle row into a promise of a further meeting rather than an ending. चलता हूँ announces your own going, so its ending matches whoever speaks.
