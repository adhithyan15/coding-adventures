Lesson 1 of 1.
vi, di — the shortest pasts in the language.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, because the lesson points at something written down. You can listen to all of it now and come back to the parts that need looking at once you have stopped.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
The -er past, first person: comí, with an accent, because the stress lands at the end.
[pause 1 second]
Now do the same to ver, whose stem is a single letter.

Grammar Lens: nothing left to accent.
ver becomes vi, viste, vio.
[pause 2 seconds]
Look at vi and vio: no accent, where comí and comió both had one.
[pause 2 seconds]
And the reason is not an exception. A written accent exists to say the beat is here, not where you would expect. A word of one syllable has nowhere else the beat could be, so there is nothing to correct. Spanish does not spend a mark on a choice that does not exist.

Grammar Lens: and dar, which behaves like an -er verb.
Dar means to give. It is an -ar verb — and in the preterite it flatly refuses to behave like one:
dar becomes di, diste, dio, dimos, disteis, dieron.
[pause 2 seconds]
Those are the -er endings, on an -ar verb, with the same one-syllable forms going bare. Ver does the same in the plural: vimos, visteis, vieron.
[pause 2 seconds]
Two verbs, one shared oddity, and now the last two named irregulars of the preterite are yours.

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 2 seconds]
Why do vi and di carry no accent? (One syllable — the beat has nowhere else to fall.)
[pause 2 seconds]
And which endings does dar take in the past? (The -er ones, though it is an -ar verb.)
