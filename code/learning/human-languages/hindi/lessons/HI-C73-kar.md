# -कर (*-kar*) — having done it

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[HI-JOIN-JAB-TAB-01, HI-GRAMMAR-SAKNA-STEM-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say the **जब … तब** frame. Two clauses, two verbs, a word at each
end. That is a lot of scaffolding for *first this, then that* — and Hindi has a
much shorter way that it uses far more often.

## Grammar Lens: put -कर on the stem and the clause disappears
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[HI-GRAMMAR-KAR-01]; assesses=[HI-LEX-LOOK, HI-LEX-COME, HI-CONCEPT-C05-KARNA-01, HI-GRAMMAR-VERB-FINAL-01] -->

> **मैं किताब देखकर आता हूँ.** — *maiṁ kitāb dekhkar ātā hūṁ.* —
> **I look at the book and come.** (Literally: *having looked, I come.*)

The move is the same shape as **सकना**: **take the -ना off the first verb and
put -कर on the bare stem.**

- **देखना** → **देखकर** — having looked
- **करना** → **करके** — having done (this one is irregular, and it is the
  commonest of all)

What you get is not a second clause. It is one clause with a chained action
inside it, and only the LAST verb takes a person ending. That is why Hindi
reaches for it constantly: a speaker can string three actions together and
conjugate once.

The order is the order of events. **देखकर आता हूँ** is look-then-come, never the
other way round, so you say the actions in the sequence they happen.

The ending is the verb **करना** frozen solid. *Having looked* is literally
*look-do*. Marathi does the same job with its own *-ūn*; both languages inherited the
construction from Sanskrit and each reinforced it with its own material.
