# विद्यालय (vidyālay) — an abode of knowledge

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[HI-LEX-C81-SCHOOL-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] You have **स्कूल**, borrowed whole. Hindi also has its own word,
built out of two pieces you can take apart. Guess which register it belongs to
before reading on — the sign above the gate, or the sentence a child says?

## You'll want to know: विद्यालय
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[HI-LEX-C81-SCHOOL-02]; assesses=[] -->

**विद्यालय** (*vidyālay*) — "school", masculine, and noticeably more formal
than **स्कूल**. It is what you read on the gate, on a form, in a newspaper. A
child telling you where they are going says *skūl*.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[] -->

Two Sanskrit pieces, joined the way Sanskrit joins everything:

> **विद्या** (*vidyā*) — "knowledge" — plus **आलय** (*ālaya*) — "abode".
> An abode of knowledge.

**आलय** is productive on its own: *himālaya* is **हिम** ("snow") + आलय — the
abode of snow. You already know that mountain's name; now you know it is a
compound, and the second half is sitting in this word too.

And **विद्या** rests on the root **विद्** (*vid*), "to know" — which is one of
the furthest-travelled roots in any language on earth:

- Sanskrit **वेद** (*veda*) — knowledge, the name of the oldest texts.
- Latin *vidēre* "to see", giving **vid**eo, **vis**ion, e**vid**ence.
- English **wit**, **wis**dom, and the *-wise* in *likewise*.

So *vidyālay* and the English word *wit* are built on the same ancient syllable.
The borrowed **स्कूल** carried a Greek word for leisure into Hindi; the native
**विद्यालय** was here all along, and its root reaches English by a different
road entirely.
