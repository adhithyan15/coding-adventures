Latin, chapter 520: Breathing.
6 lessons. All 6 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 6.
spīritus, spīritūs — breath; spirit.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to smell, then the Latin for to perceive.

You'll want to know: spīritus, spīritūs.
spīritus, spīritūs — "breath; spirit".
Post cursum spīritum vix dūcō. After the run I can hardly draw breath.
Masculine, fourth declension, from spīrō. Breath first, then the spirit. English spirit.

The word, taken apart.
spīritus is the action noun of spīrō "to breathe" (Lewis & Short; EtymDB). English spirit comes from it.

anhēlō, anhēlāre — "to pant, gasp".
Canis post cursum anhēlat. The dog pants after the run.
anhēlō, anhēlāre, anhēlāvī, anhēlātum.

The word, taken apart.
anhēlō is built, as Lewis & Short explains it, on a prefix an- and hālō "to breathe out", but the details are not settled.

Lesson 3 of 6.
singultus, singultūs — a sob; a hiccup.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for breath, then the Latin for to pant.

You'll want to know: singultus, singultūs.
singultus, singultūs — "a sob; a hiccup".
Puella inter singultūs loquitur. The girl speaks between sobs.
Masculine, fourth declension. Breath broken by weeping, or a hiccup.

The word, taken apart.
singultus is usually traced to singulī "one at a time" (Lewis & Short): speech broken into single sounds.

Lesson 4 of 6.
oscitō, oscitāre — to yawn, gape.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to pant, then the Latin for a sob.

You'll want to know: oscitō, oscitāre.
oscitō, oscitāre — "to yawn, gape".
Discipulus fessus in scholā oscitat. The tired pupil yawns at school.
oscitō, oscitāre: to open the mouth wide; also to be drowsy and careless.

The word, taken apart.
oscitō is built on ōs "mouth" and citō "to set in motion" (EtymDB): to set the mouth moving. English oscitation "yawning" is a rare learned word from it.

Lesson 5 of 6.
sternuō, sternuere — to sneeze.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a sob, then the Latin for to yawn.

You'll want to know: sternuō, sternuere.
sternuō, sternuere — "to sneeze".
Puer propter pulverem sternuit. The boy sneezes because of the dust.
sternuō, sternuere, sternuī. To Roman eyes a sneeze could be an omen.

The word, taken apart.
sternuō is a native Latin verb; Lewis & Short compares Greek ptarnusthai "to sneeze", but EtymDB gives no parent, so the origin is not settled.
