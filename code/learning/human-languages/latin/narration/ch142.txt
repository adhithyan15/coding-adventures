Lesson 1 of 5.
furca, furcae — a fork, a pitchfork.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a water jar, then the Latin for a cup.

You'll want to know: furca, furcae.
furca, furcae — "a fork, a pitchfork".

The word, taken apart.
furca has no sure origin; no proposal is generally accepted. English fork was borrowed from it early.

Lesson 2 of 5.
arātrum, arātrī — a plough.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a cup, then the Latin for a fork.

You'll want to know: arātrum, arātrī.
arātrum, arātrī — "a plough".

The word, taken apart.
arātrum is inherited, usually traced to Proto-Indo-European h2erh3trom, "plough", from h2erh3-, "to plough" (EtymDB 2.1); Greek arotron, "plough", is a cousin (Lewis & Short). arō, "to plough", is the Latin verb from the same root.

Lesson 3 of 5.
tintinnābulum, tintinnābulī — a bell.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a fork, then the Latin for a plough.

You'll want to know: tintinnābulum, tintinnābulī.
tintinnābulum, tintinnābulī — "a bell".

The word, taken apart.
tintinnābulum is built on tintinnō, "to ring, to jingle" (Lewis & Short), an imitation of the sound, with -bulum, an ending for a tool.

Lesson 4 of 5.
tībia, tībiae — a flute, a pipe.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a plough, then the Latin for a bell.

You'll want to know: tībia, tībiae.
tībia, tībiae — "a flute, a pipe".

The word, taken apart.
tībia first meant the shin-bone: Lewis & Short give tībia that sense first. The pipe may have taken its name from pipes made of bone. Where the word comes from is unknown.

Lesson 5 of 5.
lyra, lyrae — a lyre.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a bell, then the Latin for a flute.

You'll want to know: lyra, lyrae.
lyra, lyrae — "a lyre".

The word, taken apart.
lyra was borrowed from Greek lyra, "lyre" (Lewis & Short); where Greek got the word is unknown. English lyric comes from the Greek family.
