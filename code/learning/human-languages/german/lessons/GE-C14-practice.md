# Practice — the whole of haben

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GE-LEX-HABEN-01, GE-SOUND-HABEN-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] No new words. Four of the six forms below cost you nothing, and the
other two have each had a lesson, so this is the first place it is safe to set
them out together.

## Grammar Lens: the six, assembled
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GE-GRAMMAR-HABEN-REGULAR-01, GE-GRAMMAR-HABEN-DU-HAST-01, GE-GRAMMAR-HABEN-ER-HAT-01, GE-GRAMMAR-HABEN-B-DROP-01] -->

| singular | plural |
|---|---|
| ich **habe** | wir **haben** |
| du **hast** | ihr **habt** |
| er/sie/es **hat** | sie/Sie **haben** |

Run it aloud and hear where the *b* goes missing: *habe* … **hast** … **hat** …
*haben*. Four forms carry it, two do not, and the two that do not are the two
shortest.

## The exchange
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GE-LEX-EINEN-01, GE-GRAMMAR-HABEN-DU-HAST-01, GE-GRAMMAR-HABEN-ER-HAT-01] -->

| German | English |
|---|---|
| *Hast du einen Bruder?* | Do you have a brother? |
| *Und du?* | And you? |
| *Ich habe eine Schwester.* | I have a sister. |
| *Wir haben Brot und Wein.* | We have bread and wine. |
| *Gut! Dann haben wir alles.* | Good! Then we have everything. |

Two things slip in without lessons of their own, because the translation carries
them: **eine**, which is what *ein* becomes for a *die*-word, and **alles**
("everything"). And notice *Hast du …?* — a question puts the verb first, which
is the one move German makes that English needs *do* for.

## Wrap-up Recall
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GE-GRAMMAR-HABEN-B-DROP-01, GE-ETYMON-HABEN-HABERE-01, GE-LEX-EINEN-01] -->

[PAUSE 3s] Run the six without looking. Which two drop the *b*? (***Hast***
**and** ***hat***.) Say "I have a brother." (**Ich habe einen Bruder**.) Is
*haben* related to Latin *habēre*? (**No**.) Which English word is it related
to? (***Capture***.) Next: the one everyday sentence where German refuses this
verb altogether.
