Latin, chapter 466: Corn supply, Dearness, Cheapness, To value, To be sold.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
annōna, annōnae — the corn supply; the price of grain.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a quarter-as, then the Latin for a money-changer.

You'll want to know: annōna, annōnae.
annōna, annōnae — "the corn supply; the price of grain".
Hōc annō annōna cāra est, quod pluvia nūlla fuit. This year grain is dear, because there was no rain.
First declension, feminine: annōna, annōnae f. First the year's harvest, then the city's grain supply and its price.

The word, taken apart.
annōna is built on annus 'year' (EtymDB; Lewis & Short): the produce of the year, and so the grain supply and its price.

Lesson 2 of 5.
cāritās, cāritātis — dearness, high prices; affection.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a money-changer, then the Latin for the corn supply.

You'll want to know: cāritās, cāritātis.
cāritās, cāritātis — "dearness, high prices; affection".
Propter cāritātem annōnae pauperēs parum cibī habent. Because of the high price of grain the poor have too little food.
Third declension, feminine: cāritās, cāritātis f., from cārus 'dear'. Dear in price, and dear to the heart. English charity comes from it.

The word, taken apart.
cāritās is built on cārus 'dear, costly', from PIE keh₂- 'to desire' (EtymDB; Lewis & Short). English charity comes from it, in its sense 'affection'.

Lesson 3 of 5.
vīlitās, vīlitātis — cheapness, low prices.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for the corn supply, then the Latin for dearness.

You'll want to know: vīlitās, vīlitātis.
vīlitās, vīlitātis — "cheapness, low prices".
Post bonum annum vīlitās est, et agricolae minus pecūniae habent. After a good year prices are low, and the farmers have less money.
Third declension, feminine: vīlitās, vīlitātis f. The opposite of cāritās. Also worthlessness.

The word, taken apart.
vīlitās is built on vīlis 'cheap, of little value' with the suffix -itās (EtymDB; Lewis & Short). English vile comes from vīlis.

Lesson 4 of 5.
aestimō, aestimāre — to value, to put a price on; to judge.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for dearness, then the Latin for cheapness.

You'll want to know: aestimō, aestimāre.
aestimō, aestimāre — "to value, to put a price on; to judge".
Mercātor equum centum dēnāriīs aestimat. The merchant values the horse at a hundred denarii.
aestimō, aestimāre, aestimāvī, aestimātum, with the price in the ablative or genitive: magnī aestimāre, to value highly. English estimate and esteem come from it.

The word, taken apart.
aestimō comes from older aestumāre (EtymDB), and Lewis & Short link it with aes 'bronze, money', a valuing in money; the second part is not settled. English estimate and esteem come from it.

Lesson 5 of 5.
vēneō, vēnīre — to be sold, to go on sale.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for cheapness, then the Latin for to value.

You'll want to know: vēneō, vēnīre.
vēneō, vēnīre — "to be sold, to go on sale".
In forō servī et equī vēneunt. Slaves and horses are sold in the forum.
Irregular like eō: vēneō, vēnīre, vēniī. It serves as the passive of vendō. Do not confuse vēnīre with venīre 'to come'.

The word, taken apart.
vēneō comes from vēnum eō 'I go to sale' (EtymDB; Lewis & Short), with vēnum 'sale' and eō 'to go'; vendō 'to sell' is from vēnum dō 'I give to sale'.
