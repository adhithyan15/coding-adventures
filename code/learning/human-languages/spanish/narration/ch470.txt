Spanish, chapter 470: Un paraguas azul.
6 lessons. The first lesson already needs your eyes or your hands, so save this one for when you have stopped.


Lesson 1 of 6.
el paraguas — the umbrella.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, because the lesson points at something written down. You can listen to all of it now and come back to the parts that need looking at once you have stopped.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say la parada and el cumpleaños. One lends this word its first half, the other lends it its shape.

You'll want to know: el paraguas.
el paraguas — the umbrella.
Me he dejado el paraguas en el ascensor.
Look at it before you are told anything: para plus aguas. A verb and a plural noun fused into one word, which is the move el cumpleaños taught you — cumple plus años, a sentence that hardened.
And like cumpleaños, it wears a plural -s and is singular: el paraguas, one umbrella.

Grammar Lens: the plural that does not move.
So what is the plural? Nothing changes.
[a table of 1 row, read aloud]
one: el paraguas. more than one: los paraguas.
This is not a quirk of this word. El análisis gave you the rule: a word already ending in s, with its stress somewhere other than the last syllable, takes no plural ending. El lunes / los lunes works the same way.
The article is doing all the counting. Drop it and the sentence cannot tell you how many umbrellas there are.

The word, taken apart.
The para- is the one la parada already unpacked: Latin parare, to make ready — and parada reminded you it is not the Greek para- of parallel. A parasol is made ready against the sun, a parachute against a fall. A paraguas is made ready against the waters.
Which is worth a second look, because English named the same object from the opposite direction:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
language: Spanish paraguas. what the word notices: the water it keeps off.
language: English umbrella. what the word notices: the shade it makes.
Umbrella is Latin umbra, a shadow, with a diminutive on it — a little shade, and English took it from Italian. So one language named the object for the shade it casts and the other for the water it turns away, and both are describing the same thing held over the same head.

Lesson 2 of 6.
el mango — the handle.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say la mano and el paraguas. This word is a relative of the first and a part of the second.

You'll want to know: el mango.
el mango — the handle.
Un paraguas azul, con el mango de madera.
The handle of a knife, a pan, a door, an umbrella: whatever part of a thing is built for the hand.
Which is what the word says. It is a cousin of la mano, and once you hear that, you will not need to store it separately.

Grammar Lens: two mangos, and only one of them grows.
There is a second mango in Spanish, and it is a completely different word that happens to be spelled the same. This is the situation la cola prepared you for.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
the word: el mango — handle. where it comes from: Latin manus, the hand.
the word: el mango — the fruit. where it comes from: Portuguese manga, from Malay.
The Academy traces the fruit through Portuguese manga to Malay mangga; behind that stands a Dravidian word, Tamil māṅkāy. It travelled by ship and the handle did not. Nothing connects them but the spelling.
Context settles it every time, so this is not a hazard — it is a reminder that same letters and same word are different claims.

The word, taken apart.
Latin manus, the hand — the root la mano already gave you, along with manual, manufacture and maintain.
A mango is the hand-part. Spanish built it on a late Latin form of that root and let the meaning settle on the graspable end of a thing.
English does not have this word, so it has nothing to lend you here. What it does have is manual, manuscript, manacle — all of them hands. Put mango beside them and it stops looking like a fruit.

Lesson 3 of 6.
encontrar — to find.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say buscar. You have had the looking for a long time. Say el mango. You still have no word for the moment the looking succeeds.

You'll want to know: encontrar.
encontrar — to find.
No lo encuentro por ninguna parte.
Buscar and encontrar are the two halves of one search, and Spanish keeps them strictly apart:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
what you do: buscar — look for. what happens: the effort.
what you do: encontrar — find. what happens: the result.
Busco mi paraguas is work in progress. He encontrado mi paraguas is over. You can buscar all afternoon and never encontrar.

Grammar Lens: the o that breaks.
You wrote encuentro above, not encontro. That is mostrar's rule arriving again, unchanged: push the stress back onto the stem and the o breaks into ue.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
stress on the stem: encuentro, encuentras. stress on the ending: encontramos.
stress on the stem: encuentra, encuentran. stress on the ending: encontráis.
The infinitive keeps its o because the stress is on the ending there — encon-TRAR. Nothing about the verb is irregular once you know where the weight falls.

The word, taken apart.
en- plus contra: to come up against something.
That is the whole picture, and it is more honest than the English word. Find says nothing about how. Encontrar says you went along and the thing was in your way.
English has the same word by a different road: encounter, borrowed from French, is in plus contra exactly. To encounter somebody is to come against them — which is why an encounter can be a meeting or a fight, and un encuentro in Spanish is both as well.
So encontrarse con alguien is to run into somebody. The contra has not gone anywhere.

Lesson 4 of 6.
avisar — to let know.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say el aviso. Say encontrar. If somebody finds the thing, this is what you want them to do next.

You'll want to know: avisar.
avisar — to let know, to notify.
Si lo habéis visto, avisadme, por favor.
You have met this verb once already without being given it. El aviso named it in passing — "behind it is the verb avisar, to notify" — and then went back to the noun. Here it is as yours.
Avísame — let me know. Avisar a alguien — to tell somebody. Avisar de algo — to give notice of something.

Grammar Lens: it shows, it does not order.
El aviso made a point that the verb inherits whole: an aviso is not an instruction but a making-visible. Somebody puts a thing where you can see it, and what you do next is yours.
So avisar is the weakest of the telling verbs, and that is its use.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
you want: somebody to know, no more. you say: avisar.
you want: somebody to do it. you say: decir that they must.
Avísame si lo encuentras asks for information and nothing else. It is what you say about a paraguas you left behind: you are not giving an order to a neighbour, you are asking to be shown something.

The word, taken apart.
El aviso already traced this: Latin ad vīsum, toward what has been seen, on vidēre, to see. There is nothing to add to the derivation, so take instead the thing it predicts.
Every relative of avisar is about seeing, not speaking:
English advise is the same formation — to put something in somebody's view;
advice is what you were shown;
evident, vision, provide, survey are all seeings.
A word for telling, built entirely out of sight. That is why avisar feels lighter than it looks: nobody in it is talking.

Lesson 5 of 6.
(el paraguas, el mango, encontrar, avisar) — from cold.

Warm-up.
[pause 3 seconds]
Four words, no prompts. The thing you left behind, the part of it you would describe, what you cannot do, and what you want the finder to do.

You'll want to know: the four, cold.
[a table of 4 rows, read aloud]
English: the umbrella. Spanish: el paraguas.
English: the handle. Spanish: el mango.
English: to find. Spanish: encontrar.
English: to let know. Spanish: avisar.

Grammar Lens: one English verb, two Spanish ones.
English says look for it and tell me. Spanish makes you choose twice, and the choices are not the ones you would guess.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
the English: look / find. what Spanish splits it into: buscar is the effort, encontrar the result.
the English: tell. what Spanish splits it into: avisar shows; decir says.
Avísame si lo encuentras holds both splits in five words: let me know — not order me, not explain to me — if the search actually lands.
That sentence is the whole chapter, and you could not have built it a lesson ago.

The words, taken apart.
Three of the four are older than they look, and each keeps a picture inside.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
word: el paraguas. the picture: made ready against the waters.
word: el mango. the picture: the part built for the hand.
word: encontrar. the picture: to come up against a thing.
Avisar is the fourth, and its picture is seeing rather than telling — which is what made it the gentle verb in the lens above.

Lesson 6 of 6.
(el mensaje del vecino) — read it, then answer.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say el paraguas and avisar. One is lost and the other is what the message is asking for.

Reading: the message.
To the building group, this morning:
Hola, ¿sois vosotros los del piso doce? Me he dejado un paraguas azul, con el mango de madera, en el ascensor esta mañana y ya no lo encuentro por ninguna parte. Si lo habéis visto, avisadme, por favor.
Two things to notice before you answer.
Azul, de madera. The colour is the umbrella's and the wood is the handle's. Spanish hangs each description on the noun it belongs to, and de madera is how you say what a thing is made of.
Por ninguna parte. The stacking is no … nada's rule, unchanged: no in front of the verb and the negative word after it, both required. What is new is only the phrase. Ninguna parte joins nada, nadie and nunca as a negative word, and it is two words rather than one.
The trap is the English. No lo encuentro por ninguna parte comes out as I can't find it anywhere, which looks positive — so the pull is to reach for en cualquier parte. Spanish keeps the negative on both halves.

Grammar Lens: what the message is actually asking.
Read the last sentence again. It does not ask anyone to look.
[a table of 1 row, read aloud]
what it says: si lo habéis visto. what it does not say: buscadlo.
The writer is asking to be told about something that may already have happened, not asking the building to start a search. That is avisar doing exactly the work it was built for, and it is the polite shape of this kind of message: you ask for information, not labour.
Answer in the same register.

Guided Practice.
[pause 3 seconds]
Say what was lost, where, and what it looks like — colour and handle both.
[pause 3 seconds]
Now answer the message twice. First: you have seen it, and you say where. Then: you have not, and you offer to let them know if it turns up.

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
What is lost? (Un paraguas azul con el mango de madera.) What can the writer not do? (Encontrarlo.) What are they asking for? (Que les avisen — to be told.)
