Arabic, chapter 10: Family.
3 lessons. All 3 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 3.
أب, أم (ab, umm) — father and mother.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Two short, ancient words — each just two or three letters — sitting at the root of Semitic family vocabulary across several languages, not just Arabic.

Taken apart.
أب (ab) = "father." A short, ancient Semitic root, cousin of Hebrew אב (av) and Aramaic אבא (abba) — the very word behind "Abba, Father" in the New Testament.
أم (umm) = "mother." Equally ancient, cousin of Hebrew אם (em).
These aren't borrowings between Arabic and Hebrew — both descend from the same ancient Semitic family vocabulary, the way pater and English father are cousins through PIE rather than one copying the other.

Why it's said this way — A living naming pattern.
أم stays productive in everyday Arabic naming: أم + a child's name forms a kunya, an honorific "mother of ___" (and أبو, abū, does the same for fathers — "father of ___"). Someone might be known publicly as Umm Khālid, "mother of Khālid," rather than by their own given name — a naming custom still very much alive today.

[your turn — say: the Hebrew cousins — "ab/av, umm/em"]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: the naming pattern — "Umm + [child's name]" = mother of ___]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
What are Arabic's words for father and mother? (أب ab, أم umm.) What are their Hebrew cousins? (av, em — not borrowings, both from the same ancient Semitic root.) What naming pattern does umm still form today? (Umm + [child's name], "mother of ___," a living honorific.)


Lesson 2 of 3.
أخ, أخت (akh, ukht) — brother and sister.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Unlike the color words you just learned (a whole reshaped template for masculine/feminine), siblings here use the simplest possible gender marker.

Taken apart.
أخ (akh) = "brother" — another short, ancient Semitic root, cousin of Hebrew אח (ach).
أخت (ukht) = "sister" — the same root, plus a plain feminine ‑ت (‑t) ending, cousin of Hebrew אחות (achot).
So ukht isn't a separate word — it's akh wearing the ordinary feminine ending, the simple pattern you already know from آسف/آسفة (āsif/āsifa, "sorry"), not the elaborate colors template.

Lesson 3 of 3.
The name exchange, cold — the introduction exchange run cold, eight lessons after it was taught and never asked for since — four suffixes, one missing verb, and a root that means honour.

Warm-up.
[pause 4 seconds]
Eight lessons ago this book taught you to give your name and ask for somebody else's, and has not asked since. Say the whole exchange from memory before you read another line.

The two words.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
word: اسم ism. letters, right to left: alif, sīn, mīm. what it is: name — root s–m–w, cousin of Hebrew shem.
word: ما mā. letters, right to left: mīm, alif. what it is: what, standing before a noun.
Neither needed a new letter. اسم has no tie to English name or Latin nōmen — the resemblance is an accident, and the real cousin is Hebrew.

Grammar Lens: four suffixes and no verb.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
glued on: ـي -ī. means: my. gives: اسمي ismī.
glued on: ـك -ka. means: your, to a man. gives: اسمك ismuka.
glued on: ـك -ki. means: your, to a woman. gives: اسمك ismuki.
Arabic has no separate word for my. It fastens ي, the yāʾ, onto the end of the noun — the habit that also fastens الـ for the. The suffixes are the grammar of the exchange, and the two written forms of your name are identical on the page.
There is no word for is either. Ismī Arun is literally my-name Arun — the zero copula, shared with the Dravidian languages.

The two words: أنت, split by the listener.
أنت is alif with a hamza seat, then nūn, then tāʾ — one skeleton for both genders. What splits them is a vowel mark that everyday text leaves off: أنتَ anta to a man, أنتِ anti to a woman.
The European and Dravidian tracks split you by register. Arabic splits it by the listener's gender instead — and the Semitic -ta / -ti ending is the same t that marks you in Hebrew atta / att.

The exchange.
تشرفنا tasharrafnā closes it — letters tāʾ, shīn, rāʾ, fāʾ, root sh–r–f, honour: sharaf (honour), sharīf (noble, the title behind English sheriff). The polite close is not pleased to meet you but we have been honoured.

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 4 seconds]
Name the Hebrew cousin of اسم. (Shem.) Say which axis Arabic uses to split أنت, where register would be the obvious choice. (The listener's gender.) Give the literal sense of تشرفنا. (We have been honoured.) And say where the gender of the question sits. (In the suffix — -ka to a man, -ki to a woman.)
