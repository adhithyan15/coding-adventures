# buenos días — "good days," and the little blessing inside

## Warm-up

[PAUSE 2s] You already "know" this means *good morning*. Set that aside for
four minutes — because taken apart, it doesn't quite mean that at all, and
the real story is better.

## You'll want to know first

- [ES-C01-L01 (hola)](./ES-C01-L01-hola.md) — the informal greeting this one
  is the *formal* counterpart to.

## Sounds you'll need

- `diphthong-ue` — **ue** in *bueno* is a quick *WEH*: *BWEH-no*. Two vowels
  run together into one glide. → [reference](../pronunciation-reference.md)
- `accent-i` — the written accent in *días* (**dí**) pulls the stress onto
  the *i*: *DEE-as*. Without the mark, the default rule would put it in the
  wrong place.

## The word, taken apart

**bueno** — good.
Root: Latin **bonus** ("good"). Here's the useful part: *bonus* had a
sibling, the adverb **bene** ("well") — the same ancient root, split long
ago into an adjective (*bonus*) and an adverb (*bene*). Spanish inherited the
adjective; English borrowed the *adverb*, and it's hiding in a whole shelf of
"well-" words you already own:

- **bene**volent — *bene* + *volens*, "wishing **well**"
- **bene**fit — *bene* + *facere*, "to do **well**"
- **bene**diction — *bene* + *dicere*, "to speak **well**" (a blessing)
- **bene**factor, **bene**ficial, and even *bonus*, *bonanza*, *bounty*
  (worn down through French).

So *bueno* is the Spanish half of a word whose English half lives in every
blessing and benefit you can name. (In *buenos días* it's *buenos* — plural
and masculine to match *días*; that agreement is its own later lesson, but
notice it's already happening.)

**días** — days.
Singular *día* ← Latin **dies** ("day"). The same *dies* fans out across
English, mostly through French:

- **di**ary, **di**urnal ("of the day")
- **jour**nal, **jour**ney (French *jour*, "a day" — a *journey* was
  originally "a day's travel")
- quoti**di**an ("every day"), meri**di**an ("mid-day")
- even *dismal* — from Latin *dies mali*, "evil days," the unlucky days of a
  medieval calendar.

## Why it's said this way

Here's the part Spanish 101 skips. Why *días* — **plural**? Why "good
**days**," not "good day"?

Because *buenos días* is a **fossil** — the worn-down stub of a longer
blessing: **"Buenos días os dé Dios"** — *"May God give you good days."* You
weren't wishing someone a single good morning; you were asking heaven to
grant them many good days ahead. The plural is the last surviving bone of
that prayer. (*Buenas tardes* and *buenas noches*, the next two lessons, are
the same — each an amputated blessing.)

> *This is the widely-accepted explanation; the exact history is debated —
> flagged as likely, not certain.*

And that's *why* it feels formal and warm where *hola* feels casual: it is,
almost literally, a small benediction (*bene-diction* — remember?). You're
not saying "morning!" — you're wishing someone well. The politeness is baked
into the words.
