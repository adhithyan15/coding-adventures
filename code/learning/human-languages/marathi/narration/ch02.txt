Lesson 1 of 6.
धन्यवाद (dhanyavad) (dhanyavād) — "thank you".

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, for letter shapes on the page. You can listen to everything else now — leave the section called The letters in this word until you have stopped, and I will say so again when we reach it.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
The formal "thanks" — Sanskrit, shared across the Indian languages.

The letters in this word.
(Skim if you read Devanagari.) New: ध dha (a breathy d), य ya, वा vā, द da, and the conjunct न्य (na + ya). Read ध, न्य, वा, द becomes dhanyavād.

The word, taken apart.
धन्यवाद (dhanyavad) (dhanyavād) = Sanskrit dhanya ("worthy") + vāda ("a saying") — "an utterance of '[you are] worthy.'" The same Sanskrit word behind Hindi dhanyavād, Kannada dhanyavāda, Telugu dhanyavādamulu. Warmer, more spoken Marathi often says आभारी आहे (ābhārī āhe, "[I] am grateful"), on the root ābhār, "gratitude."

Lesson 2 of 6.
हो (ho) — "yes".

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, for letter shapes on the page. You can listen to everything else now — leave the section called The letters in this word until you have stopped, and I will say so again when we reach it.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
A one-syllable "yes."

The letters in this word.
(Skim if you read Devanagari.) ह (ha) + the o-mātrā ो becomes हो (ho).

The word, taken apart.
हो (ho, "yes") is Marathi's plain affirmative — distinct from Hindi's hāṁ. Marathi builds its own everyday words on shared Indo-Aryan roots. The polite, fuller "yes" is ho, baraṃ āhe ("yes, it's fine").

Lesson 3 of 6.
बरं (baram) (baraṃ) — "okay, fine".

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, for letter shapes on the page. You can listen to everything else now — leave the section called The letters in this word until you have stopped, and I will say so again when we reach it.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
The verbal shrug of Marathi — "sure, fine, all right."

The letters in this word.
(Skim if you read Devanagari.) ब (ba) + रं (ra with the anusvāra dot ं, a light nasal) becomes baraṃ. That dot on top nasalises the vowel.

The word, taken apart.
बरं (baram) (baraṃ, "okay, fine, good, all right") is the oil of everyday Marathi talk — a soft "sure, fine." Its neutral form is bare. A genuinely Marathi word, not a Sanskrit loan — the kind of small, high-frequency word that gives a language its everyday texture.

Lesson 4 of 6.
नाही (nahi) (nāhī) — "no," a word older than English.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, for letter shapes on the page. You can listen to everything else now — leave the section called The letters in this word until you have stopped, and I will say so again when we reach it.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
The partner of ho — built on one of the oldest words in the whole Indo-European family.

The letters in this word.
(Skim if you read Devanagari.) ना (nā) + ही (hī) becomes nāhī.

The word, taken apart.
नाही (nahi) (nāhī, "no / is not") sits on the ancient negative na- — the same Proto-Indo-European ne behind English no, not, none, Latin non, and Hindi nahīṁ. So Marathi's "no" and English's "no" are cousins from before either language existed. It doubles as "there isn't": Marathi, like Hindi, denies with this one word.

Lesson 5 of 6.
येतो / येते (yeto / yete) — "I'll take my leave".

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, for letter shapes on the page. You can listen to everything else now — leave the section called The letters in this word until you have stopped, and I will say so again when we reach it.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
The Marathi goodbye — which, like the Dravidian languages, refuses to say a bare "I'm leaving." And it changes with the speaker's gender.

The letters in this word.
(Skim if you read Devanagari.) ये (ye) + तो (to) becomes yeto; ये + ते (te) becomes yete.

The word, taken apart.
Marathi, like the Dravidian languages to its south, will not say a bare "I'm leaving." The everyday goodbye is येतो (yeto) — literally "I come [again]," a promise of return, never a final departure. (Fuller: yeto, namaskār.)

Grammar Lens: Marathi verbs carry gender.
yeto is what a man says; a woman says येते (yete). The verb itself changes with the speaker's gender — something Hindi does only in the past tense, but Marathi does in the present too. (And Marathi keeps three genders — masculine, feminine, and neuter — where Hindi kept only two.) You'll meet this at every verb.

Lesson 6 of 6.
Chapter 2 — courtesy, responses, and leave-taking.

Warm-up.
[a table of 6 rows, read aloud]
Marathi: नमस्कार, namaskār. Meaning: hello / goodbye.
Marathi: धन्यवाद (dhanyavad), dhanyavād. Meaning: thank you.
Marathi: हो, ho. Meaning: yes.
Marathi: नाही (nahi), nāhī. Meaning: no.
Marathi: बरं (baram), baraṃ. Meaning: okay / fine.
Marathi: येतो / येते, yeto / yete. Meaning: "I'll be going" (m./f.)

What you've built.
It prefers namaskār as the everyday greeting (Hindi leans on namaste).
It keeps three genders — masculine, feminine, neuter — where Hindi kept two.
It marks gender on the verb, even in the present: yeto (m.) / yete (f.).
It has an extra letter, ळ (retroflex ḷ), shared with the Dravidian south and absent from Hindi.
Shared with Hindi/Sanskrit: the Devanagari script, the negative nā- ( from PIE ne, English no), and much formal vocabulary (dhanyavād).
