Urdu, chapter 31: The Ten Urdu Digits.
5 lessons. The first lesson already needs your eyes or your hands, so save this one for when you have stopped.


Lesson 1 of 5.
۰ ۱ (sifr ek) — two digits, and a direction that reverses.

Before we start: this one needs your hands, so it is not a driving lesson. You will want a pen and something to write on and your eyes, for letter shapes on the page. You can listen to everything else now — leave the sections called Script — the shapes, and the direction and Guided Practice until you have stopped, and I will say so again when we reach it.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Which way does an Urdu line run? Count aloud to ten.

Script — the shapes, and the direction.
۰ — sifr, zero ۱ — ek, one.
Urdu has its own ten digit signs, and a price ticket, a bus number, a page number and a telephone number in Pakistan are written in them. A reader who can say ek and cannot read ۱ still cannot read a price.
۰ is a dot, not a ring. Fix that first: a European eye reads a dot as a decimal point, and Urdu's nine is the ring.
Now the part no earlier chapter of this book has had to say. Numbers run left to right, inside a line that runs right to left. Ten is ۱۰ — one, then zero, in the order English uses — while the words on either side of it move the other way. The page changes direction at the number and changes back after it.

Guided Practice.
[once you have stopped driving — write: ۰ — a dot; ۱ — a stroke]
[your turn — read: ۱۰ — das, ten, left to right]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.

Lesson 2 of 5.
۲ ۳ (do tīn) — one tooth apart.

Before we start: this one needs your hands, so it is not a driving lesson. You will want a pen and something to write on and your eyes, for letter shapes on the page. You can listen to everything else now — leave the sections called Script — the shapes and Guided Practice until you have stopped, and I will say so again when we reach it.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Write ۰ and ۱, then read ۱۰.

Script — the shapes.
۲ — do, two ۳ — tīn, three.
۳ is ۲ with one more tooth, and that is the whole difference. It is small enough that the two have to be written as a pair rather than one at a time: say do and tīn aloud as the hand makes them, and the shapes stop competing.

Lesson 3 of 5.
۴ ۵ ۶ (chār pānch chhe) — the three Urdu keeps for itself.

Before we start: this one needs your hands, so it is not a driving lesson. You will want a pen and something to write on and your eyes, for letter shapes on the page. You can listen to everything else now — leave the sections called Script — the shapes and Guided Practice until you have stopped, and I will say so again when we reach it.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Write ۲ and ۳, and say which has the extra tooth.

Script — the shapes.
۴ — chār, four ۵ — pānch, five ۶ — chhe, six.
These three are why this chapter exists as more than a list.
Urdu and Arabic share an alphabet, letter for letter, and a reader of this book has spent twenty-five chapters on that shared inheritance. The digits are where the sharing stops. Seven of the ten are drawn the same way in both; ۴, ۵ and ۶ are not, and Arabic writes those three with quite different shapes.
So a reader who picked the digits up from an Arabic book will misread a Pakistani price ticket at exactly these three places and nowhere else. If you drill three of the ten harder than the rest, drill these.

[your turn — read: ۱۴, ۱۵, ۱۶, left to right]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Lesson 4 of 5.
۷ ۸ ۹ (sāt āṭh nau) — a mirror pair, and the ring.

Before we start: this one needs your hands, so it is not a driving lesson. You will want a pen and something to write on and your eyes, for letter shapes on the page. You can listen to everything else now — leave the sections called Script — the shapes and Guided Practice until you have stopped, and I will say so again when we reach it.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Write ۴, ۵, ۶ — the three Arabic draws differently.

Script — the shapes.
۷ — sāt, seven ۸ — āṭh, eight ۹ — nau, nine.
۷ and ۸ are mirror images of each other, one opening upward and one downward. Write them as a pair, every time, and the mirror stops being a problem — the same trick that worked on ۲ and ۳.
۹ is a ring with a tail, and this is where a dot-shaped zero earns its keep. Had ۰ been a ring too, the page would carry two rings and no way to separate them.
Ten signs, four lessons, and the set is closed.

Guided Practice.
[once you have stopped driving — write: ۷ and ۸ as a pair, then ۹]
[your turn — run: ۰ ۱ (sifr ek) ۲ ۳ (do tīn) ۴ ۵ ۶ (chār pānch chhe) ۷ ۸ ۹ (sāt āṭh nau) (the ten Urdu digits) once, slowly]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Lesson 5 of 5.
Payoff — reading the figures.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Count aloud to ten, then say which way digits run inside an Urdu line.

Guided Practice.
Read all ten signs shuffled and say each as an Urdu word.
Read ۱۰, ۲۰ and ۱۰۰ and say each — das, bīs, sau.
Hear ten amounts named and write each in Urdu figures.
Write the ten signs from dictation, out of order, checking ۴, ۵ and ۶ against the models rather than against memory.
Read a seven-figure telephone number aloud, one digit at a time.
An A1 examiner asks for an age, a price and a telephone number. All three are now reachable. What is not: ۱۱ to ۱۹, whose words are nine irregular forms rather than a rule, so the figure can be read and its name cannot yet be said.

Wrap-up Recall.
Sources: Unicode Arabic chart; Omniglot, Numbers in Urdu.
[question — say your answer, then pause 16 seconds]
Write a hundred in Urdu figures, and say its name.
