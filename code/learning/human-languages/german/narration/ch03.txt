German, chapter 3: How Are You?.
8 lessons. All 8 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 8.
danke — "thank you," which is really "I think of you".

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
You can introduce yourself in German. Next, how are you? — and its answers all lean on danke. It hides a secret that English shares but hides too: to thank is to think.

Sounds you'll need.
vowel-a-german — danke = DAHN-kuh, a pure open ah, final -e a soft uh. Stress the front: DAHN-kuh.

The word, taken apart.
danke comes from denken, "to think." The old idea: to thank someone is to keep them in mind — "I think of you" / "you are in my thoughts." German Dank ("thanks") and Gedanke ("a thought") are the same root wearing two coats.
And here is the payoff for an English speaker: English "thank" and "think" are the same word too. Old English þancian ("thank") and þencan ("think") split from one root — to thank was to hold in thought. So German danke isn't foreign at all; it's English thank with the think still visible inside it.
Across the family, three different pictures of gratitude — worth holding side by side:
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
language: German. "thanks": danke. literal idea: "I think of you".
language: French. "thanks": merci. literal idea: a reward / mercy.
language: Spanish. "thanks": gracias. literal idea: grace / favour.

Grammar Lens: dressing it up.
danke schön — "thank you nicely" (schön = beautiful/nice) becomes "thank you very much."
danke sehr — "thanks very."
vielen Dank — "many thanks" (you'll meet viel, "much," later).

[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
What everyday verb is danke built from? (denken, "to think" — to thank is to think of someone.) What two English words share that same split? (Thank and think.) Next: the word that means both "please" and "you're welcome" — bitte.


Lesson 2 of 8.
bitte — the Swiss-army word: please and you're welcome.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
If German gives you one word to overwork, it's bitte. It's "please." It's also "you're welcome." Also "here you go," and even "pardon?" One word, and it comes straight from asking.

Sounds you'll need.
bitte = BIT-tuh — short crisp i, a clear double-t, soft -uh end.

The word, taken apart.
bitte is the plea-form of bitten, "to ask, to request, to pray." When you say bitte you are, at root, making a request — "I ask (this of you)."
That verb bitten is a close cousin of two English words:
bid — as in "I bid you welcome," "do as you're bidden" — to ask/command.
bead — astonishingly, yes: a bead was originally a prayer. People counted their prayers (Old English gebed) on a string, and the name of the prayer slid onto the little ball you counted it with. So a rosary bead is a frozen request — exactly what bitte still is.
How one word covers so much.
[a table of 4 rows, read aloud]
you say bitte when…: making a request. it means: please. why (it's all "I ask"): "I ask you…"
you say bitte when…: answering danke. it means: you're welcome. why (it's all "I ask"): "(don't mention it,) I ask nothing back".
you say bitte when…: handing something over. it means: here you go. why (it's all "I ask"): "I offer, at your request".
you say bitte when…: you didn't hear. it means: pardon? why (it's all "I ask"): "I ask you to repeat".
For this chapter, the key pairing is the courtesy loop: Danke. — Bitte. ("Thanks." — "You're welcome.") — the German twin of de nada / de rien.

Guided Practice.

[your turn — say: "danke" … "bitte" — the full thanks/you're-welcome exchange]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: "bitte" as please, then as you're welcome — same word, tone does the work]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: German bitte corresponds to English bid / bead — a prayer you can hold]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
What verb is bitte from? (bitten, "to ask / to pray.") What two English words are its cousins? (Bid and bead — a bead was a prayer.) Name two different jobs bitte does. (please; you're welcome; here-you-go; pardon.) Next: the verb behind "how are you?" — and like French, it's to go.


Lesson 3 of 8.
gehen — "to go," and it's just English go.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Spanish asks how are you? with estar ("to stand"). French and German both ask it with "to go" — how does it go? You already own the German verb without studying it, because it is English go.

Sounds you'll need.
h-pronounced — in gehen the h just lengthens the vowel (it isn't a breathy English h): GAY-en. The form you need most is geht = gayt.

The word, taken apart.
gehen = "to go." It and English go descend from the same Germanic verb (gān / gehen) — no Latin detour, no false friend, a straight cognate. When you say geht, you're saying goes.
German states its wellbeing as motion, exactly like French ça va:
Es geht mir gut. = literally "It goes for-me well." becomes "I'm doing well."
es = "it" (cognate of English it).
geht = "goes."
mir = "to/for me" (the dative — the "to-whom" form; you'll meet its partner dir, "to you," next lesson).
gut = "well" (Chapter 1 — cognate of English good).
So German literally says "it goes well to me." Wellbeing is something that goes, and it goes to a person.

Grammar Lens: the dative, gently.
German marks the person something happens to with a special form: ich ("I") becomes mir ("to me"); du ("you") becomes dir ("to you"). "How are you?" will use dir / Ihnen — "how does it go to you?" Just recognize mir = "to me" for now.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: "gehen" (GAY-en), then "geht" (gayt) = goes]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: "Es geht mir gut" — it goes well to me]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: German gehen = English go, same word]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
What English word is gehen, at the root? (Go — a straight cognate.) Es geht mir gut literally says what? ("It goes well to me.") German and French use "to go" for wellbeing — which verb does Spanish use instead? (estar, "to stand.") Next: ask it — Wie geht es dir?


Lesson 4 of 8.
Wie geht es dir? — “how are you?”

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Combine wie, “how,” and gehen, “to go,” into the everyday question for a friend.
Wie geht es dir? — “How are you?” literally: “How goes it to you?”
wie = how;
geht = goes;
es = it;
dir = to you, the dative form paired with du.
In speech, geht es contracts to geht’s. The short Wie geht’s? is the most useful neutral-sounding everyday form.
Answer with the gut you already know:
Wie geht’s? — Danke, gut!
German pictures wellbeing as going, like English “how’s it going?” French uses the same metaphor; Spanish often uses a “standing” verb. The comparison is a memory aid, not required knowledge.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: “Wie geht es dir?”]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: the contraction — “Wie geht’s?”]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: “Danke, gut! Und dir?”]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[repeat that twice]
“Wie geht’s? — Danke, gut!”

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
What does the question literally ask? (“How goes it to you?”) Which word means “to you”? (Dir.) What is the short form? (Wie geht’s?) How can you answer? (Danke, gut.) Next: switch from dir to formal Ihnen.


Lesson 5 of 8.
Wie geht es Ihnen? — formal “how are you?”

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
The informal question ends in dir, “to you.” For a stranger or elder, keep the whole sentence and change only that listener word.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
listener: friend or family. question: Wie geht es dir?
listener: stranger or elder. question: Wie geht es Ihnen?
Capitalised Ihnen is the dative form that belongs with formal Sie. The question still means “How goes it to you?”; German changes register inside the dative pronoun rather than changing the metaphor.
Bounce the question back with the same contrast:
informal: Und dir? — and you?
formal: Und Ihnen? — and you?
The short Wie geht’s? avoids the final pronoun and can be useful when the relationship is already clear.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: to a friend — “Wie geht es dir?”]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: to a stranger — “Wie geht es Ihnen?”]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: “Danke, gut. Und Ihnen?”]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[repeat that twice]
“Dir — informal. Ihnen — formal.”

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Which word means informal “to you”? (Dir.) Which is formal? (Ihnen, capitalised.) What stays the same? (Wie geht es and the “go” metaphor.) Next: practise the casual exchange before the formal one.


Lesson 6 of 8.
es geht — the German shrug: "it goes".

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Someone asks Wie geht's? You're neither great nor terrible. German's answer is beautifully understated: es geht — just "it goes." Not well, not badly — it simply goes. That bareness is the shrug.

Review first (20 seconds).
[pause 2 seconds]
From Chapter 1: gut — "well / good" (cognate of English good). So Es geht mir gut = "it goes well for me." Now drop the gut, and watch what happens to the meaning.

The word, taken apart.
es geht = literally "it goes." In the last lesson you added gut to make it positive. Say it with nothing added — "es geht" — and the very absence of a "well" or "badly" makes it mean "eh, so-so, it's going, I'm getting by." The understatement carries the whole meaning.
Your German answer ladder for Wie geht's?:
[a table of 4 rows, read aloud]
Sehr gut! / Gut, danke means very well / well, thanks.
Es geht means so-so, okay-ish.
So lala means so-so (a sing-song "meh"; lala is just a filler singsong).
Nicht so gut means not so well.
And the cross-track shrug collection, now four deep:
German es geht ("it goes"), French comme ci, comme ça ("like this, like that"), Spanish más o menos ("more or less") / regular, Italian così così ("so so").

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: "es geht" — flat, understated, a shrug in the voice]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: answer "Wie geht's?" three ways — "Gut!" / "Es geht" / "So lala"]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: the four shrugs — es geht / comme ci comme ça / más o menos / così così]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Why does bare es geht ("it goes") mean "so-so"? (Because it adds no well or badly — the understatement is the shrug.) What do you add to make it positive? (gut becomes es geht mir gut.) Next chapter: farewells — auf Wiedersehen, tschüss, bis bald.


Lesson 7 of 8.
Practice — Wie geht’s?, casually.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Assemble the chapter’s atoms into one exchange with a friend. No new words.
— Hallo! Wie geht’s? — Gut, danke, und dir? — Es geht. — [after a favour] Danke! — Bitte.
The dative dir remains because the full question means “How goes it to you?” The shortened und dir? keeps that same grammar.

What you are retrieving.
danke — thanks, related to denken, “think”;
bitte — please / you’re welcome, from bitten, “ask”;
wie geht’s? — how are you?;
gut / es geht — well / so-so.

[your turn — say: “Wie geht’s? — Gut, danke.”]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

“Wie geht’s? — Gut, danke, und dir?”

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Run the casual exchange without looking. Which answer is neutral or so-so? (Es geht.) Which word answers thanks? (Bitte.) Why dir? (The question is literally “How goes it to you?”) Next: perform the formal version.


Lesson 8 of 8.
Formal practice — Wie geht es Ihnen?

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Keep the casual exchange’s structure and replace dir with formal Ihnen.
— Guten Tag. Wie geht es Ihnen? — Danke, gut. Und Ihnen? — Es geht. — [after a favour] Danke! — Bitte.
The capital letter matters: Ihnen belongs to respectful Sie. The verb phrase does not change; only the dative listener word does.

[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

[your turn — say: “Und Ihnen? — Es geht.”]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[repeat that twice]
“Wie geht es Ihnen? — Danke, gut. Und Ihnen?”

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Which greeting opens the formal exchange? (Guten Tag.) Which dative pronoun appears twice? (Ihnen.) What changes from the casual version? (Dir becomes Ihnen.) You can now run the Chapter 3 exchange in either register.
