# सोमवार से शुक्रवार तक — a span, marked at both ends

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[HI-CONCEPT-C10-SOMAVAAR-SHUKRAVAAR-01, HI-LEX-C94-SPAN-01] -->

[PAUSE 3s] Say the first and last weekdays of the working week. Then say the
word that marks a stopping point.

## You'll want to know: से … तक
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[HI-LEX-C94-SPAN-02]; assesses=[] -->

**सोमवार से शुक्रवार तक** — from Monday to Friday.

Two days, and **each one carries its own marker**.

## The two words
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[HI-LEX-C94-SPAN-02, HI-LEX-C94-SPAN-01] -->

| piece | job |
|---|---|
| सोमवार **से** | start here |
| शुक्रवार **तक** | stop here |

English builds a span out of *from* and *to* standing **in front of** their
days. Hindi builds it out of **से** and **तक** standing **behind** theirs.

The shape looks harder than it is, because both halves are already familiar and
neither is doing anything new. What is new is only that **they are allowed to
appear together**, and that when they do, the whole thing is one idea rather
than two.

## Grammar Lens: order is not optional
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[HI-LEX-C94-SPAN-02, HI-CONCEPT-C10-SOMAVAAR-SHUKRAVAAR-01] -->

The **से** half comes first. Always, and for the same reason English says *from
Monday to Friday* and not the other way round: a span is told in the direction
it runs.

What Hindi does **not** copy is the position of the markers. Read the pair as
two small packages rather than as one long phrase:

**[ सोमवार से ] [ शुक्रवार तक ]**

Read it that way and a longer span — a written account of somebody's week —
comes apart at the right seams the first time.
