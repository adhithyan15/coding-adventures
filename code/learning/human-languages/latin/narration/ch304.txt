Latin, chapter 304: Fūr comprehēnsus.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
effringō, effringere — to break open.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a robber, then the Latin for loot.

You'll want to know: effringō, effringere.
effringō, effringere — "to break open".
Fūr portam effrēgit et in domum intrāvit. The thief broke the door open and went into the house.
effringō, effringere, effrēgī, effrāctum: ex + frangō (taught). Plautus has doors smashed in this way.

The word, taken apart.
effringō is built on frangō, "to break", with ex-, "out" (Lewis & Short); the vowel changes inside the compound.

Lesson 2 of 5.
claustrum, claustrī — a bolt, a bar; a barrier.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for loot, then the Latin for to break open.

You'll want to know: claustrum, claustrī.
claustrum, claustrī — "a bolt, a bar; a barrier".
Fūr claustra portae nocte remōvit. At night the thief removed the bolts of the door.
Neuter, from claudō, 'to shut' (taught); most often plural, claustra, the bars of a door or gate. English cloister.

The word, taken apart.
claustrum is built on claudō, "to shut" (EtymDB), with a tool ending: the thing that shuts. English cloister comes from it through French, and claustrophobia is built on it.

Lesson 3 of 5.
rapiō, rapere — to snatch, to carry off.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to break open, then the Latin for a bolt.

You'll want to know: rapiō, rapere.
rapiō, rapere — "to snatch, to carry off".
Fūr crumēnam meam rapuit et fūgit. The thief snatched my purse and ran away.
rapiō, rapere, rapuī, raptum. English rapid and rapture come from it.

The word, taken apart.
rapiō is inherited from Proto-Italic rapiō (EtymDB). English rapid comes from rapidus, "snatching, swift", and rapture from its participle.

Lesson 4 of 5.
dēprehendō, dēprehendere — to catch, to catch in the act.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a bolt, then the Latin for to snatch.

You'll want to know: dēprehendō, dēprehendere.
dēprehendō, dēprehendere — "to catch, to catch in the act".
Servus fūrem in hortō dēprehendit. A slave caught the thief in the garden.
dēprehendō, dēprehendere, dēprehendī, dēprehēnsum, also dēprendō. In fūrtō dēprehendere: to catch someone stealing.

The word, taken apart.
dēprehendō is built on prehendō, "to seize", with dē- (Lewis & Short): to snatch away, to catch something on the move.

Lesson 5 of 5.
comprehendō, comprehendere — to seize, to arrest; to grasp.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to snatch, then the Latin for to catch.

You'll want to know: comprehendō, comprehendere.
comprehendō, comprehendere — "to seize, to arrest; to grasp".
Mīlitēs latrōnem comprehendērunt et in carcerem dūxērunt. The soldiers arrested the bandit and took him to prison.
comprehendō, comprehendere, comprehendī, comprehēnsum. To seize a person, and also to grasp an idea. English comprehend.

The word, taken apart.
comprehendō is built on prehendō, "to seize", with con-, "together, wholly" (EtymDB): to take hold of on all sides. English comprehend comes from it.
