Latin, chapter 469: Anvil, Bellows, Forge, Blacksmith, To hammer.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
incūs, incūdis — an anvil.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a paint, then the Latin for a paintbrush.

You'll want to know: incūs, incūdis.
incūs, incūdis — "an anvil".
Faber ferrum calidum in incūde pōnit. The smith puts the hot iron on the anvil.
Third declension, feminine: incūs, incūdis f. Ad incūdem redīre, to go back to the anvil, to rework a piece of writing. English incus is a bone of the ear, named for its shape.

The word, taken apart.
incūs is built on incūdō 'to forge on' (EtymDB; Lewis & Short), from in 'on' and cūdō 'to strike, to hammer': the block that iron is struck on.

Lesson 2 of 5.
follis, follis — a pair of bellows; a leather bag.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a paintbrush, then the Latin for an anvil.

You'll want to know: follis, follis.
follis, follis — "a pair of bellows; a leather bag".
Servus folle ignem auget. The slave makes the fire bigger with the bellows.
Third declension, masculine: follis, follis m. Also a blown-up leather ball and a money bag. English fool comes from it through French, from 'windbag'.

The word, taken apart.
follis is inherited from PIE bʰolǵʰnis (EtymDB); Lewis & Short compare flō 'to blow'. English fool comes from it through Old French fol, from the sense 'windbag'.

Lesson 3 of 5.
camīnus, camīnī — a forge, a furnace; a fireplace.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for an anvil, then the Latin for a pair of bellows.

You'll want to know: camīnus, camīnī.
camīnus, camīnī — "a forge, a furnace; a fireplace".
In camīnō ignis magnus ardet. A big fire burns in the forge.
Second declension, masculine: camīnus, camīnī m. A smith's forge, or a stove for heating a room. English chimney comes from a word built on it.

The word, taken apart.
camīnus was borrowed from Greek kamīnos 'furnace' (EtymDB; Lewis & Short). English chimney comes, through French, from Late Latin camīnāta, built on it.

Lesson 4 of 5.
ferrārius, ferrāriī — a blacksmith, an ironworker.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a pair of bellows, then the Latin for a forge.

You'll want to know: ferrārius, ferrāriī.
ferrārius, ferrāriī — "a blacksmith, an ironworker".
Ferrārius soleās ferreās equīs facit. The blacksmith makes iron shoes for the horses.
Second declension, masculine: ferrārius, ferrāriī m., short for faber ferrārius. English farrier comes from it through French.

The word, taken apart.
ferrārius is built on ferrum 'iron' with the suffix -ārius (EtymDB; Lewis & Short). English farrier, a smith who shoes horses, comes from it through French.

Lesson 5 of 5.
cūdō, cūdere — to hammer, to forge; to strike (coins).

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a forge, then the Latin for a blacksmith.

You'll want to know: cūdō, cūdere.
cūdō, cūdere — "to hammer, to forge; to strike (coins)".
Faber ferrum in incūde cūdit. The smith hammers the iron on the anvil.
cūdō, cūdere, cūdī, cūsum. Nummōs cūdere, to strike coins.

The word, taken apart.
cūdō is inherited from Proto-Italic kūdō 'to stamp, to pound', from PIE kewh₂- 'to strike, to hew' (EtymDB). English hew is a distant Germanic cousin.
