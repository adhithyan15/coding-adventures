Mandarin Chinese, chapter 228: A Headache.
7 lessons. You can do the first one in the car; after that you will want to have stopped.


Lesson 1 of 7.
头疼 (tóuténg) — having a headache; aching in the head.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Mandarin for a sandy beach, then the Mandarin for to run into someone.

You'll want to know: 头疼.
头疼 — tóuténg — "having a headache; aching in the head".
我头疼不想吃饭 wǒ tóuténg bù xiǎng chīfàn — I've got a headache, so I don't feel like eating.
The new character, in pieces: 头 (tóu) — five strokes: two dots, a long bar, a long stroke falling to the left, and a dot at the lower right; 疼 (téng) — ten strokes: the illness frame of 病, five strokes, then inside it five more: a short fall to the left, a bar that turns and falls to the left, a long stroke to the right, and two dots. The next lesson puts it in your hand.

The word, taken apart.
头疼 (tóuténg) is built from 头 (tóu) 'head' and 疼 (téng) 'sore', both inherited from Middle Chinese, duw and downg (Guangyun, in Baxter's transcription), which glosses the first 'the head' and the second 'pain'.

Script — two dots over a shape like 大.
头 (tóu).
Two pieces: two dots, then a shape like 大.
Two dots, one under the other, each falling to the right, a little left of the middle.
Then three strokes, almost 大: a long bar across, then a long stroke from the top falling down to the left. The last stroke is not the long stroke of 大 but a short dot at the lower right.
2 + 3 = five.

[once you have stopped driving — this part needs your eyes or your hands; if you are driving, skip ahead to the next part]
Script — the frame of 病, and a shape of five inside.
疼 (téng).
Two pieces: a frame, and what sits inside it. Count the pieces, not the strokes.
The frame, five strokes, as in 病: a dot at the top, a bar across, a long stroke falling to the left, then a small dot and a short stroke rising to the right on the far left.
Inside, five strokes. A short stroke falling to the left. A bar that turns and falls to the left. A long stroke falling to the right. Then two dots, one under the other.
5 + 5 = ten.

Lesson 4 of 7.
chīyào — to take medicine.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Mandarin for to run into someone, then the Mandarin for a headache.

You'll want to know: chīyào.
chīyào — "to take medicine".
nǐ chīyào le ma — Have you taken your medicine?
Its characters come later; for now it is a word for the ear.

The word, taken apart.
chīyào is built from 吃 'to eat' and yào 'medicine'. The second is inherited from Middle Chinese yak (Guangyun, in Baxter's transcription), explained, after an older book, as a plant that cures illness. The dictionary gives the first, kj+t there, only for stammering, so how it came to mean eating is not settled, and chī is not the regular outcome of that form.

我问他头疼不疼他点头了 wǒ wèn tā tóu téng bu téng tā diǎntóu le — I asked if his head hurt, and he nodded.

The word, taken apart.
点头 (diǎntóu) is built from 点 'to dot' and 头 (tóu) 'head', both inherited from Middle Chinese, temX and duw (Guangyun, in Baxter's transcription). The dictionary glosses the first 'dots and strokes'; 'to dip' is not given there.
