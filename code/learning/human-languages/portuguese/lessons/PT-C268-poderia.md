# poderia, gostaria, deveria — the would of Portuguese

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[PT-LEX-C83-QUERER83-02, PT-LEX-DEVIA-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say "I would like". (*Gostaria.*) Say "I ought to rest", the
careful way. (*Deveria descansar.*)

[PAUSE 1s] Two forms of one tense. This lesson gives it its name and its recipe.

## Grammar Lens: the whole infinitive, plus -ia
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[PT-GRAMMAR-CONDICIONAL-01]; assesses=[PT-GRAMMAR-IMPERFEITO-01, PT-LEX-C82-PODER82-01, PT-LEX-AJUDAR-02, PT-LEX-C223-COISAS223-03, PT-LEX-DIZER-02, PT-LEX-TRAZER-02] -->

This is the **condicional**, Portuguese's "would". Keep the **whole**
infinitive and add **-ia** for "I":

> falar, **falaria** — I would speak
> comer, **comeria** — I would eat
> partir, **partiria** — I would leave

The other persons change only the ending: **falaria**, **falarias**,
**falaria**, **falaríamos**, **falariam**. Those are the imperfeito's *-ia*
endings, but glued to the whole infinitive: *comia*, I was eating; *comeria*,
I would eat.

Three common verbs squeeze their infinitive first: *fazer* gives **faria**,
*dizer* **diria**, *trazer* **traria**. *Poder*, *gostar* and *dever* follow
the plain recipe: **poderia**, **gostaria**, **deveria**.

Three jobs, all of them about softening. A **polite request**: *Poderia
ajudar-me?* — Could you help me? A **wish**: *Gostaria de viajar.* And
**advice**: *No teu lugar, estudaria.* — In your place, I'd study.

In everyday European speech the imperfeito often stands in for it, as *queria*
did at the ticket window: *podia ajudar-me?*, *gostava de viajar*. Both are
right. Notice also the *me* after the verb in *ajudar-me*; Brazil puts it
first.

## The word, taken apart: "I had to speak"
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[PT-ETYMON-CONDICIONAL-02]; assesses=[PT-LEX-HAVIA-02] -->

Latin had no would-tense. Spoken Latin made one by gluing a past of *habēre*,
"to have", onto the infinitive: roughly "I had to speak", which slid into "I
would speak". Portuguese and Spanish chose the **imperfect** of that verb,
*habēbat*, the same past that became **havia**; worn down on the end of a verb,
it is the **-ia** of *falaria*. Italian glued on the perfect instead, which is
why its endings do not match. Neither EtymDB nor Lewis & Short covers a change
this late; this is the usual explanation in histories of Romance, given as
that. The name **condicional** comes from Medieval Latin
*conditiōnālis* (EtymDB 2.1).
