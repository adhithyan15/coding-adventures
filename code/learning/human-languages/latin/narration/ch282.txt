Latin, chapter 282: A Story in Order.
4 lessons. You can do the first 2 of them in the car; after that you will want to have stopped.


Lesson 1 of 4.
prīmum, deinde, tum, postrēmō — the rungs of a story.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say "I came" and "I saw". (Vēnī. Vīdī.) The verb for "to win" is vincō, and its perfect is vīcī, "I won".
[pause 1 second]
Suetonius reports that when Caesar celebrated his victory in Pontus, a placard carried three words: vēnī, vīdī, vīcī. Three events, three perfects, and not one joining word.

Grammar Lens: four rungs.
Caesar could leave the order bare because three short perfects explain themselves. A longer story names its rungs, with four words you already own:
Prīmum ad forum ambulāvī. — First I walked to the forum. Deinde amīcum vīdī. — Next I saw a friend. Tum in caupōnam intrāvimus. — Then we went into the inn. Postrēmō domum ambulāvī. — Last of all I walked home.
[pause 2 seconds]
Each rung carries a perfect, because each is an event.
[pause 2 seconds]
Deinde and tum both come out as "then", and they are not twins. Deinde is "next", the following step. Tum is "then, at that moment": it points at a time, and the scene can stand there with it. Tum pluēbat, at that moment it was raining.

The word, taken apart: prīmum.
prīmum is the neuter of prīmus, "first", standing alone as an adverb (Lewis & Short list it under primus): "as the first thing". Postrēmō comes from postrēmus, "last", too, though it is the ablative, "at the last", and deinde is built from dē and inde, "from there": from that point on.

[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
"First", "next", "then", "lastly"? (Prīmum, deinde, tum, postrēmō.) Which "then" points at a moment and can hold a scene? (Tum.) Which tense goes on each rung? (The perfect: each is an event.) What did Caesar's three words say? (Vēnī, vīdī, vīcī: I came, I saw, I won.)


Lesson 2 of 4.
dum — while.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say "first I walked to the forum". (Prīmum ad forum ambulāvī.) Then a scene: "it was raining". (Pluēbat.)

You'll want to know: dum.
dum — "while". It opens a stretch of time, and something happens inside it:
Dum in forō ambulant, amīcam vīdērunt. — While they were walking in the forum, they saw a friend.
[pause 2 seconds]
The seeing is an event, so it is a perfect, vīdērunt. The walking is the stretch it happened in, and dum holds it open.

Grammar Lens: dum keeps the present.
Listen to ambulant. The story is in the past, and the verb after dum is the present: "they walk". When dum means "while", a past story usually keeps the present after it, and English turns it into "were walking".
Dum mercātōrēs pānem vendunt, amīca vēnit. — While the merchants were selling bread, my friend came.
[pause 2 seconds]
Caesar writes it that way: dum haec geruntur, "while these things are being done", and then the event in the perfect (Lewis & Short quote it from his war in Gaul). In the main sentence, the scene still takes the imperfect: mercātōrēs pānem vendēbant.

The word, taken apart: dum.
The origin of dum is not settled. Lewis & Short connect it with diū, "for a long time", the word whose own lesson traced it to the root of diēs, "day"; EtymDB 2.1 traces dum to a Proto-Indo-European particle de. Either way it is a small, old word, and its meaning has stayed "for the stretch of time that".

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
What does dum mean? (While.) In a past story, which tense follows dum? (The present: dum ambulant.) And the event inside the stretch? (The perfect: vīdērunt.) Is the origin of dum settled? (No.)


Lesson 3 of 4.
(reading) — a rainy day, told in both pasts.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, to read the printed text yourself. The questions are about what you read, so save the whole lesson for when you have stopped.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Your first passage gave you forty-two words in a row, all in the present. This one is twice as long, and it happened yesterday.
[pause 1 second]
Every word in it has come up before this lesson.

[once you have stopped driving — this part needs your eyes or your hands; if you are driving, skip ahead to the next part]
Reading.
Herī māne caelum obscūrum erat, et pluēbat. Mārcus in vīllā sedēbat et librum legēbat. Subitō amīcus ad portam vēnit et clāmāvit: "Venī ad forum!" Prīmum Mārcus nōn volēbat, quia fessus erat. Deinde tamen vēnit, et ad forum ambulāvērunt. Dum ambulant, amīcam vīdērunt, et amīca quoque vēnit. In forō mercātōrēs pānem et vīnum vendēbant et multum clāmābant. Tum amīcī in caupōnam intrāvērunt. In caupōnā calor erat. Ibi cēnāvērunt et vīnum bibērunt, et amīca fābulam nārrāvit. Postrēmō domum ambulāvērunt. Iam nōn pluēbat, caelum serēnum erat, et Mārcus laetus erat.
[pause 2 seconds]
[once you have stopped driving — read: the passage once without stopping; do not translate as you go]
[pause 2 seconds]
[once you have stopped driving — read: the passage again, and sort every verb into one of two piles: the scene, or what happened]

Why it's said this way.
[pause 2 seconds]
The events pile is the perfects: vēnit, clāmāvit, vēnit, ambulāvērunt, vīdērunt, vēnit, intrāvērunt, cēnāvērunt, bibērunt, nārrāvit, ambulāvērunt. Eleven things happened, and prīmum, deinde, tum and postrēmō keep them in order.
[pause 2 seconds]
Everything else is scene: the dark sky, the rain, Marcus reading, the merchants shouting, the warmth of the inn. A story is mostly background.
[pause 2 seconds]
And dum ambulant is a present inside a past story, the stretch of time the meeting happened in.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: the passage aloud, once, without stopping]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[question — say your answer, then pause 15 seconds]
Why did Marcus not want to come at first?

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 2 seconds]
Why is legēbat in the imperfect? (The reading was already going on when the friend came: it is scene.)
[pause 2 seconds]
Why is ambulant in the present? (It follows dum.)
[pause 2 seconds]
How did the day end? (Iam nōn pluēbat: the rain had stopped, and Marcus was happy.)


Lesson 4 of 4.
Review — a story, told in order.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say Caesar's three words. (Vēnī, vīdī, vīcī.) Then the four rungs. (Prīmum, deinde, tum, postrēmō.)

What you've built.
Two chapters, and you can nārrāre, tell a story. The scene in the imperfect, erat, pluēbat, sedēbat. The events in the perfect, one per rung, from prīmum to postrēmō. And dum with a present for the stretch an event happened in.
Dum in forō ambulō, amīcum vīdī. Prīmum nōn clāmāvī, quia fessus eram. — While I was walking in the forum, I saw a friend. At first I did not call out, because I was tired.
[pause 2 seconds]
In that last sentence the event is negative, nōn clāmāvī, and it is still an event; the tiredness around it is scene, fessus eram.

[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: Dum in forō ambulō, amīcum vīdī.]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: the day's last scene — Iam nōn pluēbat, et Mārcus laetus erat.]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: a day of your own: one line of scene, then three events in order]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Which rung comes last? (Postrēmō.) Which tense follows dum in a past story? (The present.) Which tense goes on each rung? (The perfect.) And what did Marcus do first, when his friend called? (He did not want to come: nōn volēbat.)
