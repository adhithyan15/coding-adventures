Latin, chapter 440: To toss on the waves, To be seasick, To carry down, To take across, To swim to safety.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
flūctuō, flūctuāre — to toss on the waves, to rise in waves; to waver.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to launch, then the Latin for navigable.

You'll want to know: flūctuō, flūctuāre.
flūctuō, flūctuāre — "to toss on the waves, to rise in waves; to waver".
In tempestāte nāvis diū flūctuat. In the storm the ship tosses on the waves for a long time.
flūctuō, flūctuāre, flūctuāvī; also a deponent flūctuor. Of the mind: to waver. English fluctuate comes from it.

The word, taken apart.
flūctuō is built on flūctus 'wave' (EtymDB; Lewis & Short), which comes from fluō 'to flow'. English fluctuate comes from it.

nauseō, nauseāre — "to be seasick; to feel sick".
Mare asperum est, et multī viātōrēs nauseant. The sea is rough, and many travellers are seasick.
nauseō, nauseāre, nauseāvī, nauseātum, from nausea 'seasickness'. English nauseate comes from it.

The word, taken apart.
nauseō is built on nausea 'seasickness' (Lewis & Short), which was borrowed from Greek nausiā, built on naus 'ship'. English nauseate comes from it.

Lesson 3 of 5.
dēferō, dēferre — to carry down, to carry off; (of ships) to drive off course; to report.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to toss on the waves, then the Latin for to be seasick.

You'll want to know: dēferō, dēferre.
dēferō, dēferre — "to carry down, to carry off; (of ships) to drive off course; to report".
Ventus nāvem ad ignōtum lītus dēfert. The wind drives the ship off course to an unknown shore.
Irregular: dēferō, dēferre, dētulī, dēlātum. In the passive, dēferrī, of a ship carried off its course. English defer comes from it.

The word, taken apart.
dēferō is built from dē- 'down, away' and ferō 'to carry' (EtymDB; Lewis & Short). English defer comes from it through French.

Lesson 4 of 5.
trāiciō, trāicere — to take across, to cross over; to throw across.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to be seasick, then the Latin for to carry down.

You'll want to know: trāiciō, trāicere.
trāiciō, trāicere — "to take across, to cross over; to throw across".
Mīlitēs equōs nāvibus trāns flūmen trāiciunt. The soldiers take their horses across the river in boats.
trāiciō, trāicere, trāiēcī, trāiectum. Mare trāicere, to cross the sea. English trajectory comes from its participle.

The word, taken apart.
trāiciō is built from trāns 'across' and iaciō 'to throw' (Lewis & Short). English trajectory comes from its participle trāiectus.

Lesson 5 of 5.
ēnatō, ēnatāre — to swim to safety, to swim out.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to carry down, then the Latin for to take across.

You'll want to know: ēnatō, ēnatāre.
ēnatō, ēnatāre — "to swim to safety, to swim out".
Nāvis frangitur, sed nauta ad lītus ēnatat. The ship breaks up, but the sailor swims to the shore and is safe.
ēnatō, ēnatāre, ēnatāvī. From ē- 'out' and natō 'to swim': to swim out of danger.

The word, taken apart.
ēnatō is built from ē(x)- 'out' and natō 'to swim' (Lewis & Short): to swim out of the water, to escape by swimming.
