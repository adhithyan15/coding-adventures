Lesson 1 of 8.
电话 (diànhuà) — telephone; phone call.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Mandarin for near, then the Mandarin for to know a fact.

You'll want to know: 电话.
电话 — diànhuà — "telephone; phone call".
你有电话吗 nǐ yǒu diànhuà ma — Have you got a phone?
The new character, in pieces: 电 (diàn) — 日 with one long stroke running down through its middle, bending right along the bottom and hooking up. The next lesson puts it in your hand.

The word, taken apart.
电话 (diànhuà) is built from 电 (diàn) 'lightning', now 'electricity', and 话 'talk', both inherited from Middle Chinese, denH and hwaejH (Guangyun, in Baxter's transcription): electric talk.

Script — 日 with a long tail.
电 (diàn).
Two parts: the box first, then the tail.
日, four strokes: the left side, the top that turns down the right side, the middle bar, and the bottom bar.
The tail, one stroke: from above the top of the box, a vertical straight down through the middle, bending right along the bottom and hooking up at the end.
4 + 1 = five.

Lesson 3 of 8.
打电话 (dǎ diànhuà) — to phone; to make a phone call.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Mandarin for to know a fact, then the Mandarin for telephone.

You'll want to know: 打电话.
打电话 — dǎ diànhuà — "to phone; to make a phone call".
我想打电话 wǒ xiǎng dǎ diànhuà — I'd like to make a phone call.
The new character, in pieces: 打 (dǎ) — the hand piece, a narrow 手, on the left, and on the right a two-stroke piece read dīng. The next lesson puts it in your hand.

The word, taken apart.
打电话 (dǎ diànhuà) is built from dǎ 'to strike' and 电话 'telephone'. The first is inherited from Middle Chinese taengX (Guangyun, in Baxter's transcription), glossed 'to strike', though dǎ is not the sound taengX would regularly give. Making a call is not among its senses there, so how the verb came to do it is not settled.

What you've built.

[once you have stopped driving — this part needs your eyes or your hands; if you are driving, skip ahead to the next part]
Script — the hand piece, then dīng.
打 (dǎ).
Two pieces, left before right.
The hand piece, three strokes, a narrow form of 手: a short horizontal, a vertical through it that hooks to the left at the foot, then a stroke that rises from the lower left up across the vertical.
The right piece, two strokes, read dīng: a horizontal across the top, then a vertical down from its middle that hooks to the left.
3 + 2 = five.

[once you have stopped driving — this part needs your eyes or your hands; if you are driving, skip ahead to the next part]
Guided Practice.
[once you have stopped driving — write: 打 (dǎ), all five, with the model in front of you]
[once you have stopped driving — cover: write it again from memory, and count the pieces, not the strokes]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
How many pieces in 打 (dǎ)? (Two.) Which character you know is the hand piece a narrow form of? (手.) Which way does the rising stroke run? (From the lower left up to the right.)
Source: Hanzi Writer Data 打 (dǎ), pinned stroke paths and medians.


Lesson 5 of 8.
wéi — hello on the phone.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Mandarin for telephone, then the Mandarin for to phone.

You'll want to know: wéi.
wéi — "hello on the phone".
wéi nǐ tīng wǒ shuō — Hello? Listen to me.
Its characters come later; for now it is a word for the ear.
On the phone it is said with a rising tone, as a question: wéi?

The word, taken apart.
wéi is the call you answer the phone with. No dictionary we use records an older form, so its history is not settled.

Lesson 6 of 8.
hàomǎ — number (of a phone, a room); identifying number.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Mandarin for to phone, then the Mandarin for hello on the phone.

You'll want to know: hàomǎ.
hàomǎ — "number (of a phone, a room); identifying number".
nǐ de diànhuà hàomǎ shì duōshao — What's your phone number?
Its characters come later; for now it is a word for the ear.

The word, taken apart.
hàomǎ is built from hào and mǎ, both inherited from Middle Chinese, hawH and maeX (Guangyun, in Baxter's transcription). The dictionary glosses the first as a call or a command and the second as agate, a stone like jade; how the pair came to mean a number is not settled.
