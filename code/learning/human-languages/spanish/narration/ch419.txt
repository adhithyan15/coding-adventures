Spanish, chapter 419: Dancing, and the Article You Drop.
1 lesson. All 1 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 1.
bailar — and the verb that needs no article.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
You have met me gusta la música, which keeps an article English throws away. Hold that thought — this lesson is the case where the rule switches off.

You'll want to know first.
música — the noun this verb belongs with, and the article rule this lesson contrasts against.

Sounds you'll need.
diphthong-ai — ai is one syllable, bai-, close to English bye. see reference.
b-soft — the b is softer than an English b; the lips barely close.
r-tap — the final r is a single flick of the tongue, not a growl.

The word, taken apart.
bailar — "to dance." An -ar infinitive, the most ordinary shape a Spanish verb can have.
Root: Late Latin ballare, "to dance," from Greek ballízein. This root travelled further into English than almost any other on this page:
a ball — the dancing kind. A ballroom is a room for ballare.
ballet — through Italian balletto, "a little dance."
ballad — Provençal balada, originally a song you danced to. That is why a ballad tells a story in verses: the verses were danced.
And now the trap worth knowing, because it looks like the strongest member of the family and is not:
A ball you throw is not related. That one is Germanic, a cousin of balloon and bowl. English has two unrelated words spelled ball, and only the one you dance at comes from ballare.

Grammar Lens: where the article rule switches off.
Put the two halves of this chapter side by side:
Me gusta la música. — I like music. Article. Me gusta bailar. — I like dancing. No article.
This is not an exception to memorise; it follows from what gustar does. It needs a subject, and an infinitive is already one — bailar means "to dance," a whole action named. A noun needs its article to be spoken of in general; a verb does not, because naming it is what the infinitive is for.
So: if what pleases you is a thing, it takes el or la. If what pleases you is an action, the bare infinitive is the whole answer.

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Why does me gusta la música take la while me gusta bailar takes nothing? (A noun needs its article to mean the thing in general; an infinitive already names the action.) Which English ball comes from this root — the dance or the thing you throw? (The dance.)
