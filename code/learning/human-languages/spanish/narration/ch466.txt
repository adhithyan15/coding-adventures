Lesson 1 of 6.
el director — the one who signs the letter.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, because the lesson points at something written down. You can listen to all of it now and come back to the parts that need looking at once you have stopped.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say directo and el actor. One gave you the verb and one gave you the ending. Put them together and you have this word already.

You'll want to know: el director.
el director, la directora — the head, the director.
Atentamente, la directora del centro.
El director del colegio — the head teacher. La directora de la empresa — the company director. El director de cine — the film director.
Unlike el guía, this one does change: director, directora. It ends in a consonant, so the feminine adds -a rather than moving the article.

The word, taken apart.
You have been given both halves already, in two different chapters, and this is where they meet.
When you learned el actor, the -tor ending was set out as Latin's way of naming the doer: take a verb, add -tor, get the one who does it. The table there listed agere becomes actor, docere becomes doctor — and dirigere becomes director. The word was printed in front of you before you needed it.
Then directo gave you dirigere itself: to set something straight, to aim it. So a director is the one who aims the thing — not the one who shouts, the one who points it where it is going.
[a table of 1 row, read aloud]
the verb: dirigere, to aim. the doer: el director.
Dirigir is the living Spanish verb: dirigir una empresa, to run a company. And dirigirse a alguien is to address somebody — to aim your words at a person, which is what the letter in this chapter does.

Lesson 2 of 6.
visible — able to be seen.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say sociable and el aviso. One gave you the ending, the other gave you the root. This word is the two of them stuck together.

You'll want to know: visible.
visible — visible, able to be seen.
El aviso debe quedar visible en el tablón.
En un lugar visible — somewhere it can be seen, the phrase notices use about themselves. Una mejora visible — a noticeable improvement.
Like sociable, it has one form for everyone: un cartel visible, una puerta visible. Adjectives in this family do not take a feminine.

Grammar Lens: -able and -ible point two ways.
They are one ending, Latin -bilis, wearing two spellings. Which vowel you get depends on the Latin verb it was built from, and there is no rule worth memorising — you learn each word.
What is worth noticing is the direction, because it is not fixed either:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
adjective: sociable. who is doing what: the person does the mixing.
adjective: visible. who is doing what: somebody else does the seeing.
Sociable describes a person inclined to do something. Visible describes a thing that can have something done to it. The ending covers both, and only the sense of the verb tells you which way it points — un niño sociable mixes, un cartel visible is looked at.

The word, taken apart.
Latin videre, to see — the root you met under el aviso, where a-vis-o turned out to be ad visum, "to what is seen".
The same videre is under a great many English words, and they arrived by different doors: vision and visible and evident came through Latin, while advice came through French and view came through French from the same place. Spanish keeps ver as the everyday verb and saves videre's own shape for the formal words: visible, la visión, evidente.

Lesson 3 of 6.
el comportamiento — how somebody carries themselves.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say importar and el aparcamiento. One holds the root of the next word and the other holds its ending.

You'll want to know: el comportamiento.
el comportamiento — behaviour, conduct.
Su comportamiento en clase ha mejorado.
El comportamiento del grupo — the group's behaviour. In a school letter it is the formal word; a parent at home is more likely to say se porta bien or se porta mal.
Comportarse is the verb: compórtate, behave yourself.

The word, taken apart.
com-, together, plus portare, to carry — the portare you had under importar, where carrying something in turned out to be the whole of what Rome meant by it.
To comportarse is to carry yourself, and English does the identical thing: conduct is con- plus ducere, to lead — to lead yourself. Two languages, two verbs of motion, the same idea that character is something you are carrying about.
[a table of 1 row, read aloud]
comportarse, to carry oneself means to conduct oneself.
Keep it away from la puerta. That is porta, a gate, and the warning came with importar: the two look alike and are not the same word.

Grammar Lens: -miento on a reflexive verb.
You have -miento from el aparcamiento: an ending that makes a noun out of a verb. This one is built on comportarse, which is reflexive, and the noun quietly drops the se.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
verb: aparcar. noun: el aparcamiento.
verb: comportarse. noun: el comportamiento.
So the noun names the carrying without saying whose. That is exactly what a school letter wants: el comportamiento is a fact about conduct, where se porta mal is an accusation about a child.

Lesson 4 of 6.
discutir — to discuss, and to argue.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say la reunión and el director. A meeting with the head is where this verb usually turns up.

You'll want to know: discutir.
discutir — to discuss, and also to argue.
Podemos discutir el comportamiento en la reunión.
Discutir un tema — to discuss a topic. Discutir con alguien — to argue with somebody. La discusión is both the discussion and the row.

Grammar Lens: the preposition decides.
One verb covers what English splits between discuss and argue, and the grammar around it does most of the sorting.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
a thing — discutir el precio means discuss it.
con a person — discutir con él means argue with him.
A thing after the verb is a topic; a person after con is an opponent. It is not watertight — discutir el precio con el jefe can be either, and tone settles it — but the pattern is reliable enough to lean on, and mistaking the two is how a polite sentence turns rude.

The word, taken apart.
Latin discutere: dis-, apart, plus quatere, to shake.
To discutere was to shake a thing to pieces — to strike it until it came apart and you could see what was inside. The Romans used it of smashing, and only later of taking an argument apart.
Which makes the two Spanish senses one sense. Discussing a topic and arguing with a person are the same act at two temperatures: something is being shaken until it comes apart. English kept the violent end elsewhere — concussion is con- plus the same quatere, a shaking-together of the head.
Percusión is the third member, the shaking-through of a struck drum.

Lesson 5 of 6.
(el director, visible, el comportamiento, discutir) — from cold.

Warm-up.
[pause 3 seconds]
Four words, no prompts. Who signs the letter, what the notice must stay, what the letter is about, and what the meeting will do with it.

You'll want to know: the four, cold.
[a table of 4 rows, read aloud]
English: the head. Spanish: el director.
English: visible. Spanish: visible.
English: behaviour. Spanish: el comportamiento.
English: to discuss, to argue. Spanish: discutir.

Grammar Lens: three endings you already had.
Not one of this chapter's endings is new. What is new is which way each one bends.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
ending: -tor. does it change for a woman?: yes — director, directora.
ending: -ible. does it change for a woman?: no — visible for everyone.
El actor gave you -tor and printed director in its own table. Sociable gave you the -able half of -ible and the rule that it has one form. El aparcamiento gave you -miento. The chapter is four words and nothing underneath them you had not already been handed.

The words, taken apart.
Two of the four are about carrying and shaking, which is not what a school letter sounds like.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
word: el comportamiento. what is underneath: com- + portare — carrying yourself.
word: discutir. what is underneath: dis- + quatere — shaking a thing apart.
El director aims (dirigere, to set straight) and visible is seen (videre). Four abstractions, four physical verbs, and the letter that uses all four is about a child in a classroom.

Lesson 6 of 6.
(una carta del colegio) — read it, then say what it wants.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say el director and el comportamiento. A letter with both words in it is rarely good news, and rarely as bad as it looks.

Reading: the letter.
Estimada familia: Les escribo para informarles de que el comportamiento de su hijo en clase ha mejorado de forma visible durante este trimestre. Me gustaría discutir con ustedes cómo mantener esa mejora. Pueden pedir cita en secretaría. Atentamente, La directora.

Grammar Lens: what the letter is not doing.
A reader who knows discutir only as argue will read this as a summons. It is not one, and two words tell you so.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
the word: ha mejorado. what it settles: the news is good.
the word: discutir con ustedes. what it settles: a conversation, not a row.
Discutir con was given as the arguing shape, and here it is doing the other job — because what is being discussed is an improvement. That is the limit of the rule: the preposition leans, the content decides.
Note also de forma visible. The letter does not say the child is visible; it says the improvement can be seen. Visible is passive, as its ending warned.

Guided Practice.
[pause 3 seconds]
Say in one sentence what the letter reports and what it asks for.
[pause 3 seconds]
Now say the same news to a friend, out of the formal register: start with se porta instead of el comportamiento.

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Who wrote it? (La directora.) What improved? (El comportamiento.) How much? (De forma visible.) What does she want? (Discutir — to talk it over.)
