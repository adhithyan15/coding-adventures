Spanish, chapter 467: El huerto comunitario.
6 lessons. All 6 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 6.
producir — to produce.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say reducir and la ducha. The verb coming next is their close relative, and it will not make you learn anything twice.

You'll want to know: producir.
producir — to produce, to make.
El huerto produce unos cien kilos al mes.
Producir comida — to produce food. La producción is the noun, and el producto is the thing that comes out.

Grammar Lens: nothing new here, which is the point.
Reducir taught you this whole verb, five chapters ago. Producir is the same class and takes the same two irregularities:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
where: the z before c. reducir: reduzco. producir: produzco.
where: the j past. reducir: reduje. producir: produje.
So the subjunctive is produzca — the form on a notice, as reduzca was on the road sign — and the past is produjo, never producí.
That is worth stopping on. Learning reducir bought you the whole -ducir corner of that class — producir, and traducir and introducir when you meet them — the verbs that take the j past as well as the z.
Not the whole class, though. Reducir's own lesson warned you: conocer shares the -zc- and not the past, going conocí. Two groups, one spelling, and the -ducir verbs are the half that also changes its past.

The word, taken apart.
pro-, forward, plus ducere, to lead — the ducere already under reducir and under la ducha.
To produce is to lead something forward, out where it can be seen. A farmer leads a crop forward out of the ground; a factory leads goods forward out of a door. English keeps the picture in produce as a noun, the stuff itself, and in a theatre producer, who leads the show out in front of an audience.
Set them side by side and the prefix does all the work: reducir leads back, producir leads forward, and conducir — which reducir's table already showed you — leads along.

Lesson 2 of 6.
suficiente — enough, and only enough.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say bastante and escaso. You already own both ends of this. The word coming next overlaps one of them, and the overlap is the lesson.

You'll want to know: suficiente.
suficiente — enough, sufficient.
No producimos comida suficiente para todos.
Comida suficiente — enough food. Lo suficiente — enough of it, as a noun. Suficientemente — sufficiently.
Like bastante, it has one form for both genders: un plato suficiente, una cantidad suficiente.
Said of a person it turns unkind — un tono suficiente is a smug one.

Grammar Lens: bastante already means this, and that is the problem.
Bastante was given to you as the middle of a scale — poco, bastante, muy — and its lesson was firm that it does not mean a lot. So what is suficiente for?
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
word: bastante. what else it does: quite, fairly — bastante caro.
word: suficiente. what else it does: names a requirement, not a place on a scale.
Bastante has that second, adverbial job: bastante caro is fairly expensive, a reading on the scale. Suficiente has no scale reading at all. It measures a thing against what was needed, and reports whether the need was met.
Both can say there is not enough — no hay bastante is the everyday phrasing. A notice reaches for no hay suficiente because it names the requirement rather than the quantity, and because it is the formal choice.

The word, taken apart.
Latin sufficere: sub-, up from under, plus facere, to make or do — the facere that la factura called one of the hardest-working roots in the language.
To sufficere was to put something under a thing: supply it from below, prop it, make it stand. Adequacy came out of that, which is why sufficiency is load-bearing rather than abundant — the amount that keeps the thing up.
Suficiente wears the -ente of exigente; bastante wears -ante. They are the same ending choosing its vowel by verb family — -ar verbs take -ante, -er and -ir verbs take -ente.
And the two words are one language at two speeds, the pattern you have from rueda against rotativo. Suficiente is a real Latin present participle, sufficientem, lifted off the page. Bastante was built inside Spanish on the inherited verb bastar — there was never a Latin bastans.

Lesson 3 of 6.
alimentar — to feed.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say el medicamento and producir. One lends its ending to this chapter's noun; the other is what has to happen before you can do the verb.

You'll want to know: alimentar.
alimentar — to feed, to nourish.
Lo que producimos alimenta a unas cuarenta familias.
Alimentar a un niño — to feed a child; the a is there because the thing being fed is a person. El alimento is the food itself, and la alimentación is the feeding, or a person's diet.
Alimentarse de algo is what an animal or a person lives on: se alimentan de fruta.

The word, taken apart.
Latin alere was wider than to feed. It meant to nourish, and by that to rear — to bring a thing on, to make it bigger. Feeding is the sense that stuck in Spanish; the others are worth having:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
English: adult. how the root shows: somebody who has finished growing.
English: alumnus. how the root shows: somebody being brought up.
An alumnus is literally one being nourished — a foster-child, then a pupil, then a graduate. Coalesce is co- plus alescere, to grow together.
So alimentar is not about hunger. It is about making something bigger, and food is the means.

Grammar Lens: -mento, and the noun it builds here.
El medicamento gave you -mento, the formal twin of -miento, arriving by the other road. Latin -mentum often named the means of a thing, and both of these are that kind:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
noun: el medicamento. the means of: healing.
noun: el alimento. the means of: growing.
Watch the cut: it is ali- plus -mento, exactly as medica- plus -mento. And the noun is older than the verb — Latin built alimentum off alere first, and alimentare came afterwards. Spanish inherited both, which is why the noun is alimento and not alimentamento.

Lesson 4 of 6.
el objetivo — the target.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say el objeto and rotativo. One is the word underneath the next; the other is its ending.

You'll want to know: el objetivo.
el objetivo — the target, the aim, the objective.
Objetivo del mes: alimentar a cincuenta familias.
Cumplir el objetivo — to meet the target. Nuestro objetivo es… — our aim is… As an adjective it means objective: un informe objetivo.

The word, taken apart.
You have the whole of this already. El objeto was ob-, before or against, plus iacere, to throw — a thing thrown before you, put in your way. That lesson listed the family: object, project, inject, eject, subject, all of them throws.
Objetivo is that same thrown thing wearing -ivo: to do with the thing placed in front. The throw does not turn round — the grammatical object is already what an action is thrown at, as el objeto's own chapter said. What narrowed is why the thing is there.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
word: el objeto. the thing placed before you: whatever is there.
word: el objetivo. the thing placed before you: the one put there to be reached.
English kept both jobs on the same word: an objective is an aim, and an objective lens is the one that faces the object.

Grammar Lens: -ivo makes an adjective that can stand alone.
Rotativo gave you -ivo: an ending that names a habit or a capacity — of the kind that does this.
What objetivo adds is that such an adjective can harden into a noun and keep both jobs:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
use: adjective. example: un informe objetivo.
use: noun. example: el objetivo del mes.
Spanish does this often and English follows it here, which makes the pair easy to trust: an objective report, the month's objective.

Lesson 5 of 6.
(producir, suficiente, alimentar, el objetivo) — from cold.

Warm-up.
[pause 3 seconds]
Four words, no prompts. What the garden does, whether it is enough, who it keeps going, and the number written at the top of the sheet.

You'll want to know: the four, cold.
[a table of 4 rows, read aloud]
English: to produce. Spanish: producir.
English: enough. Spanish: suficiente.
English: to feed. Spanish: alimentar.
English: the target. Spanish: el objetivo.

Grammar Lens: what this chapter actually cost you.
Three of the four are assembled out of parts you already held.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
word: producir. where the parts came from: reducir's verb class, ducere's prefix trick.
word: objetivo. where the parts came from: el objeto's throw, rotativo's -ivo.
Neither needed a new rule — only a new arrangement of rules you had.
Alimentar borrowed -mento from el medicamento. Only suficiente asked for something genuinely new, and even that was a distinction rather than a word: you already had bastante.
That is what the earlier chapters bought. New words stop being new machinery and start being new arrangements of it, and each one costs less than the last.

The words, taken apart.
Two of the four hide a physical action.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
word: suficiente. what is underneath: sub- + facere — putting enough underneath to hold it up.
word: alimentar. what is underneath: alere — growing a thing, with food as the means.
Producir leads forward; objetivo is the thing placed in front to be reached. Four words off a garden shed, four Latin verbs of handling and growing underneath them — the pattern this book keeps finding.

Lesson 6 of 6.
(el informe mensual del huerto) — read the figures, then judge them.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say producir and el objetivo. A sheet with both on it is asking one question: did the first reach the second?

Reading: the sheet on the shed door.
HUERTO COMUNITARIO — MARZO Objetivo del mes: alimentar a 50 familias. Producido: 380 kg. Familias atendidas: 41. No fue suficiente. En abril vamos a plantar más patata (papa), que produce más kilos por metro cuadrado.

Grammar Lens: the sheet answers its own question.
Four numbers and one verdict, and the verdict is the word this chapter was for.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
what the sheet gives: 50 against 41. what it settles: the target was missed.
what the sheet gives: no fue suficiente. what it settles: and by how the garden judges it.
It says suficiente, not bastante, and that is deliberate — though both would be understood. No fue bastante is what you would say out loud. No fue suficiente is what a sheet writes, because it names the requirement rather than the quantity, and the 380 kg is being measured against the 50 families.
Note producido too: the participle, used as a plain label for the amount. A sheet does not need a whole sentence to report a quantity.

Guided Practice.
[pause 3 seconds]
Say in one sentence what the target was and whether it was met.
[pause 3 seconds]
Now say what April is meant to change, and why the potato is the chosen crop.

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
The month's target? (Alimentar a 50 familias.) Was it enough? (No fue suficiente.) Why that word on a sheet? (Because it names the requirement, where bastante names a quantity.)
