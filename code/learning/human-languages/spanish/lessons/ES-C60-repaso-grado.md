## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-GRAMMAR-DEGREE-SCALE] -->

[PAUSE 2s] The scale, least to most. (*Poco*, *bastante*, *muy*.)

## What you've built
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-MUY, ES-LEX-BASTANTE, ES-LEX-MAL] -->

Three chants. Say each, then say what it is for.

[PAUSE 2s] **First.** *Estoy muy cansado.* — ***Muy*** is the top of the scale,
and it is *mucho* with its ending bitten off.

[PAUSE 2s] **Second.** *Estoy bastante cansado.* — ***Bastante*** is the middle,
and it means *sufficing*: *bastar* plus the *-ante* you could already read.

[PAUSE 2s] **Third.** *Estoy mal.* — ***Mal*** is the opposite of *bien*, and it
is *malo* with its ending bitten off.

## Grammar Lens: the habit underneath
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-GRAMMAR-APOCOPE-PATTERN, ES-GRAMMAR-DEGREE-MUY] -->

Two of those three came in a long form and a short one, and that is the thing
worth carrying out of this chapter.

[PAUSE 2s] *Mucho* keeps its ending in front of a noun and loses it in front of
an adjective. *Malo* keeps its ending standing alone and loses it in front of a
masculine noun. Different words, one habit: **the ending goes when the word is
leaning on the next one.**

[PAUSE 2s] So when you meet a short Spanish word that looks like a longer one
you already know, that is usually what has happened — not a new word to learn.
