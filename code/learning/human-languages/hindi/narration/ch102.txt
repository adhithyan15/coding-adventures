Hindi, chapter 102: From, Until, and Instead.
7 lessons. You can do the first 3 of them in the car; after that you will want to have stopped.


Lesson 1 of 7.
तक (tak) — where it stops.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say the word that marks where something comes from. Then say at five o'clock. One of those two words has been doing its job alone.

You'll want to know: तक.
पाँच बजे तक (tak) — until five o'clock.
से marks a start. तक (tak) marks a stop.

The word, taken apart.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
word: से. what it marks: the point something begins at.
word: तक (tak). what it marks: the point something ends at.
Both are postpositions, so both come after the word they attach to. English puts until in front and Hindi puts तक (tak) behind, and that is not a quirk of this word — it is the same fact about the language that put के in the middle of every phrase in the last chapter.
तक (tak) also stretches across space, not only time: घर तक (tak) is as far as the house. One word covers both, because a stopping point is a stopping point whether you measure it in hours or in streets.

Grammar Lens: it does not have to be a pair.
पाँच बजे तक (tak) on its own is a complete and ordinary thing to say. It names only the end, and leaves the beginning to be understood — usually now.
That matters because the next lesson pairs the two words, and a reader who has only ever seen the pair will not recognise तक (tak) standing by itself on a sign or in a notice, which is where it most often appears.

Lesson 2 of 7.
सोमवार से शुक्रवार तक (somvār se śukravār tak) — a span, marked at both ends.

Warm-up.
[pause 3 seconds]
Say the first and last weekdays of the working week. Then say the word that marks a stopping point.

You'll want to know: से … तक.
सोमवार से शुक्रवार तक (somvār se śukravār tak) — from Monday to Friday.
Two days, and each one carries its own marker.

The two words.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
piece: सोमवार से. job: start here.
piece: शुक्रवार तक (tak). job: stop here.
English builds a span out of from and to standing in front of their days. Hindi builds it out of से and तक (tak) standing behind theirs.
The shape looks harder than it is, because both halves are already familiar and neither is doing anything new. What is new is only that they are allowed to appear together, and that when they do, the whole thing is one idea rather than two.

Grammar Lens: order is not optional.
The से half comes first. Always, and for the same reason English says from Monday to Friday and not the other way round: a span is told in the direction it runs.
What Hindi does not copy is the position of the markers. Read the pair as two small packages rather than as one long phrase:
[ सोमवार से ] [ शुक्रवार तक (tak) ].
Read it that way and a longer span — a written account of somebody's week — comes apart at the right seams the first time.

Lesson 3 of 7.
सुबह से शाम तक (subah se śām tak) — the same frame, smaller units.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say morning and evening, the two Persian loanwords you met early on. Then guess how a span between them is built.

You'll want to know: सुबह से शाम तक.
सुबह से शाम तक (subah se śām tak) — from morning until evening.
If you guessed, you guessed right. Nothing about the frame changed.

The two words.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
what is spanned: days. Hindi: सोमवार से शुक्रवार तक (somvār se śukravār tak).
what is spanned: parts of a day. Hindi: सुबह से शाम तक (subah se śām tak).
what is spanned: clock hours. Hindi: दस बजे से पाँच बजे तक (tak).
Three different units, one frame. से and तक (tak) do not care what they are attached to, and that is worth noticing because English does not always manage it either — from ten to five is fine, but from Monday until Friday and until the house pull on different senses of the same word.

Grammar Lens: the clock row is two packages, not four words.
दस बजे से is one package and पाँच बजे तक (tak) is another. Each already had its own marker — बजे — before the span marker arrived, so the clock row stacks two postposition-like endings on one side.
Read it inside out:
दस becomes दस बजे (at ten) becomes दस बजे से (from ten).
Nothing is being negotiated between them. Each step adds one word to the right of what came before, which is the direction this language always builds in.

Lesson 4 of 7.
ट्रेन (ṭren) — a word that travelled with the thing.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, because the lesson points at something written down. You can listen to all of it now and come back to the parts that need looking at once you have stopped.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say by bus. Say station. You have had a station for a while without a second way of reaching it.

You'll want to know: ट्रेन.
ट्रेन (ṭren) से — by train.

The word, taken apart.
Both of your vehicles are borrowed words, and they are borrowed the same way:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
Hindi: बस. from: English bus. by …: बस से.
Hindi: ट्रेन (ṭren). from: English train. by …: ट्रेन (ṭren) से.
The से that makes them by bus and by train is the one already taught for means — the same word that says from, and that this chapter has been using for the start of a span.
Look at the first letter. ट is the retroflex one, made with the tongue curled back — and it is used here because English t in train sounds, to a Hindi ear, much more like ट than like the dental त. That is the regular habit for English loanwords, and it is why ट्रेन (ṭren) and not त्रेन.

Grammar Lens: a word Hindi also has its own name for.
Hindi does have an inherited word for a railway train, and an A1 reader will still meet ट्रेन (ṭren) far more often — on a board, in a timetable, in the middle of an ordinary sentence about getting to work.
Learn the borrowed one first because it is the one that is said. The other is worth recognising later, when reading formal notices is the task.

Lesson 5 of 7.
nahīṁ kī jagah — how much the negative takes back.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say by bus and by train. Now imagine somebody has guessed the first one and you need to give them the second.

You'll want to know: X से नहीं, Y से.
बस से नहीं, ट्रेन (ṭren) से। — Not by bus — by train.
नहीं stands between the two options, and the one it rejects is the one before it.

The two words.
Read the frame as three beats:
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
beat: 1. contents: the wrong option, with its own से.
beat: 2. contents: नहीं.
beat: 3. contents: the right option, with its own से.
Each option keeps its marker. That is the part to watch, because English drops the second one — not by bus, by train has one by, and Hindi has two से. A sentence with only one से is not this frame.

Grammar Lens: what नहीं is negating.
In every sentence so far, नहीं has stood in front of the verb and denied the whole thing. Here it stands after a noun-plus-postposition and denies only that phrase.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
what is denied: the whole sentence. example: मैं नहीं जाता.
what is denied: one option in it. example: बस से नहीं, ट्रेन (ṭren) से.
So the position of नहीं tells a reader how much of the sentence is being taken back — and getting it wrong turns a correction into a refusal. The English speaker's instinct is to reach for a word like but; this frame has no word in the middle beyond the negative itself, and the comma carries the pause.

Lesson 6 of 7.
se kī tālikā — five jobs, one word.

Warm-up.
[pause 3 seconds]
Name two things से has done before this chapter, and the word that has now joined it.

Grammar Lens: everything से does.
[a table of 5 rows, read aloud]
job: from a place. example: स्टेशन से.
job: by a means. example: ट्रेन (ṭren) से.
job: measured against. example: बच्चे से बड़ा.
job: the start of a span. example: सोमवार से.
job: one option in a correction. example: बस से नहीं.
Five jobs and no change of form. से never bends, whatever it is doing and whatever stands in front of it — unlike का, which had to become के the moment another word followed.
The reason is worth keeping: का is a possessive and possessives agree, while से is a plain postposition and has nothing to agree with.

Grammar Lens: the partner, and where it stands.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
span: days. start: सोमवार से. stop: शुक्रवार तक (tak).
span: parts of a day. start: सुबह से. stop: शाम तक (tak).
span: clock hours. start: दस बजे से. stop: पाँच बजे तक (tak).
तक (tak) can stand alone — पाँच बजे तक (tak) needs no से. से can stand alone too. The pair is a choice, not a requirement.

Grammar Lens: the one place two से are compulsory.
बस से नहीं, ट्रेन (ṭren) से।.
English keeps one by and lets it cover both halves. Hindi gives each option its own से, because each option is a complete phrase in its own right and नहीं sits between two complete phrases rather than inside one.
Count the से when you read a correction. Two means a swap is being offered; one means something else is going on.

[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Lesson 7 of 7.
मैं ट्रेन से जाऊँगा (maiṁ ṭren se jāūṁgā) — reading a correction in a paper.

Warm-up.
[pause 3 seconds]
Say I will do. Then say the verb for going. The future ending you learned on one verb attaches to any stem, and this is the sentence that wants it.

The exchange.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
speaker: A. Hindi: आप बस से जाएँगे?
speaker: B. Hindi: मैं बस से नहीं, ट्रेन (ṭren) से जाऊँगा।.
B does two things at once: rejects A's guess and supplies the replacement, in one sentence with one verb at the end of it.

How to answer.
Find नहीं first. Everything before it is what is being denied; everything after it up to the verb is what replaces it.
Then check the count: two से means a swap. An item that asks how will B travel is answered by the phrase after the negative, and the commonest wrong answer is the phrase before it — which is exactly why the paper prints the rejected option first.

Grammar Lens: the chapter in one sentence.
से marks a start and तक (tak) marks a stop, and between them they carry both a week and a working day:
मैं सोमवार से शुक्रवार तक (somvār se śukravār tak) ट्रेन (ṭren) से जाऊँगा।.
Two से in that sentence, doing different jobs — the first opens a span, the second names a means. Neither is a mistake, and telling them apart is a matter of looking for a तक further along.
