# ناک — "nose," the same three letters as کان, backwards

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[UR-LEX-KAAN, UR-GRAMMAR-MERA-GENDER-AGREEMENT] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say **merā kān** — my ear, masculine, unchanged **merā**. This
word shares every letter with it.

## You'll want to know first — one word
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[UR-LEX-NAAK]; assesses=[] -->

> **ناک** — *nāk* — **nose**

> **میری ناک۔** — *merī nāk.* — "my nose."

**ناک** is feminine — **merī**, not **merā**. Grammatical gender does not
follow shape or sound; it has to be learned noun by noun.

## The letters in this word
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[UR-SCRIPT-KAAN-NAAK-REVERSAL]; assesses=[UR-LEX-KAAN, UR-LEX-NAAK] -->

**کان** is **ک ا ن** — *k, ā, n*. **ناک** is **ن ا ک** — *n, ā, k*. The same
three letters, in exactly the opposite order. Ear and nose, spelled as
mirror images of each other — a coincidence of Urdu's script, not of its
history; the two words are not related.

## The word, taken apart — a real cousin, and English agrees
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[UR-ETYMON-NAAK-NOSE]; assesses=[UR-LEX-NAAK, UR-ETYMON-KAAN-UNCERTAIN] -->

**nāk** continues Sauraseni Prakrit *ṇakka*, from Proto-Indo-Iranian
**\*náHs**, from Proto-Indo-European **\*néh₂s**, "nose." English **nose**
comes from that very root. Where **کان**'s ancestry ran out unanswered —
recall *karṇa*'s own origin was left unsettled last lesson — **ناک**'s does
not: *nāk* and *nose* are genuine cousins, joining **بھائی** and *brother*
as this book's settled Indo-European matches.
