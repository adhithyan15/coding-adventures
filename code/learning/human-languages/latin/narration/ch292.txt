Latin, chapter 292: Repente et cōnfestim.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
repente — suddenly, unexpectedly.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for not yet, then the Latin for at last.

You'll want to know: repente.
repente — "suddenly, unexpectedly".
Repente magna pluvia cecidit, et omnēs fūgērunt. Suddenly heavy rain fell, and everyone ran away.
The ablative of the old adjective repēns, 'sudden'. Close to subitō (taught), and common in Livy.

The word, taken apart.
repente is the ablative of repēns, "sudden" (Lewis & Short), used as an adverb. Where repēns itself comes from is not settled.

Lesson 2 of 5.
cōnfestim — at once, without delay.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for at last, then the Latin for suddenly.

You'll want to know: cōnfestim.
cōnfestim — "at once, without delay".
Dux, ubi nūntium audīvit, cōnfestim mīlitēs mīsit. When the leader heard the news, he sent soldiers at once.
Common in Caesar and Livy for a swift reaction: one thing heard, the next done.

The word, taken apart.
cōnfestim is linked by Lewis & Short with festīnō, "to hurry" (taught), with the prefix con-: all in a rush. The exact formation is not settled.

Lesson 3 of 5.
continuō — straightaway, without a pause.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for suddenly, then the Latin for at once.

You'll want to know: continuō.
continuō — "straightaway, without a pause".
Puella epistulam lēgit et continuō flēvit. The girl read the letter and straightaway burst into tears.
The adverb of continuus, 'unbroken': nothing comes between. Do not confuse the verb continuō, continuāre, 'to join in a row'.

The word, taken apart.
continuō is the ablative of continuus, "unbroken", which is built on contineō, "to hold together" (EtymDB). English continue comes from the same family.

Lesson 4 of 5.
extemplō — in an instant, forthwith.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for at once, then the Latin for straightaway.

You'll want to know: extemplō.
extemplō — "in an instant, forthwith".
Extemplō portae clausae sunt. In an instant the gates were shut.
Old and lively in tone: Plautus, Vergil and Livy use it. Plautus also has the longer extempulō.

The word, taken apart.
extemplō comes, says Lewis & Short, from ex and tempulum, a diminutive of templum in its old sense "a marked-out space": out of the very moment.

Lesson 5 of 5.
prōtinus — immediately; straight on.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for straightaway, then the Latin for in an instant.

You'll want to know: prōtinus.
prōtinus — "immediately; straight on".
Ē raedā dēscendit et prōtinus domum festīnāvit. He got down from the carriage and hurried straight home.
First in space, 'onward, straight ahead'; then in time, 'at once'. Both senses are classical.

The word, taken apart.
prōtinus is built on prō, "forward", and tenus, "as far as" (Lewis & Short): onward, and then without a break.
