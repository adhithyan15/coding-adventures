Latin, chapter 315: Naufragium.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
naufragium, naufragiī — a shipwreck.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a blow, then the Latin for wounded.

You'll want to know: naufragium, naufragiī.
naufragium, naufragiī — "a shipwreck".
Naufragium prope lītus factum est. A shipwreck happened near the shore.
Neuter, built from the words for 'ship' and 'to break'. Naufragium facere: to be shipwrecked.

The word, taken apart.
naufragium stands for an older nāvifragium, from nāvis, "ship", and frangō, "to break" (Lewis & Short): a ship-breaking.

Lesson 2 of 5.
naufragus, naufraga, naufragum — shipwrecked.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for wounded, then the Latin for a shipwreck.

You'll want to know: naufragus, naufraga, naufragum.
naufragus, naufraga, naufragum — "shipwrecked".
Nautae naufragī in lītore sedēbant. The shipwrecked sailors were sitting on the shore.
As a noun, naufragus is a shipwrecked man; beggars told of their wrecks to win coins.

The word, taken apart.
naufragus joins nāvis, "ship", and frangō, "to break" (EtymDB): one whose ship is broken.

Lesson 3 of 5.
mergō, mergere — to sink, to plunge, to dip.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a shipwreck, then the Latin for shipwrecked.

You'll want to know: mergō, mergere.
mergō, mergere — "to sink, to plunge, to dip".
Undae nāvem parvam mersērunt. The waves sank the little ship.
mergō, mergere, mersī, mersum. English merge and submerge come from it.

The word, taken apart.
mergō is an inherited verb that Lewis & Short connect with a Sanskrit verb meaning "to dip". English merge, submerge and immerse come from it.

Lesson 4 of 5.
obruō, obruere — to overwhelm, to bury.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for shipwrecked, then the Latin for to sink.

You'll want to know: obruō, obruere.
obruō, obruere — "to overwhelm, to bury".
Tempestās nautās obruit. The storm overwhelmed the sailors.
obruō, obruere, obruī, obrutum: ob + ruō, to rush over. Also of earth and ash: a town buried under ash.

The word, taken apart.
obruō is built on ruō, "to rush, to fall", with ob- (EtymDB): to fall upon and cover over.

Lesson 5 of 5.
calamitās, calamitātis — a disaster, a misfortune.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to sink, then the Latin for to overwhelm.

You'll want to know: calamitās, calamitātis.
calamitās, calamitātis — "a disaster, a misfortune".
Post illam calamitātem multī cīvēs pauperēs erant. After that disaster many citizens were poor.
Feminine, 3rd declension. First harm to crops, then any disaster. English calamity.

The word, taken apart.
calamitās first meant "loss, damage", especially to crops (Lewis & Short); its origin is not settled. English calamity comes from it through French.
