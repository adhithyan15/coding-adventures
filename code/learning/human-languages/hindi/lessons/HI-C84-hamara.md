# हमारा — our, and the places Hindi prefers it to "my"

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[HI-LEX-C84-POSS-01, HI-CONCEPT-C02-MERAA-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say *merā* and *uskā*. Both end the same way and bend the same way,
so the next word brings no new grammar — only a new owner.

## You'll want to know: हमारा
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[HI-LEX-C84-POSS-02]; assesses=[] -->

**हमारा** (*hamārā*) — "our."

Built on **हम** (*ham*), "we," which you have met. Before a feminine noun it is
*hamārī*; before a plural or an oblique it is *hamāre*, exactly as *merā*
behaves.

## Grammar Lens: where Hindi says "our" and English says "my"
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[HI-LEX-C84-POSS-02, HI-LEX-C84-POSS-01] -->

The grammar is easy; the usage is the thing worth carrying:

| talking about | English habit | Hindi habit |
|---|---|---|
| the house you grew up in | my house | **hamārā** ghar |
| your family | my family | **hamārā** parivār |
| your country | my country | **hamārā** deś |

A Hindi speaker describing their family will very often say *hamārā parivār*
where an English speaker says *my family*, because the thing is understood as
belonging to the household rather than to the speaker. Neither is wrong in
either language — but the preference is real, and a listening paper will play
you the Hindi one.
