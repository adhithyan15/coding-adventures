# ਭਰਾ — a breathy letter you have not met yet

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[PA-SOUND-PUNJABI-TONE, PA-EVIDENCE-GHORA-KORA] -->

[PAUSE 2s] *Kòṛā* the horse dipped and rose because its old **ਘ** lost its
breath at the **start** of the word. This chapter's word does the same thing,
with a different consonant, and it is not a verb this time — it is somebody
you know.

## You'll want to know first — one word
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[PA-LEX-BHARA]; assesses=[] -->

> **ਭਰਾ** — *bharā* — **brother**

## The letters in this word
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[] -->

**ਭ** — new: the fourth letter of the *p*-row, once *bha*, a *b* said with a
breathy voice. Then **ਰ** *r*, **ਾ** long *ā*.

## Sounds you'll need — the other half of the tone rule
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[PA-SOUND-TONE-LOW]; assesses=[PA-SOUND-PUNJABI-TONE, PA-SOUND-TONE-HIGH-LEVEL] -->

Chapter 7 showed word-**initial** **ਘ** losing its breath and leaving a low,
rising pitch — *kòṛā*. **ਭ** is the same story with a different letter: said
at the front of a word, its breath is gone too, and what is left is a plain,
voiceless **p**, plus a **low** tone on the vowel after it.

**ਭਰਾ** is therefore not said *bharā*. It is said **prā̀** — *p*, not *bh*, and
low on the first vowel. Spelling keeps the old consonant; speech keeps only
its pitch.

| position in the word | old sound | what survives |
|---|---|---|
| initial (*bh-*, *gh-* …) | breathy voice | **low** tone, consonant plain |
| medial (*-jh-*, *-ḍh-* …) | breathy voice | **high, falling** tone |
| final, nothing after | any lost breath | **high, level** tone |

Three positions, three outcomes, and you have now met a word for each.

## The word, taken apart — the cleanest cousin in this book
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[PA-ETYMON-BHARA-BROTHER]; assesses=[PA-LEX-PARIVAR, PA-ETYMON-PARIVAR-SURROUND] -->

**bharā** is inherited from Sanskrit **bhrātṛ**, root Indo-European
\**bʰréh₂tēr*. This is one of the cleanest cognate sets in the family:
Sanskrit *bhrātṛ*, English **brother**, Latin *frāter*, Greek *phrātēr*,
Russian *brat* — every one of them the same word, by ordinary sound change,
in every branch, no borrowing anywhere. Where **parivār** kept both its
Sanskrit halves intact, **bharā** shows what centuries of everyday wear does
instead: the whole cluster *bhr-* thinned down to a single consonant and a
tone.
