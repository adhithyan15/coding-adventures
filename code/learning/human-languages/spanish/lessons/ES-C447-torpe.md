# torpe — from shameful to clumsy

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C380-COUNT-38, ES-LEX-C447-JUICIO-03] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say **feo**. Say **la urgencia**. *Feo* judges how a thing looks.
Name the word that judges how a person moves.

## You'll want to know: torpe
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-C447-JUICIO-04]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C380-COUNT-38] -->

**torpe** — clumsy, awkward, and of a person's mind, slow.

*Soy muy torpe con las manos* — I am very clumsy with my hands. *Perdona, qué
torpe* — sorry, how clumsy of me, which is what you say after knocking
something over.

It does not change for gender: *un niño torpe*, *una niña torpe*. The noun is
**la torpeza**, clumsiness — and also a blunder: *fue una torpeza decir eso*.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C447-JUICIO-04, ES-LEX-C380-COUNT-38] -->

Latin **turpis** meant **ugly, foul, shameful** — a heavy word, used of
disgraceful conduct rather than of somebody who drops things.

Spanish kept the shape and moved the meaning from **how you look** to **how you
act**, and softened it on the way. *Turpis* was an accusation; *torpe* is close
to an apology.

| the word | what it judges |
|---|---|
| **feo** | how a thing **looks** |
| **torpe** | how a person **manages** |

Both are from Latin words for ugliness — *feo* from *foedus*, *torpe* from
*turpis* — and only one of them is still about appearance. English took *turpis*
too, in **turpitude**, which kept the original severity and is now used almost
nowhere.

## Why it's said this way
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C447-JUICIO-04] -->

That softening is the useful part.

**Torpe** is not an insult in ordinary Spanish. It is what people call
themselves after a small mistake, and calling yourself *torpe* is a way of
apologising without making the moment larger. Said of somebody else it is mild
— closer to *butterfingers* than to any real charge.

A word whose ancestor was severe and whose descendant is gentle is worth
noticing, because the direction is not guaranteed. The reverse happens too. If
you learn a word from its etymology alone you will pitch it wrong about half the
time, so the root tells you where a word **came from** and never how hard it
lands today.
