Latin, chapter 301: Rūmor et susurrus.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
susurrus, susurrī — a whisper, a murmur.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for an embassy, then the Latin for to bring back word.

You'll want to know: susurrus, susurrī.
susurrus, susurrī — "a whisper, a murmur".
Per tōtam urbem susurrus erat. There was a whisper all through the city.
Masculine. A sound word: the hum of bees, the rustle of wind, the murmur of gossip. English susurration.

The word, taken apart.
susurrus is a doubled word for a low hum, which Lewis & Short build on a root sur for sound. English susurration comes from its family.

Lesson 2 of 5.
susurrō, susurrāre — to whisper.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to bring back word, then the Latin for a whisper.

You'll want to know: susurrō, susurrāre.
susurrō, susurrāre — "to whisper".
Ancilla dominae aliquid in aurem susurrāvit. The maid whispered something into her mistress's ear.
susurrō, susurrāre, susurrāvī, susurrātum, mostly poetic (Vergil, Ovid). In aurem: into the ear.

The word, taken apart.
susurrō is built on susurrus, "a whisper, a hum" (EtymDB), itself a sound word.

Lesson 3 of 5.
vulgō, vulgāre — to spread abroad, to make public.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a whisper, then the Latin for to whisper.

You'll want to know: vulgō, vulgāre.
vulgō, vulgāre — "to spread abroad, to make public".
Rūmor dē pāce celeriter vulgātus est. The rumour about peace was quickly spread abroad.
vulgō, vulgāre, vulgāvī, vulgātum, also written volgō. Its compound dīvulgō gives English divulge.

The word, taken apart.
vulgō is built on vulgus, "the common people, the crowd" (EtymDB): to put something among the crowd. English vulgar comes from vulgus.

Lesson 4 of 5.
crēbrēscō, crēbrēscere — to grow stronger, to spread (of a rumour).

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to whisper, then the Latin for to spread abroad.

You'll want to know: crēbrēscō, crēbrēscere.
crēbrēscō, crēbrēscere — "to grow stronger, to spread (of a rumour)".
Fāma dē victōriā in diēs crēbrēscēbat. The report of the victory grew stronger day by day.
crēbrēscō, crēbrēscere, crēbruī, from crēber (taught). Tacitus uses it of a rumour that keeps growing.

The word, taken apart.
crēbrēscō is built on crēber, "frequent, close-packed", with the ending -ēscō for something that is becoming so (Lewis & Short): to become frequent.

Lesson 5 of 5.
perhibeō, perhibēre — to assert, to say (that).

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to spread abroad, then the Latin for to grow stronger.

You'll want to know: perhibeō, perhibēre.
perhibeō, perhibēre — "to assert, to say (that)".
Hunc fluvium altissimum esse perhibent. They say this river is very deep.
perhibeō, perhibēre, perhibuī, perhibitum: per + habeō. Perhibent + accusative and infinitive: 'they say that…', the way rumour talks.

The word, taken apart.
perhibeō is built on habeō, "to hold", with per- (EtymDB): to hold out, to put forward, and so to say.
