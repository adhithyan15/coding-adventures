Tamil, chapter 66: Which Way.
8 lessons. You can do the first 2 of them in the car; after that you will want to have stopped.


Lesson 1 of 8.
மேலே (mēlē) — up, above.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
One word back from last chapter first: what did mūḍu mean?

You'll want to know: மேலே.
மேலே (mēlē) — "up, above".
It sits in front of the verb, where every Tamil adverb sits: மேலே பார் (mēlē pār) — "look up". You already have பார் from the seeing lesson, so this is one new word buying you a whole instruction.
Underneath it is மேல் (mēl), the bare word for "the top", a native Dravidian positional word. Kannada says ಮೇಲೆ (mēle) for the same idea, and it is the same word, worn the same way.

Why it's said this way: you have met this ending before.
Listen to the end of the word, and then say the two pointing words you already have. இங்கே (iṅgē, "here") and அங்கே (aṅgē, "there") end the same way. That -ஏ is Tamil's way of turning a bare place-word into "at that place, to that place" — and மேல் plus -ஏ is மேலே (mēlē), "at the top, upward".
So this is not a new machine. It is a machine you already own, running on a new word.

Lesson 2 of 8.
கீழே (kīḻē) — down, below.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say the word for "up", and the instruction you built with it.

You'll want to know: கீழே.
கீழே (kīḻē) — "down, below". The other half of the pair, and built by the same two pieces: கீழ் (kīḻ, "the bottom") plus the -ஏ you met one lesson ago.
கீழே பார் (kīḻē pār) — "look down". Same slot, same verb, opposite direction.

Sounds you'll need.
The middle of this word is ழ (ḻ), the retroflex glide you first met inside மகிழ்ச்சி and again in தமிழ் itself. Curl the tongue back and let the air pass beside it; it is not an English l and it is not an English r.
The vowel in front of it is long: kīḻ, not kiḻ. Tamil separates words by vowel length, so the length is doing work here, not decoration.

Lesson 3 of 8.
மேலே (mēlē) — the same sign twice.

Before we start: this one needs your hands, so it is not a driving lesson. You will want a pen and something to write on, your eyes, for letter shapes on the page and your eyes, because the lesson points at something written down. You can listen to everything else now — leave the sections called Script you'll notice: two letters, one sign, used twice, Script you'll notice: the hook goes in front, the sound comes after and Guided Practice until you have stopped, and I will say so again when we reach it.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Two lessons ago you learned to say mēlē, and kīḻē came between. Now look at it on the page.

Script you'll notice: two letters, one sign, used twice.
Like குடி before it, மேலே (mēlē) brings no new shape. It brings something better: the same vowel sign riding two different letters, one after the other.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
piece: மே, mē — ம with the ே sign, ம from the letter-ma lesson; the ே sign from the vowel-sign lesson, and again inside pēsu.
piece: லே, lē — ல with the ே sign, ல from the letter-la lesson; the same ே again.
மே, லே becomes மேலே (mēlē).
Read it once as two beats. Both beats are long, and both are long for the same reason: the ே hook in front of each letter.

Script you'll notice: the hook goes in front, the sound comes after.
The ே sits to the left of the letter it belongs to, and is said after it. மே is written hook-then-ம and read mē. That is not a quirk of this word — it is how this sign has always worked, and மேலே (mēlē) shows it twice in a row so you can watch the eye do it.

Lesson 4 of 8.
உள்ளே (uḷḷē) — inside.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Read மேலே (mēlē) off the page, and then say its opposite.

You'll want to know: உள்ளே.
உள்ளே (uḷḷē) — "inside, in".
The same shape a third time: உள் (uḷ, "the inside") plus the -ஏ that turns a place-word into a place you can go to.
And now it earns its keep. உள்ளே வா (uḷḷē vā) — "come in." That is what a Tamil household says at the door, and you have had வா since the coming-and-going lesson. One new word, and you can answer a doorway.

Why it's said this way: the doubled ள.
Written out, உள் ends in the retroflex ள், and the ending brings a second one: உள் + ஏ becomes உள்ளே (uḷḷē), with ள்ளே doubled in the middle. Hold it a beat longer than a single ள, the way you already hold the kk in வணக்கம்.
You have met that doubling before without being told it was the same thing: வெள்ளை (veḷḷai, "white") carries it too.

Lesson 5 of 8.
வெளியே (veḷiyē) — outside.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
What do you say at a door to bring someone in?

You'll want to know: வெளியே.
வெளியே (veḷiyē) — "outside, out". The answer to உள்ளே (uḷḷē), and the fourth word built the same way: வெளி (veḷi, "the open, the outside") plus -ஏ.
வெளியே போ (veḷiyē pō) — "go out". Put it beside உள்ளே (uḷḷē) வா and you have a matched pair of instructions, each one an old verb with a new word in front of it.

Why it's said this way: what the ending does to a word ending in a vowel.
உள் ends in a consonant, so -ஏ doubled it. வெளி ends in a vowel, and two vowels cannot sit against each other, so Tamil slides a ய் (y) between them: வெளி + ஏ becomes வெளியே (veḷiyē).
Tamil grammar has a name for that sliding letter: உடம்படுமெய் (uḍambaḍumey), the "body-joining consonant". Its whole job is to stand between two vowels that would otherwise meet, and it is either ய் or வ். Once you know it is there, you stop hearing வெளியே (veḷiyē) as a word with a strange extra letter and start hearing it as வெளி with an ending on it.

Lesson 6 of 8.
வலது (valadu) — the right side.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say the pair you can use at a door.

You'll want to know: வலது.
வலது (valadu) — "the right side, the right-hand one".
It does not take the -ஏ ending, because it is not that kind of word. It goes straight in front of a noun: வலது கை (valadu kai) — "the right hand". You have had கை since the head-and-hand lesson, so again the new word is the only new thing.

Why it's said this way: an ending you already know.
Listen to how it ends, and say the two pointing words again — this time the pair that names things rather than places. இது (idu, "this one") and அது (adu, "that one") end in the same -து. That ending means "the one that is" — so வலது (valadu) is, quite literally, "the right one".
Two endings from the same stretch of the book, and now you can hear the machine from both sides: -ஏ makes a place you can move to, -து makes a thing you can point at.
Tamil's word for the right side sits close to வலி (vali), "strength" — the strong hand naming the side, a link many languages make (English gets dexterity from the Latin for "right"). Take that as the usual account rather than a settled derivation.

Lesson 7 of 8.
இடது (iḍadu) — the left side.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Which hand is the வலது (valadu) கை? Hold it up.

You'll want to know: இடது.
இடது (iḍadu) — "the left side, the left-hand one". The last of the six, and it behaves exactly like வலது (valadu): no -ஏ, straight in front of the noun. இடது கை (iḍadu kai) — "the left hand".
Both hands, in two words you can now hold up and check against each other.

Why it's said this way: the ட you have to place.
Written out, இடது carries ட between two vowels — and you learned in the reading lesson for குடி that a ட in that position softens. So the book writes this word iḍadu, not iṭadu, for the same reason it writes kuḍi and eppaḍi. One letter, read by where it stands.
Tamil's word for the left side looks a great deal like இடம் (iḍam), "place, room" — and dictionaries do connect them. Treat that as the usual account rather than a settled fact; it is the kind of link that is easy to see and hard to prove.
