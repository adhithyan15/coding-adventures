# प्रिय मीरा — a page with somebody at the top of it

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[HI-LEX-C97-PRIYA-01, HI-SCRIPT-DANDA-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say the written opening for a reader called Mira. Then recall the
upright stroke that closes a Hindi sentence.

## Writing: the three parts, with the model in front of you
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[HI-CONCEPT-C97-MESSAGE-01]; assesses=[HI-LEX-C97-PRIYA-01, HI-CONCEPT-C97-ADDRESS-01] -->
<!-- hl-writing-stage: controlled-composition -->

A short Hindi message has three parts stacked down the page:

> **प्रिय मीरा,**
>
> **नमस्ते। कल मिलते हैं।**
>
> **— अरुण**

Copy it once, keeping the three parts on their three lines.

Every word of that is something you have written before. **What is new is the
arrangement** — two sentences you could already produce, lifted onto a page that
now has a reader at the top of it and a writer at the bottom.

## Grammar Lens: why the opening earns its own line
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[HI-CONCEPT-C97-MESSAGE-01, HI-CONCEPT-C97-ADDRESS-01] -->

| part | what it carries |
|---|---|
| प्रिय मीरा, | who is being written to |
| नमस्ते। कल मिलते हैं। | what is being said |
| — अरुण | who is writing |

The opening is not a sentence and takes no **।** after it. A comma does that
work, which is the one piece of punctuation here that behaves the way English
punctuation does.

The two sentences underneath keep their **।** each. Being inside a message
leaves the end of a Hindi sentence exactly as it was.
