# मिलनसार — the opposite of shy, built out of a word you have had for ages

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[HI-LEX-C83-CHAR-04] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say **शर्मीला**. Now think of the opposite kind of person — the one
who is easy with strangers. Hindi builds that word out of something you learned
in order to say goodbye.

## You'll want to know: मिलनसार
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[HI-LEX-C83-CHAR-05]; assesses=[] -->

**मिलनसार** (*milansār*) — "sociable, friendly, easy to get on with."

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[] -->

Two pieces, and you already have the first:

- **मिलन** (*milan*) — a meeting, from **मिलना**, "to meet" — the verb inside
  *phir milenge*, the farewell you learned early
- **-सार** (*-sār*) — a Persian ending meaning *inclined toward, full of*

So a *milansār* person is **inclined toward meeting**. Hindi names the trait
after the act rather than after the feeling, which is a plainer way to describe
a person than English manages: *sociable* goes back to Latin *socius*, a
companion, and is a noun about a relationship; *milansār* is a verb about what
someone actually does.

The **-सार** ending is rare enough to be worth flagging as a small set rather
than a rule to apply. You will meet **शर्मसार** (*śarmsār*), "ashamed" —
built on the same *śaram* as the last lesson.

## Grammar Lens: a consonant ending, so it stands still
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[HI-LEX-C83-CHAR-05, HI-LEX-C83-CHAR-04, HI-LEX-C83-CHAR-02, HI-GRAMMAR-C41-ADJ-SYSTEM] -->

| adjective | masculine | feminine |
|---|---|---|
| शर्मीला | शर्मीला | शर्मीली |
| होशियार | होशियार | होशियार |
| मिलनसार | मिलनसार | मिलनसार |

The pair **शर्मीला** / **मिलनसार** is the sharpest demonstration in the chapter:
two opposite traits, and one bends while the other does not. Meaning has nothing
to do with it. Only the last letter does.
