# el dentista / la dentista — the dentist

## You'll want to know: el dentista
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-C464-CITA-02]; assesses=[ES-LEX-MEDICO] -->

**el dentista**, **la dentista** — the dentist.

*Tengo cita con el dentista* — I've got a dentist's appointment. *Voy al
dentista* — I'm going to the dentist's.

Note *al dentista* for the place as well as the person: Spanish sends you to
the professional, not to the surgery, where English says "to the dentist's".

## Grammar Lens: -ista does not change for gender
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C464-CITA-02, ES-LEX-C464-CITA-01] -->

**-ista** nouns have one form for both, and only the article moves:

| the person | masculine | feminine |
|---|---|---|
| dentist | el dentista | la dentista |
| artist | el artista | la artista |
| tourist | el turista | la turista |

The **-a** ending misleads, because it looks feminine and is not. *El dentista*
is correct and *el dentisto* does not exist.

Compare *el médico / la médica*, which does change. The difference is the
ending: *-ista* is invariable, *-o* is not. Learn the ending and the article
stops being a guess — the same bargain the noun-making suffixes offered.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C464-CITA-02, ES-LEX-C314-BODY-04] -->

Latin **dens**, **dentem** — **tooth** — plus the Greek ending **-istēs**,
which names somebody who does a thing.

So *dentista* is a Latin body part with a Greek job-ending welded on, which is
how most modern profession words were built: *el periodista*, *el electricista*,
*el taxista*. The ending is productive, meaning Spanish still makes new ones.

*Dentem* gives **el diente**, and English **dental**, **dentist** and
**indent** — to cut a tooth-shaped notch. A **dandelion** is *dent de lion*,
a lion's tooth, named for its leaves.
