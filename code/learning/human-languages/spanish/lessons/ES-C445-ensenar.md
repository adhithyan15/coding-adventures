# enseñar — to put a sign into somebody

## You'll want to know: enseñar
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-C445-PUESTO-04]; assesses=[ES-LEX-APRENDER] -->

**enseñar** — to teach, and equally **to show**.

*Enseña matemáticas* — she teaches maths. *¿Me enseñas la foto?* — will you show
me the photo? *Me enseñó a conducir* — he taught me to drive, with **a** before
the second verb.

One word for both jobs, where English keeps *teach* and *show* apart.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C445-PUESTO-04] -->

**en-** in, plus **señ-** from Latin **signum**, a sign or mark.

To *enseñar* is **to mark something into somebody** — to put a sign in them. The
Latin was *insignāre*, and once you see it, *teach* and *show* stop being two
meanings. Both are **putting a mark where somebody can see it**: on a wall, or
in a head.

| word | the sign in it |
|---|---|
| la señal | the sign itself |
| enseñar | to put the sign in |
| diseñar | to mark it **out** — to design |

English took the same *signum* through French into **ensign**, a banner, and
into **design**, which is *de-* plus the mark.

## Why it's said this way
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C445-PUESTO-04, ES-LEX-C358-REF-14] -->

That one verb covering two English ones is not vagueness; it is a different
cut of the same territory, and it goes the other way too.

Spanish splits what English joins all the time — *ser* and *estar* for one
*to be*, *saber* and *conocer* for one *to know*. Here it joins what English
splits. Neither language is being imprecise; they have drawn the lines in
different places, and the only reliable way through is to learn where the line
is rather than to translate across it.

A practical consequence: if you want *show* specifically and fear being
misheard as *teach*, Spanish has **mostrar** — but in speech *enseñar* is far
commoner for both, and the context does the work.
