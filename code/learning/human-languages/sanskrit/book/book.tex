\chapter*{Preface}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Preface}

This is the Sanskrit volume of a wider project --- and, like the Latin volume, it
plays a special role. Sanskrit is a \textbf{taproot}: the classical ancestor of
the Hindi, Marathi, Punjabi, and Bengali tracks, whose every ``hello'' and
``thank you'' is a worn-down form of a Sanskrit word you will meet here in its
full shape. And Sanskrit is at the same time a \emph{sister} of Latin, Greek, and
English --- so its roots reach west (to \emph{thee}, to Greek \emph{eu-}, to
\emph{come}, to \emph{no}) as surely as they reach east. Learning it ties the two
halves of this curriculum together.

Sanskrit is classically written in \textbf{Devanagari}, the script you may
already know from Hindi. This book teaches the reading \emph{inside} the word
lessons, with attention to the features Sanskrit needs even from a Devanagari
reader --- the \emph{visarga} (\d{h}), the vocalic \d{r}, the conjuncts, and the
\emph{sandhi} that fuses words at their seams. (A reader new to the script learns
it here from scratch; one who knows it skims the notes.)

We use standard \textbf{IAST} transliteration (the dotted \d{r}, \d{h}, \'{s}, and
macrons) alongside the Devanagari, and trace roots back toward Proto-Indo-European
where the trail is clear --- every form supplied in full. When Sir William Jones
told the Asiatic Society in 1786 that Sanskrit bore to Latin and Greek ``a
stronger affinity\ldots\ than could possibly have been produced by accident,'' he
launched the whole science of comparative linguistics; this book is, in miniature,
a tour of the words he meant.

Same three commitments as the other volumes: one word per lesson, gone deep; the
widest \emph{honest} web of connections; the reason, not just the rule. Released
under CC~BY-SA~4.0.
