Latin, chapter 512: Gratitude and praise.
6 lessons. All 6 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 6.
grātus, grāta, grātum — welcome, pleasing; grateful.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to ease, then the Latin for tame.

You'll want to know: grātus, grāta, grātum.
grātus, grāta, grātum — "welcome, pleasing; grateful".
Epistula tua mihi grāta est. Your letter is welcome to me.
Two sides: pleasing to someone (with the dative), and grateful for something. Grātiās agō, already known, belongs here. English grateful.

The word, taken apart.
grātus is inherited and traced to a Proto-Indo-European root meaning "to praise, welcome" (EtymDB); Lewis & Short compares Greek charis. English grateful and gratitude come from its family.

Fīlius ingrātus parentēs nōn vīsitat. The ungrateful son does not visit his parents.

Lesson 3 of 6.
meritum, meritī — a kindness, service; merit.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for welcome, then the Latin for ungrateful.

You'll want to know: meritum, meritī.
meritum, meritī — "a kindness, service; merit".
Prō meritīs tuīs tibi grātiās agō. I thank you for your kindnesses.
Neuter, second declension, from mereō — to earn. A good deed that deserves thanks. English merit.

The word, taken apart.
meritum is the neuter past participle of mereō "to earn, deserve", used as a noun (Lewis & Short; EtymDB). English merit comes from it.

Lesson 4 of 6.
laus, laudis — praise.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for ungrateful, then the Latin for a kindness.

You'll want to know: laus, laudis.
laus, laudis — "praise".
Mīles laudem ducis accipit. The soldier receives the general's praise.
Feminine, third declension. Laudō, already known, comes from it. English laud.

The word, taken apart.
laus is a native Latin noun, and laudō "to praise" is built on it (Lewis & Short). Lewis & Short links it with clueō "to be spoken of", but the origin is not settled. English laud and laudable come from the family.

Lesson 5 of 6.
vituperō, vituperāre — to blame, scold.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a kindness, then the Latin for praise.

You'll want to know: vituperō, vituperāre.
vituperō, vituperāre — "to blame, scold".
Magister discipulum pigrum vituperat. The teacher scolds the lazy pupil.
vituperō, vituperāre, vituperāvī, vituperātum, the opposite of laudō. English vituperation.

The word, taken apart.
vituperō is built on vitium "a fault" and parō "to prepare" (Lewis & Short; EtymDB): to make out a fault. English vituperate comes from it.
