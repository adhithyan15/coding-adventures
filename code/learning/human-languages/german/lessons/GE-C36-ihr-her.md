# ihr — "her," and a word with three jobs

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GE-LEX-IHR-01, GE-LEX-IHNEN-01, GE-LEX-SIE-SHE-02, GE-LEX-SEIN-HIS-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] **Ihr** already means *you all*. **Sie** already means *she*, *they*
and formal *you*. German is comfortable with a word doing several jobs, and this
lesson adds one more.

## You'll want to know: ihr
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[GE-LEX-IHR-HER-01]; assesses=[GE-LEX-IHR-01, GE-GRAMMAR-EIN-WOERTER-01, GE-LEX-BRUDER-01, GE-LEX-SCHWESTER-01] -->

> **ihr** — "her".

> **ihr Bruder** · **ihre Schwester** · **ihren Bruder**

Said *eer*: the **h** is silent and stretches the *i*, exactly as it does in
*Ihnen*. The endings are *ein*'s, for the fourth time.

## Grammar Lens: three jobs, all settled by position
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GE-LEX-IHR-HER-01, GE-LEX-IHR-01, GE-GRAMMAR-IHR-T-02, GE-LEX-SIE-SHE-02, GE-GRAMMAR-GROSSSCHREIBUNG-01] -->

Where the word stands says which job it is doing:

- **ihr wohnt** — a verb with *-t* follows, so this is *you all*.
- **ihr Bruder** — a noun follows, so this is *her*.
- **Ihr Bruder**, capitalised — the formal *your*, which the capital marks the
  same way it marks formal *Sie*.

Nothing here is ambiguous in a real sentence, and the capital does real work — a
third reason for the capitalisation rule the writing chapter taught.

The same *ihr* also means *their*, which is one job too many to add today; the
book will come back to it.
