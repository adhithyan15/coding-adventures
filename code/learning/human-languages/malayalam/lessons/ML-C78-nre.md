# അധ്യാപകന്റെ (adhyāpakanṟe) — "the teacher's"

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ML-LEX-ENRE-01, ML-LEX-C48-ROLE-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say *my*. Then say the word for *teacher*.

[PAUSE 2s] You have owned the first of those since the second chapter, and you
have never been able to do to any other word what it does.

## Grammar Lens: the ending was there all along
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ML-GRAMMAR-C78-GENITIVE-NRE-01]; assesses=[ML-LEX-ENRE-01, ML-LEX-C48-ROLE-01, ML-LEX-C46-ASK-05] -->

**എന്റെ** was handed to you whole, as a single word meaning *my*. Look at its
end:

| | |
|---|---|
| എ**ന്റെ** | my |
| അധ്യാപക**ന്റെ** | the teacher's |

**-ന്റെ is a suffix, and it makes whatever it lands on the owner.** That is the
one thing this lesson adds, and it turns a word you memorised into a machine you
can run.

> **അധ്യാപകന്റെ പുസ്തകം**
>
> *adhyāpakanṟe pustakaṁ* — "**the teacher's book**"

The owner comes first, wearing the ending; the thing owned follows bare. English
does the same with *'s* and the opposite with *of*. Malayalam only has the first
order.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ML-GRAMMAR-C78-GENITIVE-NRE-01] -->

The script lesson for **എന്റെ** already showed you **ന്റെ** on its own, as a
conjunct to read. It was a piece of writing then. It is a piece of grammar now,
and it is the same piece.

That is worth expecting from this language: **something introduced as a shape
often turns out later to be a part.**

## Wrap-up Recall
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ML-GRAMMAR-C78-GENITIVE-NRE-01] -->

[PAUSE 3s] What does **-ന്റെ** do to a noun? (**Makes it the owner**.) How do
you say *the teacher's book*? (***അധ്യാപകന്റെ പുസ്തകം***.) And which comes
first, the owner or the thing? (**The owner**.)
