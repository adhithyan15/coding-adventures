# mañana, español — the ñ in the words you are about to need

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-SCRIPT-ENYE, ES-SOUND-ENYE] -->

[PAUSE 2s] You can write **ñ** and you can say it. Two of the next words in
the course carry it, and both are worth a minute on their own — because
neither of them got its *ñ* from a doubled *n*.

## You'll want to know first
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[] -->

- [ñ](./ES-W02-enye.md) — the letter, the sound, and the scribe's shorthand.

## Script — the two forms
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-FORM-MANANA, ES-FORM-ESPANOL]; assesses=[ES-ETYMON-DOUBLE-N] -->

- **mañana** — "tomorrow," and also "morning." *ma-NYA-na*: the *ñ* is in
  the **middle**, and it is the stressed syllable.
- **español** — "Spanish." *es-pa-NYOL*: the *ñ* is at the **end**, right
  before the *-ol*.

Now the honest footnote. The scribe's doubled *nn* is **one** road to *ñ*,
the one you met with *año*. These two words took a **different** road:
Latin's **-ni-** before a vowel. *Mañana* traces back through *maneana* to
**māne** ("morning" — the root of English **matinée**); *español* comes from
**Hispaniolus**, built on **Hispania**. In both, a *ni* sequence squeezed
together into the same single palatal sound.

So *ñ* is where **two** separate Latin sequences ended up — *-nn-* and
*-ni-*. One letter, one sound, two histories.

## Wrap-up Recall
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-SCRIPT-ENYE, ES-SOUND-ENYE, ES-ETYMON-DOUBLE-N, ES-FORM-MANANA, ES-FORM-ESPANOL] -->

[PAUSE 3s] Where does the *ñ* sit in *mañana*, and where in *español*?
(Middle; end.) Did those two get their *ñ* from a doubled *n*? (**No** —
from Latin **-ni-** before a vowel, a second road to the same letter.) What
was the first road? (**-nn-**, the scribe's shorthand, as in *año*.)
