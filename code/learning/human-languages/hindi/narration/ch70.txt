Hindi, chapter 70: Able, and Willing.
4 lessons. You can do the first 2 of them in the car; after that you will want to have stopped.


Lesson 1 of 4.
सकना (saknā) — to be able to.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Two verbs you own: आना, to come, and बोलना, to speak. You can say that you speak. You cannot say that you can speak, and until you can, a whole examiner's question — can you come tomorrow? — has no answer.

You'll want to know: सकना.
सकना — saknā — to be able to; can, may.
मैं हिंदी बोल सकता हूँ. — maiṁ hindī bol saktā hūṁ. — I can speak Hindi.
Hindi has no separate words for can, could, may and be able to. All four run through this one verb, which is why it is worth more than its size.

Grammar Lens: सकना never stands alone.
One move does it: strip the -ना off the first verb and put सकना (saknā) behind it.
बोलना becomes बोल सकता हूँ — I can speak.
आना becomes आ सकता हूँ — I can come.
The bare stem carries the meaning and सकना (saknā) carries the grammar, so it is सकना (saknā) that takes the ending and sits last, exactly where the verb belongs. सकना (saknā) can never appear on its own: there is no Hindi sentence that is just maiṁ saktā hūṁ.
Negate it and you have the most useful excuse in the language: मैं कल नहीं आ सकता. — I can't come tomorrow.

[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
What happens to the first verb in front of सकना (saknā)? (It drops to its bare stem.) Can सकना (saknā) be used on its own? (No — it always needs another verb in front of it.)

Lesson 2 of 4.
चाहना (chāhnā) — to want.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say मैं हिंदी बोल सकता हूँ. You can now say what you are ABLE to do. The next question an interviewer asks is what you WANT to do.

You'll want to know: चाहना.
चाहना — chāhnā — to want.
मैं हिंदी बोलना चाहता हूँ. — maiṁ hindī bolnā chāhtā hūṁ. — I want to speak Hindi.
Put it beside the sentence from last lesson and one letter is different, and it is the important one:
मैं हिंदी बोल सकता हूँ — bare stem बोल.
मैं हिंदी बोलना चाहता हूँ — full infinitive बोलना.
सकना (saknā) takes the stem; चाहना (chāhnā) takes the whole dictionary form. Both then sit last and take the ending themselves. Getting the two mixed up is the ordinary beginner's error, and hearing them back to back is the cure.
मैं आना चाहता हूँ — I want to come. Say it against मैं आ सकता हूँ and listen for the -ना.
Inside चाहना (chāhnā) sits the noun चाह, "longing," and its cousin चाहिए is how Hindi wants a THING rather than an action. They look alike and take opposite kinds of object; this lesson teaches only the verb.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: maiṁ hindī bolnā chāhtā hūṁ]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: the pair back to back — bol saktā hūṁ / bolnā chāhtā hūṁ]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: maiṁ ānā chāhtā hūṁ against maiṁ ā saktā hūṁ]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[question — say your answer, then pause 30 seconds]
Say that you can speak Hindi and then that you want to speak Hindi, and name the difference.

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Which of the two takes the FULL -ना form in front of it? (चाहना (chāhnā).) Which takes the bare stem? (सकना (saknā).)
Sources: Wiktionary: चाहना (chāhnā).


Lesson 3 of 4.
One verb inside another — one verb inside another — the -ना form used as a noun, which is what सकना and चाहना have been doing all along.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, because the lesson points at something written down. You can listen to all of it now and come back to the parts that need looking at once you have stopped.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say both sentences from this chapter: मैं हिंदी बोल सकता हूँ and मैं हिंदी बोलना चाहता हूँ. Something is going on in the second one that is worth naming.

Grammar Lens: the -ना form is a noun.
In मैं हिंदी बोलना चाहता हूँ, the word बोलना is not doing verb work. It is sitting where किताब sits in मैं किताब देखता हूँ — in the object slot. The dictionary form of a Hindi verb is also a noun, and it can go anywhere a noun can go:
मैं किताब देखता हूँ. — I look at the book. — an ordinary noun object.
मैं बोलना चाहता हूँ. — I want to speak. — a VERB in the same slot.
That is a bigger gift than it looks. You have sixty-nine chapters of verbs, and every single one of their -ना forms is now also a thing you can like, want, or talk about.
The order is unchanged, because it never changes: the inner verb comes first as a noun, the outer verb last as the verb. सकना (saknā) is the same construction with one shortcut — it wears the -ना away and takes the bare stem — which is why the two lessons before this one belonged together.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: maiṁ kitāb dekhtā hūṁ]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: maiṁ bolnā chāhtā hūṁ]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: which word in each sentence is the object — kitāb, then bolnā]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[question — say your answer, then pause 30 seconds]
Put a noun and then a verb in the same object slot: say that you look at the book, and that you want to speak.

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
What part of speech is बोलना in मैं बोलना चाहता हूँ? (A noun, in the object slot.) What does सकना (saknā) do to that same form? (Wears the -ना away and takes the bare stem.)
