\chapter{Introducing yourself}
\label{ch:russian-introductions}

The first chapter supplied courtesy moves. This chapter adds pronouns, one
naming construction, its matching question, and a complete first conversation.
Case appears only through the forms the exchange needs. Companion lessons:
\texttt{RU-C02-*.md}.

\section{\texorpdfstring{\ru{я}}{ya} \emph{(ya)} --- I}

\begin{scriptstep}
\ru{я} looks like a mirrored Latin $R$, but it is a vowel letter pronounced
``ya.'' Learn the whole word and letter together: \ru{я} means ``I.'' Do not
copy the Latin sound onto the Cyrillic shape.
\end{scriptstep}

\begin{rootweb}
First-person pronouns resist borrowing. Russian \ru{я} continues Proto-Slavic
*az\u{u}, from the ancient Indo-European pronoun family also behind Latin
\emph{ego}, German \emph{ich}, and English \emph{I}. The modern shapes differ
dramatically because this tiny word has travelled inside each language for
millennia.
\end{rootweb}

\begin{checkpoint}
What does \ru{я} say and mean? ``Ya, I.'' Read it without calling it an $R$.
\end{checkpoint}

\section{\texorpdfstring{\ru{ты, вы}}{ty, vy} \emph{(ty, vy)} --- two forms of you}

\begin{scriptstep}
Both words contain \ru{ы}, a vowel English lacks. Begin with the ``ee'' of
\emph{ill}, then draw the tongue slightly back. \ru{ты} is informal singular;
\ru{вы} is plural or polite. The first letters are familiar \ru{т} $t$ and the
false friend \ru{в} $v$.
\end{scriptstep}

\begin{rootweb}
English once kept a number and familiarity contrast in \emph{thou} and
\emph{you}. Russian still distinguishes \ru{ты} and \ru{вы}. Polite plurals
also appear in French \emph{vous}; German formal \emph{Sie} instead uses a
third-person form, while Spanish \emph{usted} grew from a title meaning ``your
grace.'' Similar social work can arise through different grammar.
\end{rootweb}

\begin{usage}
Use \ru{ты} with one friend or child. Use \ru{вы} with a stranger, in a formal
relationship, or with more than one person. When unsure with an adult stranger,
begin with \ru{вы}.
\end{usage}

\begin{checkpoint}
Choose for one close friend: \ru{ты}. Choose for a new professor: \ru{вы}.
Produce the unfamiliar vowel once without turning it into plain ``ee.''
\end{checkpoint}

\section{\texorpdfstring{\ru{меня зовут ...}}{menya zovut ...}
  \emph{(meny\'a zov\'ut ...)} --- my name is ...}

\begin{scriptstep}
\ru{меня} ends in the new \ru{я} sound; \ru{зовут} reuses \ru{в} $v$ and
\ru{т} $t$. Stress the final syllable of each part:
\emph{me-NYA zo-VOOT}. Insert the name after the verb:
\ru{Меня зовут Анна.}
\end{scriptstep}

\begin{usage}
The literal pattern is ``[they] call me Anna.'' Russian supplies no separate
word for ``my'' or ``name'' here. The plural verb \ru{зовут} has an unnamed
general subject --- people call me --- much like English ``they say it will
rain.'' Learn the frame, not a word-for-word English substitution.
\end{usage}

\begin{rootweb}
\ru{Меня} is the object form paired with subject \ru{я}. English preserves a
small version of this contrast in \emph{I/me}; Russian case changes every noun
and pronoun. No table is due: notice only that ``I'' and ``me'' have different
shapes in both languages.
\end{rootweb}

\begin{checkpoint}
Does the Russian sentence contain a word for ``name''? No. Replace Anna with
your name and say \ru{Меня зовут ...} once.
\end{checkpoint}

\section[Ask a name]{\texorpdfstring{\ru{как вас зовут?}}{kak vas zovut?}
  \emph{(kak vas zov\'ut?)} --- what is your name?}

\begin{scriptstep}
\ru{как} is ``how.'' \ru{вас} is the object form associated here with polite
\ru{вы}; \ru{зовут} is unchanged from your answer. Russian asks literally:
``How do they call you?''
\end{scriptstep}

\begin{rootweb}
Russian \emph{kak}, French \emph{comment}, and Spanish \emph{c\'omo} all frame
the naming question as ``how.'' English asks ``what'' because it treats the
answer as a possessed name. The same social question can be organized around an
action or a thing.
\end{rootweb}

\begin{usage}
Pair the question and answer:
\ru{Как вас зовут?} --- \ru{Меня зовут Анна.} Keep \ru{зовут} exactly the same.
\end{usage}

\begin{checkpoint}
Which word asks ``how''? \ru{как}. Ask the polite question, then answer it with
your name.
\end{checkpoint}

\section{\texorpdfstring{\ru{очень приятно}}{ochen priyatno}
  \emph{(\'ochen priy\'atno)} --- pleased to meet you}

\begin{scriptstep}
\ru{очень} is ``very''; its final \ru{ь} is a soft sign, which has no sound of
its own but changes the consonant before it. \ru{приятно} is ``pleasant.'' Read
the phrase literally as ``very pleasant.''
\end{scriptstep}

\begin{rootweb}
\ru{приятно} belongs to a Slavic family of favour and affection, traced to
Indo-European *preyH- ``love, please.'' Germanic used the same deep family for
English \emph{friend}, a person who is dear; the usual historical account also
connects \emph{free}. Russian and English preserve opposite branches of the
same old idea.
\end{rootweb}

\begin{usage}
Russian needs no spoken ``it is'' in this present-tense expression:
\ru{Очень приятно.} literally ``Very pleasant.'' Use it after names are exchanged.
\end{usage}

\begin{checkpoint}
What does the soft sign pronounce by itself? Nothing. Say the literal two words,
``very pleasant,'' then the Russian phrase.
\end{checkpoint}

\section{A complete formal introduction}

\begin{usage}
Read one turn at a time:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A: \ru{Здравствуйте!}
  \item B: \ru{Здравствуйте!}
  \item A: \ru{Как вас зовут?}
  \item B: \ru{Меня зовут Анна.}
  \item A: \ru{Очень приятно.}
  \item B: \ru{Спасибо.}
\end{enumerate}
Every expression is already known. Only their conversation order is new.
\end{usage}

\begin{checkpoint}
Cover the list. Rebuild the exchange from these cues: formal hello, ask a name,
give a name, pleased to meet you, thank you.
\end{checkpoint}

\section{The informal switch}

\begin{scriptstep}
Case gives the three pronouns different object forms:
\ru{я} ``I'' becomes \ru{меня} ``me''; \ru{ты} becomes \ru{тебя};
\ru{вы} becomes \ru{вас}. Learn these as three pairs, not as a complete case
table.
\end{scriptstep}

\begin{usage}
With one friend, replace \ru{Здравствуйте} with \ru{Привет} and ask
\ru{Как тебя зовут?} The answer stays \ru{Меня зовут ...}; the verb
\ru{зовут} does not change.
\end{usage}

\begin{checkpoint}
Which two pieces change from the formal exchange? The greeting and the object
pronoun: \ru{Здравствуйте / вас} become \ru{Привет / тебя}. Produce the informal
question once.
\end{checkpoint}
