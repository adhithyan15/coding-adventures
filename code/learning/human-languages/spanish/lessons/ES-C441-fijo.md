# fijo — nailed down

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C427-AGENT-06, ES-LEX-C441-TRATO-02] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say **la empresa** and **firmar**. A company offers you two kinds of
contract, and only one of them ends on a date.

## You'll want to know: fijo
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-C441-TRATO-03]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C427-AGENT-06] -->

**fijo** — fixed, permanent, settled.

*Un contrato fijo* is a permanent contract, the one a Spanish job advert
contrasts with *temporal*. *Un precio fijo* is a fixed price. *Un cliente fijo*
is a regular. The verb is **fijar**, to fix or to set: *fijar una fecha* — to set
a date.

It agrees: *fijo*, *fija*, *fijos*, *fijas*.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C441-TRATO-03] -->

Latin **fīgere**, to drive in, to fasten — the verb for putting a stake in the
ground. **Fijo** is its past participle, worn down: *the thing that has been
fastened*.

English took the same participle intact as **fix**, and kept the original sense
in **transfix**, to pin something through.

So the chapter now has three ways of binding, and they are all physical:

| word | the picture |
|---|---|
| contrato | pulled together |
| firmar | made solid |
| fijo | driven in |

## Why it's said this way
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C441-TRATO-03, ES-LEX-C439-VIA-03] -->

**cerrado** is a participle you can still feel as one: *cerrar* is right there,
and nobody would call *cerrado* a plain adjective.

**fijo** is a participle too, and nothing about it feels like one. The verb
*fijar* exists, but it was built backwards *from* the adjective rather than the
other way round, and the form you would expect — *fijado* — also exists and means
something narrower.

That is what age does. A participle used constantly enough stops being read as
the result of an action and becomes a quality in its own right. **Cerrado** is
still at the first stage; **fijo** finished the journey centuries ago.

When you meet a short adjective ending in a bare vowel where a verb also exists
— *fijo*, *limpio*, *seco* — it is worth asking which came first. The answer is
often not the one the modern shape suggests.
