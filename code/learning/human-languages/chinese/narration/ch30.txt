Sounds you'll need.
zěnmeyàng — a dip, a light syllable, then a fall.
zěn is third tone. me is the same toneless syllable that ends 什么. yàng is fourth tone, and it carries the weight of the word.
nǐ zěnmeyàng — How are you doing?
Say it once slowly and once at speed: zěn me yàng.

Why it's said this way — two ways to ask.
You already have 你好吗 nǐ hǎo ma, and it is correct. It is the question textbooks open with.
Between people who know each other, the one you will hear far more often is nǐ zěnmeyàng: "how are things with you?" On its own, zěnmeyàng asks how something is, and putting 你 in front makes the something you.
So keep both. 你好吗 is the careful one; nǐ zěnmeyàng is the one a friend asks.

The word, taken apart.
Mandarin builds zěnmeyàng from three syllables. me is the light 么 from 什么, a character you know. The characters for zěn and yàng come later; this chapter keeps them to the ear.
No dictionary we use records an older form of zěn or yàng, so the history of the word is not settled, and where 么 comes from is not settled either.

Sounds you'll need.
很 is hěn — third tone, the dip, the same as 好.
Its pieces, in words: on the left, the person piece from 你 with one more left-falling stroke above it; on the right, a piece read gěn. The next lesson puts it in your hand.

Grammar Lens: no 是, and a plain 很 instead.
我很好 wǒ hěn hǎo — I'm fine.
In Mandarin an adjective is the whole predicate: there is no 是 in "I am fine", and a plain 很 (hěn) stands in front of 好 instead. Here 很 (hěn) has lost most of its very, so 我很好 is "I'm fine", while a bare 我好 sounds like the start of a contrast — I am fine, but somebody else is not.

The word, taken apart.
很 (hěn) is an old character: the Guangyun records it as honX (in Baxter's transcription), glossed "defiant". How that word came to mean "very" is not settled.

Script — two pieces, left before right.
很 (hěn).
Nine strokes, but two pieces. Do not count to nine. Read the character across, left piece first, the way you write 你.
The left piece, 彳, three strokes. Its lower two are the person piece 亻 you have written since 你: a left-falling stroke, then a vertical down to the foot. 彳 adds one more short left-falling stroke above them. So: a short fall, a longer fall, then the vertical.
The right piece, 艮, six strokes. A horizontal that turns down the right side of a box without lifting. A short horizontal inside it. A horizontal that closes the box. Then the left side, down to the foot, flicking up to the right. A short left-falling stroke on the right, and last the long right-falling stroke.
3 + 6 = nine, and the first three are a shape your hand already knows.

Sounds you'll need.
很好 is hěn hǎo — two third tones side by side.
You met this in the first chapter with 你好, and the same rule fires again: the first of the two rises. Spoken, it is hén hǎo. Written, it stays hěn hǎo, and the characters do not change at all.
Say it slowly once, then at speed, and let 很 (hěn) rise into 好.

Why it's said this way — fine, not very well.
我很好 wǒ hěn hǎo — I'm fine.
That is the answer to nǐ zěnmeyàng and to 你好吗. It means "I'm fine", not "I'm very well": 很 (hěn) is there because 好 is an adjective and wants it in front. Put weight on 很 (hěn), and the very comes back.

Reading — where the words end.
我很好你呢.
Five characters, and nothing on the page marks where one word stops. Find the words before reading on.
[pause 3 seconds]
我 — 很好 (hěn hǎo) — 你 — 呢. Four words; 很好 (hěn hǎo) is the only one with two characters.

The word, taken apart.
很好 (hěn hǎo) is built from 很 and 好. 好 is inherited from Middle Chinese xawX (Guangyun, in Baxter's transcription); 很 is the character the Guangyun records as honX, "defiant", and how it came to mean "very" is not settled.

Sounds you'll need.
máng — second tone, rising, like a surprised "huh?". Its character comes later; this chapter keeps it to the ear.

Grammar Lens: the same shape as 我很好.
máng is an adjective, so it works exactly like 好: no 是, a plain 很 (hěn) in front.
wǒ hěn máng — I'm busy. zǎoshang wǒ hěn máng — I'm busy in the mornings.
zǎoshang goes first, before the person: the time is what the sentence is about, then comes what happens in it.

The word, taken apart.
máng is probably inherited from Middle Chinese mang (Guangyun, in Baxter's transcription), where it is glossed "afraid". Flustered becoming busy is a likely path for the sense, but it is not settled.

lèi — fourth tone, a short clean fall. Its character comes later; this chapter keeps it to the ear.

Grammar Lens: a statement, then 吗.
lèi is an adjective too. Ask with 吗 on the end, and answer with 很 (hěn):
nǐ lèi ma — Are you tired? wǒ hěn lèi — I'm tired.
The question is the statement with 吗 added; nothing moves.

The word, taken apart.
The character for lèi is in the Guangyun, read ljweX and ljweH (Baxter's transcription), but glossed "accumulate" and "implicate". "Tired" is not among its senses there, so how the word came to mean it is not settled.

The last line hands the question back. This lesson gives you a third way to answer it.

Sounds you'll need.
hái xíng — two rising tones.
hái is "still"; xíng is "OK, it works". Together: "still OK" — not bad. Its characters come later; this chapter keeps the word to the ear.
hái xíng, nǐ ne — Not bad. And you?

Why it's said this way — the middle answer.
很好 (hěn hǎo) says things are good. hái xíng says they are all right, and it is the answer people give when they are not going to complain. Said with a shrug, it can mean "could be better".

The word, taken apart.
hái xíng is built from hái "still" and xíng "all right". Both characters are in the Guangyun (Baxter's transcription): the one for hái as hwaen, "return", and the one for xíng as haeng, "walk, go". How they came to mean "still" and "all right" is not settled.

The exchange.
A: 早上好 zǎoshang hǎo — good morning. B: 早上好 — nǐ zěnmeyàng zǎoshang hǎo, nǐ zěnmeyàng — good morning. How are you doing? A: 我很好 你呢 wǒ hěn hǎo, nǐ ne — I'm fine. And you? B: hái xíng, wǒ hěn máng — not bad. I'm busy. A: nǐ lèi ma — are you tired? B: hěn lèi, zàijiàn — I am. Goodbye. A: 再见 zàijiàn — goodbye.
The words whose characters this book has not written yet stay in pinyin; you know them by ear, and here the ear carries them. The morning greeting is one of the first things this book gave you, and this is the first exchange to carry on past it.
