# el bombero — named after the pump, not the fire

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C427-AGENT-03] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say *el policía*. The other emergency service has a name built a
different way again — and it is named after a piece of equipment.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-C427-AGENT-04]; assesses=[] -->

**el bombero** — "the firefighter."

From **la bomba**, which in Spanish means **a pump** as well as a bomb. A
*bombero* is the person who works the pump.

English names the same person after what they fight: *fire*-fighter, *fire*-man.
Spanish names them after what they carry. Two languages looking at the same
person and choosing opposite halves of the scene.

The **-ero** ending is the third person-making ending in this chapter, and it
attaches to **things** rather than to verbs:

| thing | the person who works with it |
|---|---|
| la bomba — the pump | el bomb**ero** |
| el pan — bread | el pan**adero** |
| la carta — the letter | el cart**ero** |

So Spanish has **-dor** for *the one who does the action* and **-ero** for *the
one who works with the thing*.

## Grammar Lens: three endings, three logics
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C427-AGENT-04, ES-LEX-C427-AGENT-01, ES-LEX-C427-AGENT-03] -->

| ending | built from | example |
|---|---|---|
| **-dor** | a verb | vendedor |
| **-ero** | a thing | bombero |
| **-ía** | an institution | policía |

None of the three is guessable from the meaning; all three are readable once you
know which is which. That is the honest position: you cannot predict which
ending a profession takes, but you can take any profession apart.
