# comer es bueno — the infinitive as the subject

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-COMER, ES-GRAMMAR-SER-IDENTITY] -->

[PAUSE 2s] *El libro es grande.* A noun, then *es*, then what it is.

[PAUSE 1s] Now put an **action** in the noun's place.

## Grammar Lens: the infinitive stands there
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-GRAMMAR-INFINITIVE-AS-SUBJECT]; assesses=[ES-MORPH-BUENO-BUENA] -->

> ***Comer*** *es bueno.* — Eating is good.
> ***Hablar*** *español es bueno.* — Speaking Spanish is good.

[PAUSE 2s] The infinitive — the plain dictionary form — simply occupies the
subject slot, and the sentence carries on as normal. Nothing is added and
nothing changes shape.

[PAUSE 2s] Note the verb after it: ***es***, singular. An action counts as one
thing, however many words are wrapped around it.

## Why it's said this way
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-GRAMMAR-INFINITIVE-NOT-GERUND]; assesses=[ES-GRAMMAR-GERUND-AR] -->

Here is the trap, and it is entirely an English one.

[PAUSE 2s] English uses *-ing* for this — *eating is good* — and you have an
*-ing* form now. So it is tempting to reach for ***comiendo***.

> *Comiendo es bueno.* — **wrong.**

[PAUSE 2s] The gerund never does this job in Spanish. It is for an action
**underway**, leaning on *estar*; it is not a name for the activity. The
infinitive is the name.

[PAUSE 2s] So the two languages divide the work differently, and Spanish's
division is the simpler one: English argues about *to eat* against *eating*,
and Spanish uses one form for both.

## Wrap-up Recall
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-GRAMMAR-INFINITIVE-NOT-GERUND] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Which form names an activity — the infinitive or the gerund? (The

[PAUSE 2s] And what is the gerund for? (An action **underway**, with *estar*.)
