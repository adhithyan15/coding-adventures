\section*{The three facts}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Consonants carry a built-in vowel --- and it is \^{o}, not ``a.''}
    \bn{ক} k\^{o}, \bn{ন} n\^{o}, \bn{ম} m\^{o} --- written left to right under a
    connecting top line, like Devanagari but with rounder shapes. This single
    fact (\emph{a}$\to$\emph{\^{o}}) is why Sanskrit words sound ``Bengali.''
  \item \textbf{A vowel sign changes it.} \bn{কা} k\=a, \bn{কি} ki, \bn{কী} k\=\i,
    \bn{কু} ku, \bn{কে} ke, \bn{কো} ko. The \emph{i}-signs \bn{ি}/\bn{ী} are
    written \emph{before} the consonant but read \emph{after} it. A
    \emph{chandrabindu} \bn{ঁ} nasalises the vowel.
  \item \textbf{Standalone vowels have their own letters.} A vowel that
    \emph{starts} a word is written in full: \bn{আ} \=a, \bn{অ} \^{o}, \bn{ই} i,
    \bn{উ} u, \bn{এ} e, \bn{ও} o.
\end{enumerate}

\section*{Worth flagging}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{\emph{s} $\to$ ``sh.''} The letter \bn{স} (and \bn{শ}, \bn{ষ}) is
    usually pronounced ``sh'' --- \bn{আসি} is \emph{\=ashi}, not \emph{\=asi}.
  \item \textbf{No separate \emph{v} or \emph{w}.} Bengali has only \bn{ব}
    \emph{b}, so Sanskrit \emph{v\=ada} $\to$ \emph{b\=ad}, \emph{Vishnu} $\to$
    \emph{Bishnu}.
  \item \textbf{Conjuncts.} A \emph{hasanta} strips a consonant's vowel so it
    fuses with the next: \bn{স্} $+$ \bn{ক} $\to$ \bn{স্ক}, \bn{চ} $+$ \bn{ছ}
    $\to$ \bn{চ্ছ}. Many conjuncts have special shapes you learn as you meet
    them.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Bengali in the family}

Bengali (\emph{B\=angl\=a}) is \textbf{Indo-Aryan} --- from Sanskrit, like Hindi,
Marathi, and Punjabi --- and its script descends, with Devanagari, from the old
Brahmi writing of India, which is why they share the hanging top line. It is
spoken across West Bengal (India) and Bangladesh, and carries an immense literary
tradition --- Tagore's poetry, the anthems of two countries. The lessons trace
its Sanskrit roots and, where they reach, the wider Indo-European family that
also shaped English.
