## Why it's said this way — a goodbye that refuses to be one
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[TA-LEX-C04-POY-VARUGIREN-01, TA-LEX-C04-PO-01, TA-LEX-C04-MINDUM-SANDIPPOM-01, TA-LEX-C04-NAALAI-01, TA-LEX-C04-PRACTICE-01] -->

**போய் வருகிறேன்** is what a Tamil speaker says on leaving, and it does not mean
*goodbye*. It means **I will go and come back**.

| what you say | what it literally says |
|---|---|
| **போய் வருகிறேன்** | I will go and come back |
| **மீண்டும் சந்திப்போம்** | we will meet once more |
| **நாளை பார்க்கலாம்** | tomorrow, let us see |

Read the right-hand column down and the pattern is unmistakable: **not one of
the three names the parting**. Each one names the next meeting instead. A
language can decline to have a word for goodbye, and this one does.

## Grammar Lens: four verbs, two shapes
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[TA-LEX-C05-PESU-01, TA-LEX-C05-VAAZH-01, TA-LEX-C05-VELAI-SEY-01, TA-LEX-C05-NAAN-TAMIZH-PESUGIREN-01, TA-LEX-C05-PRACTICE-01] -->

**போ** *go* · **பேசு** *speak* · **வாழ்** *live, flourish* — three plain stems.
Then **வேலை செய்**, which is not one word but two: *work* plus *do*.

That second shape is worth naming, because Tamil uses it constantly. A noun
parked in front of **செய்** makes a verb out of it, which is how the language
keeps borrowing things to do without borrowing verbs to do them with.

And **நான் தமிழ் பேசுகிறேன்** shows what the stem does under pressure: **பேசு**
takes a tense piece and then a person piece, in that order, and the speaker is
named at both ends of the sentence.
