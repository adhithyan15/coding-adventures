# trabajador — the same ending as actor, four hundred years further along

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-C423-CHAR-03]; assesses=[] -->

**trabajador** — "hard-working." Take *trabajar*, drop the **-ar**, add
**-dor**.

That ending is the Latin **-tor** from *actor*, worn down by centuries of
Spanish mouths. Latin *-tor* became Spanish *-dor* between vowels, and Spanish
then used it on its own verbs to build new words the Romans never had:

| verb | meaning | the one who does it |
|---|---|---|
| trabajar | to work | trabaja**dor** |
| jugar | to play | juga**dor** |
| hablar | to speak | habla**dor** |
| ganar | to win | gana**dor** |

*Actor* was borrowed from Latin whole and kept the old **-tor**. *Trabajador*
was built inside Spanish and got the modern **-dor**. Same ending, two
different ages, and the pair tells you which words came in through books and
which grew at home.

Here is the piece that surprises people: *trabajador* is **both a noun and an
adjective**. *Un trabajador* is a worker. *Es trabajador* means he is
hard-working. The word does not change; only the sentence around it does.

## What you've built
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C423-CHAR-03, ES-LEX-C423-CHAR-02] -->

A third shape of adjective, and it is not the **-o** one:

| adjective | masculine | feminine |
|---|---|---|
| antipático | antipático | antipátic**a** |
| inteligente | inteligente | inteligente |
| trabajador | trabajador | trabajador**a** |

An adjective ending in a consonant normally refuses to agree. **-dor** is one
of the exceptions that does: it adds **-a** and nothing else. *Una mujer
trabajadora.*
