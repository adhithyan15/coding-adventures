Latin, chapter 507: Pride and arrogance.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
superbia, superbiae — pride, haughtiness.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for country manners, then the Latin for generosity.

You'll want to know: superbia, superbiae.
superbia, superbiae — "pride, haughtiness".
Superbia rēgis cīvēs vexat. The king's arrogance troubles the citizens.
Feminine, first declension, from superbus. Usually a fault in Latin, not a virtue.

The word, taken apart.
superbia is built on superbus "proud", which Lewis & Short and EtymDB take from super "above": one who holds himself above others.

Lesson 2 of 5.
arrogāns, arrogantis — arrogant, presumptuous.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for generosity, then the Latin for pride.

You'll want to know: arrogāns, arrogantis.
arrogāns, arrogantis — "arrogant, presumptuous".
Iuvenis arrogāns nēminem audit. The arrogant young man listens to no one.
One ending. The present participle of arrogō — to claim for oneself. English arrogant.

The word, taken apart.
arrogāns is the present participle of arrogō "to claim for oneself", built on ad- and rogō "to ask" (Lewis & Short). English arrogant comes from it.

Lesson 4 of 5.
īnsolēns, īnsolentis — insolent, haughty; unaccustomed.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for arrogant, then the Latin for arrogance.

You'll want to know: īnsolēns, īnsolentis.
īnsolēns, īnsolentis — "insolent, haughty; unaccustomed".
Mīles īnsolēns agricolīs male dīcit. The insolent soldier speaks abusively to the farmers.
One ending. First unaccustomed (in- + soleō), then behaving beyond the usual bounds. English insolent.

The word, taken apart.
īnsolēns is built on in- "not" and solēns, the participle of soleō "to be accustomed" (Lewis & Short). English insolent comes from it.

Lesson 5 of 5.
vānitās, vānitātis — boastfulness, vanity; emptiness.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for arrogance, then the Latin for insolent.

You'll want to know: vānitās, vānitātis.
vānitās, vānitātis — "boastfulness, vanity; emptiness".
Vānitās eius rīdicula est. His boastfulness is ridiculous.
Feminine, third declension, from vānus — empty. English vanity.

The word, taken apart.
vānitās is built on vānus "empty" (Lewis & Short), which EtymDB traces to a Proto-Indo-European root meaning "empty". English vanity and vain come from the same family.
