# The pair rule — ਧੰਨਵਾਦ and ਸ਼ੁਕਰੀਆ

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[PA-LEX-DHANVAAD-01, PA-LEX-SHUKRIYA-01, PA-JOIN-PAR-01, PA-LEX-PATA-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] From the very first chapter of this book, the two ways to say
**thank you**. And from two lessons back, the two ways to say **but**.

- [YOU RECALL: *dhannavād*, *shukrīā* — then *par*, *lekin*]
- [YOU RECALL: *mainū̃ nahī̃ patā* — from two chapters back]

You have owned that first pair since page one and have never been told which to
choose.

## The pattern, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[PA-REG-DOUBLET-01]; assesses=[PA-LEX-DHANVAAD-01, PA-LEX-SHUKRIYA-01, PA-JOIN-LEKIN-01] -->

Both pairs are the same shape, and it runs through the whole language:

- **ਧੰਨਵਾਦ** · **ਪਰ** — the **inherited and Sanskritic** side
- **ਸ਼ੁਕਰੀਆ** · **ਲੇਕਿਨ** — the **Perso-Arabic** side

Punjabi has drawn on both streams for centuries, so a great many everyday ideas
have two perfectly good words. What the choice carries:

- The Sanskritic word leans **formal and written**, and is the one you will most
  often see in print and hear in institutional or Sikh religious settings.
- The Perso-Arabic word leans **everyday and spoken**, and is more usual in
  Pakistani Punjabi and in Muslim communities.
- **Both are understood everywhere by everyone.** Choosing the other one is
  never an error, and it is never taken as one.

So this is not a rule about correctness. It is a rule about **what your choice
tells the listener** — which is the same thing **ਤੂੰ** against **ਤੁਸੀਂ** told
them, and the same thing the book's three greetings told them.
