Spanish, chapter 438: El Cuerpo Y El Descanso.
7 lessons. All 7 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 7.
el polideportivo — one roof, many sports.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say el deporte and el gimnasio. A gimnasio holds one room of machines. Now picture a building that holds a pool, a court and a running track at once.

You'll want to know: el polideportivo.
el polideportivo — the municipal sports centre.
Every town of any size has one, and its sign is the one you read to find out what the pool costs and when the court is free. It is a deporte word, so it is masculine: el polideportivo, los polideportivos.

The word, taken apart.
Three pieces, and the middle one is already yours.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
piece: poli-. where it comes from: Greek polýs, many.
piece: deportiv-. where it comes from: from deporte, sport.
piece: -o. where it comes from: masculine ending.
Many-sport-place. Spanish did not borrow this word from anywhere; it built it, in the twentieth century, out of a Greek prefix and a Latin stem, because town councils needed a name for a thing that did not exist before.
That Greek poli- is the same one inside polígono and poliglota. It is not the poli- of policía, which is a different Greek word entirely — pólis, the city. Two prefixes that look identical and mean nothing like each other.

Lesson 2 of 7.
la natación — the same root, arriving twice.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say nadar. Now say el polideportivo. The timetable on its door does not list nadar; it lists a noun.

You'll want to know: la natación.
la natación — swimming, the activity as a named thing.
Hago natación los martes — I swim on Tuesdays. Un curso de natación — a swimming course. nadar is what your arms do; la natación is what the timetable, the club and the medal call it.

The word, taken apart.
nat- from Latin natāre, to swim, plus -ción, the ending you met on documentación for turning an action into a thing.
documentar gave documentación; natar gives natación. Same machine.

Why it's said this way.
Here is the part worth slowing down for. nadar and la natación come from the same Latin word, and they do not look alike. That is because they took different roads out of Latin.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
the word: nadar. the road it took: carried in every mouth, worn smooth.
the word: natación. the road it took: lifted off the page, centuries later.
natāre was spoken without a break for a thousand years, and a thousand years of speaking wore the t soft into a d and dropped a syllable: natāre became nadar. Then scholars who could read Latin needed a formal noun, went back to the written form, and took natātiōnem out intact.
A pair like this is called a doublet: one word, two arrivals, both alive. Once you can see them, Spanish is full of them, and the learned twin is nearly always the one on the official sign.

Lesson 3 of 7.
torcerse — the ankle that turns the wrong way.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say la natación, el pie and el tobillo. Getting out of the pool, the tiled edge is wet. Name the part of you that is about to go wrong.

You'll want to know: torcerse.
torcer — to twist something. torcerse — to twist a part of yourself.
Me he torcido el pie — I have twisted my foot. This is the sentence a Spanish doctor's waiting room hears twenty times a week, and it is the one you want ready before you need it.
Spanish does not say my foot here. It says el pie and lets me carry the ownership: me torcí el tobillo, not mi tobillo. When the body part is yours and the verb is reflexive, the possessive is redundant and Spanish drops it.

The word, taken apart.
Latin torquēre, to twist. It is one of those roots that stayed busy.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
word: torcer. the twist in it: a joint, the wrong way.
word: torcido. the twist in it: crooked, not straight.
word: antorcha. the twist in it: a torch, of twisted rags.
English took the same root twice over: torque, the twisting force on a shaft, and torture, which needs no explanation once you know the root.

Grammar Lens: the stem that breaks.
dormir taught you that some verbs split their vowel when the stress lands on it: dormir but duermo. torcer does the same thing.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
form: torcer, torcemos. vowel: o, unstressed.
form: me tuerzo, se tuerce. vowel: ue, stressed.
What decides it is stress, not person. Wherever the stress falls on the stem vowel, the o breaks into ue; wherever the ending pulls the stress away, the o holds. That is why torcemos keeps its o while tuerce does not — and why you never have to memorise which forms change.

Lesson 4 of 7.
descansar — to un-tire yourself.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say cansado, the word for a body with nothing left in it. Say torcerse. You have twisted your ankle and you are tired. Name what the doctor is about to tell you to do.

You'll want to know: descansar.
descansar — to rest.
Tiene que descansar dos días — you have to rest for two days. Descanse on a sign at a viewpoint or a clinic is an invitation to sit. The related noun el descanso is what a timetable calls a break, and what a football match calls half-time.

The word, taken apart.
des- plus cansar, and you already own the second half.
Cansado came from Latin campsāre, which was a sailor's word: to turn a ship round a headland. Turning the ship round, over and over, was the hard work that wore a crew out, and the word slid from the manoeuvre to the exhaustion it caused.
des- is the undoing prefix. Put it on cansar and you get the reversal:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
word: cansar. what it does to the tiredness: puts it there.
word: descansar. what it does to the tiredness: takes it away.

Why it's said this way.
English rest names a condition you are in. Spanish descansar names an action you perform on a condition: you go in and remove the tiredness.
That is not a curiosity, it changes how the word behaves. Because it is a reversal and not a state, it takes a duration the way any action does — descansé media hora, I rested for half an hour — where English has to reach for had a rest to make rest countable.
The prefix is productive, so the move is reusable. des- on a verb you already know gives you its undoing, and you can often guess the result before anyone teaches it to you.

Lesson 5 of 7.
reír — the prefix that is not there.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say la risa, the noun for the sound itself. Say descansar. You are resting a twisted ankle and the whole thing is faintly ridiculous. Name the verb risa belongs to.

You'll want to know: reír.
reír — to laugh. reírse de — to laugh at something.
Nos reímos mucho — we laughed a lot. No te rías — do not laugh. And sonreír, with son- from Latin sub-, under: a laugh kept low, which is exactly what a smile is.

The word, taken apart.
Here is the trap, and it is worth setting off deliberately.
reír looks like re- plus ír. It is neither. There is no prefix and there is no ir. The whole word is Latin rīdēre, to laugh, arriving in one piece — the r-e at the front is nothing more than the first two letters of the root.
You met batería, a word that wears an ending it never earned. This is the other half of that lesson: a word that wears a prefix it never had. Spanish has both kinds, and neither announces itself.

Why it's said this way.
The written accent on reír is physical evidence, and you can read it.
Rīdēre had a d between its vowels: ri-d-ere. Spanish wore intervocalic d away in word after word, and when it went here it left the i and the e standing next to each other with nothing between them.
Two vowels side by side would normally collapse into one syllable. These do not, because they were never together — a consonant used to hold them apart. The accent on the í is how Spanish writes down that refusal: re-ír, two syllables, the join still visible where the d used to be.
The family keeps the evidence too: la risa lost the same d, while ridículo was borrowed back from Latin late enough to keep it. One root, one missing consonant, and a word on each side of the loss.

Lesson 6 of 7.
(polideportivo, natación, torcerse, descansar, reír) — from cold.

Warm-up.
[pause 3 seconds]
Close the lessons before this one. Say all five: the sports centre, swimming, to twist your ankle, to rest, to laugh.

Grammar Lens: pieces that are real, and pieces that are not.
Three of this chapter's words look built. Two of them are.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
word: polideportivo. the front piece: real — Greek polýs, many.
word: descansar. the front piece: real — des-, the undoing.
word: reír. the front piece: not a piece at all.
The test is not how the word looks; it is whether the rest of the word survives without the piece. Take poli- off and deportivo stands. Take des- off and cansar stands. Take re- off reír and nothing is left, which is the proof that it was never a prefix.
Carry that test forward. It is the one move that keeps morphology from turning into guesswork, and it costs you a second per word.

What you've built.
You can now say where you exercise, what goes wrong there, what you have to do about it, and how you feel about the whole business. That is a complete small episode, and it is the shape a clinic conversation and a message to a friend both take.
You also have a second doublet. nadar and la natación are one Latin verb arriving twice, once worn down by mouths and once lifted off the page — and the learned twin is the one on the timetable.

Why it's said this way.
Now a message from a friend who went anyway, on a morning.
Fui por la mañana y me torcí el tobillo saliendo de la piscina. Nada grave: el médico dice que tengo que descansar tres días. Luego nos reímos, porque llevaba veinte años sin caerme en ningún sitio.
Notice what each verb is doing. Me torcí puts the injury on the speaker without blaming anyone. Descansar is an instruction with a duration attached. Nos reímos closes the episode, and that closing move is what a Spanish speaker expects at the end of a small misfortune: name it, size it, laugh it off.
A report that stops after me torcí el tobillo sounds like a complaint. The same report with nos reímos on the end sounds like a story. Same facts, and the last clause decides which one the listener hears.
