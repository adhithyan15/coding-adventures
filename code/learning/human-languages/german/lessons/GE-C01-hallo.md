# hallo — "hello," and this time it *is* a cousin

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Your first German word — and a reassuring one, because German and
English are close cousins: *both are Germanic languages*. That kinship will
help you all through this book, and *hallo* is the first proof.

## Sounds you'll need
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[GE-SOUND-HALLO-02]; assesses=[] -->

- German **h** is pronounced (not silent like Spanish/French): *HA-lo*.
- German **a** is a clean, open *ah*.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[GE-LEX-HALLO-01, GE-ETYMON-HALLO-03]; assesses=[] -->

**hallo** — informal "hello / hi." Unlike Spanish *hola* (which only *looks*
like "hello" by coincidence — a false friend), German **hallo** and English
**hello** are the real thing: both grew from the same old Germanic hailing
call (*hala*, a shout to get attention or hail a ferryman), the same family as
English *holla*, *hollo*, and *hail*. Nineteenth-century English "hello" was
even shaped partly by German *hallo*.

> The pattern to carry through this whole book: because English is a Germanic
> language, German words are often **direct cousins** of English words — same
> family, no Latin middleman. (The Romance languages like Spanish and French
> reach English mostly *through* Latin; German reaches it directly.)

## Why it's said this way
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GE-LEX-HALLO-01] -->

*hallo* is informal-to-neutral — fine among friends, and increasingly common
generally. Polite or first meetings often use a daytime greeting instead. You
will build that greeting one familiar piece at a time over the next few
lessons.

## Writing — follow one word you can see
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GE-LEX-HALLO-01] -->
<!-- hl-writing-stage: observe-trace -->

Keep **Hallo** visible. Trace directly over it, then copy it once in large,
slow letters. Say *HA-lo* while your hand moves. Stop there.

This is not spelling from memory and not free writing. Your eye and hand are
only following a word whose meaning and sound are already on the page.
