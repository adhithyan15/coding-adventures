# ê ç ï — three marks, three different jobs

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[FR-SCRIPT-CIRCUMFLEX-08] -->

[PAUSE 3s] **forêt**, **hôpital**, **île**. One letter is missing from each, and
English still has it. Say which letter, before reading on.

## Script: what each mark is actually for
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[FR-SCRIPT-CIRCUMFLEX-08, FR-SCRIPT-CEDILLE-09, FR-SCRIPT-TREMA-10] -->

The three arrived a page apart and do three unrelated jobs. Sorted by what they
change, they stop looking like a single topic called *accents*:

| mark | what it changes |
|---|---|
| **ê** the circumflex | **nothing you say** — it records an *s* that fell out |
| **ç** the cédille | **the sound** — it keeps **c** soft before a, o, u |
| **ï** the tréma | **the grouping** — read this vowel on its own |

The circumflex is the odd one and the useful one. French dropped an *s* before
certain consonants and left a hat where it stood; English borrowed the older
spelling and kept the *s*. So **forêt** is forest, **hôpital** is hospital,
**île** is isle. **A hat is a decoder ring**, not a pronunciation instruction.

The other two are instructions. **ç** exists because **c** would otherwise go
hard before those three vowels; **ï** exists because two vowels side by side
would otherwise be read as one sound.

## Wrap-up Recall
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[FR-SCRIPT-CIRCUMFLEX-08, FR-SCRIPT-CEDILLE-09, FR-SCRIPT-TREMA-10] -->

[PAUSE 3s] Which of the three changes nothing you say? (**The circumflex**.) What does it record? (**A lost *s***, which English usually still has.) What does the cédille stop happening? (**A hard c** before a, o, u.) And what does the tréma ask you to do? (**Read the vowel separately**.)
