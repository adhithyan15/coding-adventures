Spanish, chapter 474: El curso que paga la empresa.
6 lessons. All 6 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 6.
invitar — to invite.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say el invitado and pagar. Today's verb made the first, and in one very common use it means the second.

You'll want to know: invitar.
invitar — to invite.
Nos invitaron a la fiesta.
You have met this verb once without being handed it. El invitado named it in passing — "from invitar, Latin invitare, to invite; invitado is its participle" — and then stayed with the guest. Here it is as yours.
Invitar a alguien a algo — the person with a, and the thing with a as well.

Grammar Lens: in Spanish, inviting can mean paying.
This is the use you will meet first in real life, and English has no single word for it.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
te invito means I'm paying.
invito yo means this one's on me.
Somebody who says te invito a un café is not asking whether you are free. They are telling you they will pay for it. Answering vale, ¿a qué hora? when the coffee is in front of you will land oddly.
So invitar covers both the asking and the paying, and which one is meant is settled by whether a time is mentioned.

The word, taken apart.
Latin invitare, to invite — and the honest answer about what is inside it is that nobody is sure. The older guess tied it to a root about willingness, which would make an invitation an appeal to somebody's good will.
English took the whole word twice: invite, and the noun invitation. There is no native English word for this, which is a small clue that the custom travelled with the Latin.
What Spanish added is the paying. That is not in the Latin and not in the English — it is a courtesy that attached itself to the word here.

Lesson 2 of 6.
el importe — the amount.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say importar and importante. Today's noun is the third member of that family, and it is the one that appears on a bill.

You'll want to know: el importe.
el importe — the amount, the sum.
Hay que pagar el importe completo.
El importe total — the total. El importe del curso — the fee for a class. This is a form-and-invoice word: in speech people say cuánto es, but anything printed says importe.

Grammar Lens: one verb, three jobs.
Importar told you two of these. Here is the third, and seeing them together is what makes the word stick.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
the sense: to matter. example: no me importa.
the sense: to import goods. example: importar café.
the sense: to amount to. example: el importe.
The thread is Latin in- plus portare, to carry in. Goods carried in are imports. A thing that carries weight matters. And what a bill carries in for you to pay is its importe.
So the noun is not a fourth meaning bolted on. It is the same carrying, applied to money.

The word, taken apart.
Portare is the carrying verb, and once you have it a great many words open at once. Spanish has already given you two of its children without saying so:
el deporte — dis- plus portare, carrying yourself away from work;
el comportamiento — carrying yourself with, which is behaviour.
English carries the same root in port, portable, export, transport and report — a thing carried back.
And importante is literally carrying-in-ish: something that brings weight with it.

Lesson 3 of 6.
descontar — to deduct.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say contar and el importe. Put des- on the first and you have what happens to the second.

You'll want to know: descontar.
descontar — to deduct, to take off.
Te lo descuentan en doce meses.
El descuento is the noun — a discount. Con descuento — at a reduced price.
Note the stem: descuento, descuentan. The o breaks into ue under stress, exactly as it does in contar itself — cuento, cuentas.

Grammar Lens: the des- that reverses, and the one that does not.
Cubrir set this out plainly: des- sometimes undoes the verb and sometimes does nothing of the kind, and only the whole word tells you which.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
word: descubrir. what des- does: takes the cover off — it reverses.
word: disfrutar. what des- does: nothing — the prefix does not reverse.
word: descontar. what des- does: takes off the count — it reverses.
This one is in the first camp, and cleanly. Contar adds up; descontar takes away from the total. If you can count the thing, des- will subtract it.
Descansar is the same family — des- undoing cansar, to tire — and it is where you first met the prefix doing real work.

The word, taken apart.
Contar is Latin computare: com-, together, plus putare, to reckon. So counting is a reckoning-together, and descontar is a reckoning taken back out.
That computare is where English compute and count both come from — the second worn down through French — so contar and count are the same word wearing different amounts of Latin.
And putare first meant to prune a tree. Reckoning, in Latin, began as cutting away what does not belong, which makes descontar an oddly exact return to the root.

Lesson 4 of 6.
el golpe — the blow.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say descontar and pagar. If the money is not taken off in instalments, it comes in one of today's word.

You'll want to know: el golpe.
el golpe — the blow, the knock.
No te lo cobran de golpe.
The literal sense is a hit: un golpe en la puerta, a knock at the door.
But the phrase is what you need: de golpe — all at once, suddenly.

Grammar Lens: the phrase a payment plan is defined against.
Spanish has two ways to say all at once, and a notice about money will use one to rule out the other.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
phrase: de golpe. what it pictures: in one blow — suddenly, often unwelcome.
phrase: de una vez. what it pictures: in a single go — neutral.
De golpe carries the impact. Se me olvidó de golpe — it went out of my head all at once. Subió de golpe — it shot up.
So no de golpe about a fee is doing more than saying in instalments: it is saying you will not be hit with it. That is why the reassurance lands, and why a question about it may use the calmer de una vez instead.

The word, taken apart.
Latin colaphus, a blow with the fist, borrowed from Greek kólaphos, a box on the ear.
The road from colaphus to golpe is worth a look, because two things happened: the c softened to g, and the middle syllable fell out entirely — co-la-phus worn down to gol-pe. That is ordinary wear, and it left a word that no longer looks Greek at all.
English has the same blow in coup, taken from French rather than Spanish and kept intact: a coup is one sharp strike, which is what a golpe is. Spanish says golpe de estado where English says coup d'état — the same two words, twice.
French went further and made a quantity out of it. Beaucoup is beau plus coup, a beautiful blow, and it means a lot — so the same punch that gives Spanish its golpe gives French its word for plenty.

Lesson 5 of 6.
(invitar, el importe, descontar, el golpe) — from cold.

Warm-up.
[pause 3 seconds]
Four words, no prompts. What somebody does when they are paying, what the bill says, what happens to it over twelve months, and what it would have been if it had all come at once.

You'll want to know: the four, cold.
[a table of 4 rows, read aloud]
English: to invite. Spanish: invitar.
English: the amount. Spanish: el importe.
English: to deduct. Spanish: descontar.
English: the blow. Spanish: el golpe.

Grammar Lens: three of the four were already in the room.
Only one of these arrived from nowhere.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
word: invitar. what it came out of: el invitado, its own participle.
word: el importe. what it came out of: importar, its third sense.
word: descontar. what it came out of: contar, with des- reversing.
El golpe is the outsider, and even it has a relative you know in English.
So the chapter was mostly a matter of being handed things the book had been holding: invitar had been named inside el invitado, and importar had told you two of its three jobs already.

The words, taken apart.
Two roots do most of the work here.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
root: portare, to carry. what it gave: importe, deporte, comportamiento.
root: computare, to reckon. what it gave: descontar, contar, English count.
And golpe is Latin colaphus from Greek kólaphos, a box on the ear — the same blow English keeps in coup, and the one French built beaucoup out of. Golpe de estado and coup d'état are the same two words said twice.

Lesson 6 of 6.
(el curso que paga la empresa) — match it.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say el importe and de golpe. The exchange below turns on whether the second applies to the first.

Reading: the exchange, and the statement.
— El setenta por ciento. El resto lo pones tú, pero te lo descuentan en doce meses, no de golpe. — Ah, eso no lo sabía.
And the statement you have to match to it:
Pensaba que había que pagar el importe completo de una vez.

Grammar Lens: the match is between two phrases, not two words.
This is a matching task, so nothing is quoted back at you. The speaker and the statement describe the same fact in different words, and you have to see it.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
the speaker says: no de golpe. the statement says: de una vez.
the speaker says: te lo descuentan en doce meses. the statement says: el importe completo.
Neither phrase appears in the other. De golpe and de una vez are the pair that carries the match, and if you only hold one of them the item is closed to you even though you understood every sentence.
That is worth knowing in general: a matching task rewards paraphrase, so the words to learn are the ones that come in pairs.

Guided Practice.
[pause 3 seconds]
Say what the company pays and what happens to the rest, using descontar.
[pause 3 seconds]
Now say the misunderstanding twice — once with de golpe and once with de una vez — and hear that they are the same claim.

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
What happens to the rest? (Te lo descuentan en doce meses.) Not how? (De golpe.) What did the listener think? (Que había que pagar el importe completo de una vez.)
