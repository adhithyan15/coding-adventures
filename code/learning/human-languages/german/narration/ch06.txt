German, chapter 6: Numbers One to Ten.
2 lessons. The first lesson already needs your eyes or your hands, so save this one for when you have stopped.


Lesson 1 of 2.
eins, zwei, drei, vier, fünf — counting to five.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes for one table that cannot be read aloud. You can listen to everything else now — leave the section called The numbers, taken apart until you have stopped, and I will say so again when we reach it.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Here's where German feels closer to English than any Romance language does. German numbers aren't borrowed from Latin like Spanish or French — they're German's own Germanic words, and they're the near-twins of English one, two, three, four, five. The reason is a single sound-law.

Sounds you'll need.
diphthong-ei — eins, zwei, drei all carry the ei = English "eye" sound: eyns, tsvy, dry.
umlaut-ue — fünf: the ü is "ee" said with rounded lips; fünf ≈ fewnf.

The numbers, taken apart.
There is a table here I cannot read to you — 4 columns and 5 rows, and it has more columns than can be held in the ear at once. Its columns are: German, English twin, shared ancestor, Latin cousin. Come back and look at it when you have stopped.
German and English are siblings; Latin is a cousin. So drei isn't from Latin trēs — both descend from one prehistoric word, and Grimm's Law is the recipe for the differences: an old t became Germanic th/d (trēs becomes three becomes drei), and an old p became f (pénkʷe becomes five / fünf, while Latin kept it as quīnque). Say fünf and five back to back — same word, one p-turned-f apart.

Grammar Lens: eins becomes ein / eine (one = a/an).
Like English one/an and Spanish uno/un, German eins becomes the article ein (masc./neut.) / eine (fem.) before a noun: ein Kaffee "a/one coffee," eine Katze "a/one cat." The counting form is eins; the article drops the -s. The other numbers don't change for the article.

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Give 1–5 in German. (Eins, zwei, drei, vier, fünf.) Are these borrowed from Latin? (No — Germanic twins of English, cousins of Latin via Grimm's Law.) What sound-change links fünf and five to Latin quīnque? (Old p becomes Germanic f.) Next: 6–10.


Lesson 2 of 2.
sechs, sieben, acht, neun, zehn — finishing the count.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes for one table that cannot be read aloud. You can listen to everything else now — leave the section called The numbers, taken apart until you have stopped, and I will say so again when we reach it.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
The rest of the way to ten — and again, each is the twin of its English number, with Latin as the cousin one sound-shift away. Two of them show Grimm's Law beautifully.

Sounds you'll need.
ch-ach — acht: the ch is the raspy back-of-throat sound (as in Bach): akht. It matches the gh in English eight — both once a real throat sound.
diphthong-eu — neun = noyn (the eu = "oy").

The numbers, taken apart.
There is a table here I cannot read to you — 4 columns and 5 rows, and it has more columns than can be held in the ear at once. Its columns are: German, English twin, shared ancestor, Latin cousin. Come back and look at it when you have stopped.
Two showpieces of Grimm's Law:
zehn corresponds to ten corresponds to decem: an old d became Germanic t — so Latin kept decem, English shifted to ten, and German went one hop further (t becomes z) to zehn. Same number, three snapshots of one consonant drifting.
acht corresponds to eight corresponds to Nacht/night: the German -cht- answers English -ght- every time (acht/eight, Nacht/night, Licht/light) — a fossil of a throat sound both languages once had.

Grammar Lens: German borrowed the months, not the numbers.
English and German both took their month names straight from Latin — September, Oktober, November, Dezember in German too. So the same "off by two" trick works: those months mean Latin 7, 8, 9, 10 (the Roman year began in March), even though German's own numbers sieben/acht/neun/zehn look nothing like them. The numbers are homegrown; the month labels are Latin imports.
