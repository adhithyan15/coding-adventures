Sanskrit, chapter 45: Times of the Day.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
प्रातः (prātaḥ) — morning.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new word: what did nīlaḥ mean?

You'll want to know: प्रातः.
प्रातः (prātaḥ) — "morning".
प्रातः (prātaḥ) is early morning: the stretch before the sun stands high, and the hour the first greeting of the day belongs to.
You have said pra- at the front of a word twice already, in प्रणामः and in प्रसन्नः, where it carries the sense "forward, out in front". प्रातः (prātaḥ) is the front end of the day.
Greek prōi, "early", is the same small piece put to the same work, and Latin prae is its cousin; English carries it as the pre- in front of a thousand words.

What you've built.
The first part of the day, named.

Lesson 2 of 5.
मध्याह्नः (madhyāhnaḥ) — noon.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new word: what did prātaḥ mean?

You'll want to know: मध्याह्नः.
मध्याह्नः (madhyāhnaḥ) — "noon".
मध्याह्नः (madhyāhnaḥ) is midday, the sun at the top of its climb.
It is two pieces joined: madhya, "middle", and ahan, a day. Sanskrit keeps more than one word for a day, and this is the other one — दिनम् is the day you count, ahan the day you are standing in. English mid is madhya's own worn-down cousin.
So noon is the middle of the day said in one breath, which is what noon is in most languages once you take the word apart.

What you've built.
The top of the day, between its two ends.

Lesson 3 of 5.
सायम् (sāyam) — evening.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new word: what did madhyāhnaḥ mean?

You'll want to know: सायम्.
सायम् (sāyam) — "evening".
सायम् (sāyam) is evening: the end of the working day, when a thing is set down rather than picked up.
Put it beside प्रातः and you hold the two ends of the daylight — the greeting at one end and the leave-taking at the other. sāyaṁ prātaḥ, "evening and morning", is one of the oldest paired phrases the language has.
It rests on a piece meaning "to finish, to let go", so evening is named as the slackening of the day rather than as its darkness.

What you've built.
Both ends of the daylight, and the middle between them.

Lesson 4 of 5.
मासः (māsaḥ) — a month.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new word: what did sāyam mean?

You'll want to know: मासः.
मासः (māsaḥ) — "a month".
मासः (māsaḥ) is a month, and it is the moon's own word.
You have चन्द्रः for the moon overhead. मासः (māsaḥ) is that same moon counted: the stretch from one to the next.
Latin mēnsis, Greek mēn, English month and moon are all one word travelled west, and this is among the securest cousins in the whole family. Ends in -अः.

What you've built.
A stretch of time measured by the moon you already know.

Lesson 5 of 5.
संवत्सरः (saṁvatsaraḥ) — a year.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the last word of the chapter: what did sāyam mean, and what did māsaḥ mean?

You'll want to know: संवत्सरः.
संवत्सरः (saṁvatsaraḥ) — "a year".
संवत्सरः (saṁvatsaraḥ) is a year: the whole round of months, come back to where it started.
The sam- in front is the piece meaning "together, complete" that also opens the name of the language itself. The vatsara behind it is kin to Latin vetus, "old", and to Greek etos, a year — a year being what leaves a thing one year older. English veteran sits on that same piece.
Beside मासः (māsaḥ), the small round, संवत्सरः (saṁvatsaraḥ) is the large one. Ends in -अः.

What you've built.
Five stretches of time: morning, noon, evening, a month, a year.
