# ich finde, dass — an opinion, with its verb at the end

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GE-LEX-WEIL-01, GE-GRAMMAR-VERB-LETZT-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say "because German is beautiful". (*Weil Deutsch schön ist.*)
Where did *ist* go? (To the end.)

## Grammar Lens: dass does what weil does
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[GE-GRAMMAR-DASS-VERB-ENDE-01]; assesses=[GE-LEX-C64-VERBEN1-04, GE-LEX-C67-VERBEN4-02, GE-LEX-DEUTSCH-01, GE-LEX-SCHOEN-01] -->

**dass** means "that", the "that" of "I think that …". It opens a clause,
and like *weil* it sends the verb to the end:

> *Ich finde, dass Deutsch schön* **ist**. — I think German is beautiful.
> *Ich glaube, dass der Zug Verspätung* **hat**. — I believe the train is late.

German sets the *dass* clause off with a comma. German can drop
*dass*, as English drops "that", but then the verb returns to second
place: *Ich glaube, der Zug* **hat** *Verspätung.* Both are standard.

To disagree, put *nicht* on the opinion verb: *Ich finde nicht, dass das
richtig ist.*

## Why it's said this way: three verbs of opinion
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[GE-LEX-MEINEN-02]; assesses=[GE-LEX-C165-THING165-05, GE-LEX-C93-ADJ5-01, GE-LEX-C192-THING192-04] -->

**ich finde** is a judgement of your own, often about something you have
tried.

**ich glaube** is a belief you hold without being sure.

**ich meine** is "I think, I mean". The verb is **meinen**, and you know its
noun already: *die Meinung*, the opinion. *Meinen* also means "to mean":
*Was meinst du?*, what do you mean?

One trap in the spelling: **dass** with two *s* joins clauses; **das** with
one is "the" or "that one". Before 1996 the joiner was written *daß*.
Swiss spelling does without *ß* altogether, so Swiss writers used *dass*
long before then.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GE-LEX-MEINEN-02] -->

**dass** and **das** go back to one Old High German word, *daz*, from
Proto-Germanic *\*þat* (EtymDB 2.1), which EtymDB sets beside English
*that*. English kept one spelling for both jobs; German later split it in
two, by the usual account in the sixteenth century.

**meinen** ← Old High German *meinen* ← Proto-Germanic *\*mainijaną*, "to
mean, to think" (EtymDB 2.1): the same verb as English *mean*.
