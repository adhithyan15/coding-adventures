Latin, chapter 286: By Sea.
4 lessons. All 4 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 4.
nāvis, nāvis — a ship.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say "I am making a journey". (Iter faciō.) Then "to sail", and "a sailor". (Nāvigāre. Nauta.)
[pause 1 second]
A long Roman journey often went by sea. The word for what the sailor sails has been hiding inside nāvigāre all along.

You'll want to know: nāvis, nāvis.
nāvis, nāvis — "a ship". It is feminine, and its accusative is nāvem.
In nāvem ascendō. — I go on board the ship. Nāvis solvit. — The ship sets sail.
[pause 2 seconds]
You own ascendō, "to climb, to go up", and Romans went up into a ship. And you own solvō, "to untie": a ship that solvit has cast off its ropes from the shore.

The word, taken apart: nāvis.
nāvis is inherited, usually traced to Proto-Indo-European néh₂us, "boat" (EtymDB 2.1). Greek naus, "ship", is a cousin (Lewis & Short set them side by side). From the Latin come English naval, navy and the nave of a church, and nāvigō is nāvis with agō, "to drive" (Lewis & Short): to drive a ship.

Lesson 2 of 4.
naulum, naulī — the fare.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say "I go on board the ship". (In nāvem ascendō.)
[pause 1 second]
Romans had no tickets. A passenger found a ship going his way and paid its master a fare.

You'll want to know: naulum, naulī.
naulum, naulī — "a fare, passage money". It is neuter, like pretium, "a price", which is the word for what it costs.
Naulum solvō. — I pay the fare. Decem dēnāriōs solvī. — I paid ten denarii.
[pause 2 seconds]
Solvō does both jobs of this chapter: it unties a ship from the shore, and it pays what you owe.
[pause 1 second]
The man you pay is the magister nāvis, "the master of the ship": the same magister as a teacher, the one in charge.

The word, taken apart: naulum.
naulum was borrowed from Greek naulon, "passage money" (Lewis & Short), a word usually connected with naus, "ship", the Greek cousin of nāvis. The satirist Juvenal uses it, and so do the Roman law books, for the money owed to a ship; the law books also name the magister nāvis, the ship's master (Lewis & Short).

Lesson 3 of 4.
āmittō — something went wrong.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Ask "what happened?" (Quid accidit?) Then say "I paid the fare". (Naulum solvī.)
[pause 1 second]
On a journey, the answer to quid accidit is often bad news.

You'll want to know: āmittō, āmittere.
āmittō, āmittere — "to lose, to let slip". Its perfect is āmīsī, "I have lost".
Sarcinam āmīsī! — I have lost my pack! Occāsiōnem āmīsī. — I let the chance slip.
[pause 2 seconds]
You own perdō, "to lose", too. The two overlap, but they lean different ways. Perdō leans to losing by wasting or wrecking: money thrown away, a thing ruined. Āmittō leans to losing by letting go: it slipped away from you.

Grammar Lens: reporting the problem.
A problem on the road is an event, so it comes in the perfect:
— Quid accidit? — What happened? — Nāvis iam discessit! — The ship has already left!
[pause 2 seconds]
Discessit is the perfect of discēdō, "to go away", and iam, "already", makes it the missed boat. Answer quid accidit with what happened, then say what it means for you: Sarcinam āmīsī; nummōs nōn habeō. I have lost my pack; I have no money.

The word, taken apart: āmittō.
āmittō is built from ab, "away", and mittō, "to send" (EtymDB 2.1; Lewis & Short): to send away from yourself. In early Latin it meant "to let someone go", and from there "to let a thing slip", and so "to lose". Its perfect āmīsī keeps the perfect of mittō, mīsī.

Lesson 4 of 4.
Review — a journey by sea.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say "a ship", then "the fare". (Nāvis. Naulum.)

What you've built.
Two chapters, and a whole journey. The way through the town, quā viā …, rēctā viā, ad dextram. The ship, the fare and the master who takes it. And the trouble: quid accidit?, answered in the perfect.
Iter Rōmam faciēbam. Prīmum naulum solvī. Deinde sarcinam āmīsī. Postrēmō nāvis discessit! — I was making a journey to Rome. First I paid the fare. Then I lost my pack. And last of all, the ship left!
[pause 2 seconds]
One line of scene, faciēbam, and three events.
