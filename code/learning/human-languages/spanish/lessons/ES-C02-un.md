# un — "a," and the number hiding in it

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-GRAMMAR-DEFINITE-ARTICLES, ES-LEX-DIA] -->

[PAUSE 2s] How do you say "the day"? (*El día*.) Which word is doing the work
of *the*? (*El*.)

## Grammar Lens: the other article
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-GRAMMAR-INDEFINITE-ARTICLE-MASCULINE]; assesses=[ES-GRAMMAR-DEFINITE-ARTICLES, ES-GRAMMAR-NOUN-GENDER, ES-LEX-DIA] -->

*El día* is **the** day — a particular one, the one we both know about.

For **a** day, any day, not one we have settled on, Spanish swaps the article:

> **un día** — *a day.*

That is the second article, and Spanish has only these two kinds. *El* points at
something known; **un** does not.

Like *el*, it carries the noun's gender — so *un* is the masculine one, and its
partner is coming in the next lesson.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-ETYMON-UNUS]; assesses=[ES-GRAMMAR-INDEFINITE-ARTICLE-MASCULINE] -->

Here is the part worth keeping.

**Un is the number one.** Latin *ūnus*, "one", and Spanish never really separated
the two — *un día* is *a day* and *one day* at once, and context decides which
you meant.

**English did exactly the same thing and hid it better.** Our *a* and *an* are
the word **one**, worn down over centuries of being unstressed. That is why *an
hour* and *one hour* start with the same sound, and why *a* feels like almost
nothing when you say it: it has been eroded from a whole number down to a single
vowel.

So the two languages made the same move. Spanish just left the seam visible.
