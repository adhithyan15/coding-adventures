\chapter*{Portuguese pronunciation --- a reference}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Portuguese pronunciation (reference)}
\markboth{Pronunciation}{Pronunciation}

Not to be read before the lessons --- each lesson gives the sounds its word
needs. This page gathers them. Portuguese spelling is fairly regular, but its
\textbf{nasal vowels} and soft consonants surprise English speakers.

\section*{The traps for an English speaker}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Nasal vowels.} A vowel before \emph{m}/\emph{n} (or under a
    tilde) is sent through the nose: \emph{bom} $=$ \emph{bõ}, \emph{sim}
    (``yes'') $=$ \emph{sĩ}, \emph{p\~{a}o} (``bread'') $=$ \emph{pãw}. English
    has no exact match --- hum the vowel.
  \item \textbf{Vowels reduce.} Unstressed \emph{e} often vanishes to a whisper
    or \emph{i}; final \emph{o} sounds like \emph{u} (\emph{obrigado} $\approx$
    ``oh-bree-GAH-doo'').
  \item \textbf{s} between vowels $=$ \emph{z}; at the end of a syllable it is
    often \emph{sh} (European) or \emph{s} (most of Brazil).
  \item \textbf{lh} $=$ the \emph{lli} of \emph{million}; \textbf{nh} $=$ the
    \emph{ny} of \emph{canyon}; \textbf{\c{c}} $=$ \emph{s}.
  \item \textbf{r} at the start of a word, or \textbf{rr}, is a strong guttural
    (like a Scottish/French \emph{r} or even an \emph{h}); a single \emph{r}
    between vowels is a light tap.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Stress}

Usually the second-to-last syllable; a written accent (\emph{\'{a}}, \emph{\^{a}},
\emph{\~{a}}) marks any exception (\emph{ol\'{a}}, \emph{caf\'{e}}, \emph{av\^{o}}).

\section*{Portuguese in the Romance family}

Portuguese, Spanish, French, and Italian are all worn-down Latin. Portuguese
often eroded the most (the article \emph{ille} $\to$ \emph{o}); it turned Latin
\emph{-ct-} into \emph{-it-} (\emph{noite}, like French \emph{nuit}); and it kept
\emph{dies} close (\emph{o dia}, like Spanish \emph{d\'{i}a}). The lessons draw
these contrasts as they arise.
