Tamil, chapter 12: Family.
2 lessons. You can do the first 1 of them in the car; after that you will want to have stopped.


Lesson 1 of 2.
அப்பா, அம்மா, and four sibling words — father, mother, and FOUR sibling words — Tamil splits "brother"/"sister" by age, where Spanish/French use just one word each.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
You've learned Spanish hermano and hermana, plus French frère and sœur — one word each for "brother" and "sister." Tamil asks a question those languages never do: is this sibling older or younger than you?

The word, taken apart - Parents — simple and native.
அப்பா (appā) = "father".
அம்மா (ammā) = "mother".
Both are native Dravidian words, of the same simple, worldwide "baby-talk" shape (a repeated consonant plus a vowel) found across unrelated language families everywhere — not borrowed from Sanskrit, not a coincidence with any one language in particular, just how small children's mouths tend to form their first words for parents.

You'll want to know: Siblings — split by age, not just gender.
Here's the real lesson: Tamil doesn't have one word for "brother" and one for "sister." It has four:
[a table of 4 rows, read aloud]
அண்ணன் (aṇṇaṉ) means older brother.
தம்பி (tambi) means younger brother.
அக்கா (akkā) means older sister.
தங்கை (tangai) means younger sister.
There is no single Tamil word that just means "brother" the way Spanish hermano does — you're expected to know, and say, whether they're older or younger than you every time you refer to a sibling. This isn't a small detail: it reflects how central relative age and seniority are in South Asian family structure and address — the same instinct that makes "older sibling" versus "younger sibling" a live, spoken distinction, not just something you could mention if you wanted to.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: "appā, ammā" — father, mother]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: the four siblings — "aṇṇaṉ, tambi" (older/younger brother), "akkā, tangai" (older/younger sister)]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: the key question — "older, or younger?" — before you can even say "brother" or "sister" in Tamil]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
How many Tamil words mean "brother" or "sister," and why? (Four — அண்ணன்/தம்பி for older/younger brother, அக்கா/தங்கை for older/younger sister — Tamil requires knowing the sibling's age relative to you.) Are அப்பா and அம்மா borrowed from Sanskrit? (No — native Dravidian baby-talk-type words, of a shape found across unrelated languages worldwide.)

Warm-up.

Before the new one: ழ — what does it do?
[pause 2 seconds]
One character this time. Just one — and you have been saying it for pages without knowing which mark on the page it was.

Script you'll notice: ச.
ச — ca.
It is a consonant, and in this script a consonant is never bare: it comes with an a already in it. So it is not c, it is ca.
You already say these, and every one of them has ச (ca) somewhere inside it:
சரி sari — okay / alright / correct (sari).
ஆம் / இல்லை / சரி ām / illai / sari — answering in Tamil.
மீண்டும் சந்திப்போம் mīṇḍum sandippōm — we'll meet again.
பேசு pēsu — to speak.

Writing: ச — copy what you see.
Put your pen on ச and follow its line. Copy the shape you can see — slowly, and larger than it is printed.
This book does not yet tell you where to start the character or which way to travel. That is a real thing, taught with real variation from school to school, and it is not written down here until it can be written down with a source. Copying what is in front of you needs no such source, and it is how the shape gets into your hand in the meantime.
