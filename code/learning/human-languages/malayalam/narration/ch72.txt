Malayalam, chapter 72: The Thing Said.
3 lessons. All 3 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 3.
എന്ന് (ennŭ) — the word that makes a sentence quotable.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say enikku malayāḷaṁ aṟiyāṁ — "I know Malayalam." A whole sentence, and yours since the knowing chapter.
[pause 2 seconds]
Now say ñān paṟayunnu. Today one word joins those two.

You'll want to know.
എന്ന് — ennŭ — marks the sentence before it as the thing said.
"എനിക്ക് മലയാളം അറിയാം" എന്ന് ഞാൻ പറയുന്നു. "enikku malayāḷaṁ aṟiyāṁ" ennŭ ñān paṟayunnu — 'I say, "I know Malayalam."'
Nothing in the quoted sentence changed. It is the same five syllables you have been saying since chapter nine, sitting in front of ennŭ exactly as they stand on their own.
That is the whole of it, and it is worth more than the one word costs. English rebuilds a reported sentence — I know Malayalam becomes that I know Malayalam, and a different person or tense forces more changes still. Malayalam leaves the sentence alone and puts a marker after it.

Grammar Lens: where it sits.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
the sentence said, എനിക്ക് മലയാളം അറിയാം.
the marker, എന്ന് (ennŭ).
who says it, ഞാൻ പറയുന്നു.
Read the three rows in that order and you have the sentence. The quoted part comes first and the saying comes last, which is the opposite of English and the same order Malayalam uses everywhere else — the verb at the end.
You have seen this shape before without the marker: എന്നാൽ (ennāl), the word for but, begins with the same എന്ന. They are relatives, and meeting one helps you notice the other on a page.

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
What does എന്ന് (ennŭ) do? (Marks the sentence in front of it as the thing said.) What has to change inside that sentence? (Nothing.) Which comes first, the saying or the thing said? (The thing said — the verb goes last.)


Lesson 2 of 3.
എന്ന് ചിന്തിക്കുന്നു (ennŭ cintikkunnu) — the marker does not care which verb follows.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say your quoted sentence with ennŭ ñān paṟayunnu after it.
[pause 2 seconds]
Now swap the last verb for a different one. That is today's whole lesson.

Grammar Lens: one marker, four verbs.
എന്ന് (ennŭ) marks the thing said. What happens to it afterwards is up to the verb at the end, and you already have four of them:
[a table of 4 rows, read aloud]
the verb after it: പറയുന്നു paṟayunnu. what the sentence becomes: I say it.
the verb after it: ചിന്തിക്കുന്നു cintikkunnu. what the sentence becomes: I think it.
the verb after it: അറിയാം aṟiyāṁ. what the sentence becomes: I know it.
the verb after it: ചോദിക്കുന്നു cōdikkunnu. what the sentence becomes: I ask it.
"എനിക്ക് മലയാളം അറിയാം" എന്ന് ഞാൻ ചിന്തിക്കുന്നു. "enikku malayāḷaṁ aṟiyāṁ" ennŭ ñān cintikkunnu — 'I think I know Malayalam.'
This is why the marker is worth a chapter. It is one word, and it turns every sentence you can already build into something you can report, wonder about, claim to know, or put as a question — without learning a new shape for any of them.
The knowing verb keeps its own shape: അറിയാം puts what is known at the front and leaves the knower in the dative, so the sentence arrives at you rather than being done by you. The marker sits in front of it unchanged.

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Does എന്ന് (ennŭ) change when the verb after it changes? (No.) Name two verbs it can hand a sentence to. (Paṟayunnu, cintikkunnu — also aṟiyāṁ and cōdikkunnu.) And how much of the quoted sentence has to be rebuilt? (None of it.)


Lesson 3 of 3.
(the thing said) (paṟaññatŭ) — from cold.

Warm-up.
[pause 3 seconds]
Close the lessons before this one. Say I say in Malayalam, and say which three places in this book used that verb before anybody taught it.

Grammar Lens: three pieces, in this order.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
a sentence you can already say, എനിക്ക് മലയാളം അറിയാം.
the marker, എന്ന് (ennŭ).
what happens to it, ഞാൻ പറയുന്നു / ചിന്തിക്കുന്നു.
The middle row never changes and the top row never changes. Only the bottom one does, and it picks between verbs you have had since the thirties.
That is the payoff worth holding on to: every sentence in this book is now something you can report. The chapter added one marker and one verb, and what it bought was the whole of what you already knew, usable a second way.
