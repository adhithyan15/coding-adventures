Latin, chapter 497: Worry and distress.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
sollicitūdō, sollicitūdinis — anxiety, worry.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for dreadful, then the Latin for frightful.

You'll want to know: sollicitūdō, sollicitūdinis.
sollicitūdō, sollicitūdinis — "anxiety, worry".
Māter magnā sollicitūdine fīlium exspectat. The mother waits for her son with great anxiety.
Feminine, third declension, from sollicitus — anxious. English solicitude.

The word, taken apart.
sollicitūdō is built on sollicitus "anxious, troubled" with -tūdō (Lewis & Short; EtymDB). English solicitude comes from it.

Lesson 2 of 5.
angō, angere — to distress, torment; to choke.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for frightful, then the Latin for anxiety.

You'll want to know: angō, angere.
angō, angere — "to distress, torment; to choke".
Cūra dē fīliō patrem angit. Worry about his son torments the father.
angō, angere, anxī. First to squeeze the throat, then to torment the mind. Anxius, already known, comes from it.

The word, taken apart.
angō is inherited from Proto-Italic angō, from a Proto-Indo-European root meaning "narrow, tight" (EtymDB). English anxious and anguish come from its family.

Lesson 3 of 5.
aegritūdō, aegritūdinis — distress of mind, grief; sickness.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for anxiety, then the Latin for to distress.

You'll want to know: aegritūdō, aegritūdinis.
aegritūdō, aegritūdinis — "distress of mind, grief; sickness".
Post mortem amīcī aegritūdō eum diū tenet. After his friend's death, grief holds him for a long time.
Feminine, third declension, from aeger — sick. In philosophy it often means sorrow, a sickness of the mind.

The word, taken apart.
aegritūdō is built on aeger "sick" with -tūdō (Lewis & Short), and it names sickness of body or of mind.

Lesson 4 of 5.
molestia, molestiae — annoyance, trouble.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to distress, then the Latin for distress of mind.

You'll want to know: molestia, molestiae.
molestia, molestiae — "annoyance, trouble".
Muscae aegrō molestiam afferunt. The flies cause the sick man annoyance.
Feminine, first declension, from molestus — annoying. English molest comes from the related verb molestō.

The word, taken apart.
molestia is built on molestus "troublesome", which Lewis & Short connects with mōlēs "a heavy mass": a burden. English molest comes from the related verb molestō.

Lesson 5 of 5.
cruciō, cruciāre — to torment, torture.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for distress of mind, then the Latin for annoyance.

You'll want to know: cruciō, cruciāre.
cruciō, cruciāre — "to torment, torture".
Dolor dentium senem cruciat. Toothache torments the old man.
cruciō, cruciāre, cruciāvī, cruciātum, from crux — a cross. In comedy also of worry: mē cruciō — I fret. English excruciating.

The word, taken apart.
cruciō is built on crux "a cross" (Lewis & Short; EtymDB): to torment as on a cross. English excruciating comes from the compound excruciō.
