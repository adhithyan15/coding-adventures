# dich — "you," as the one it happens to

## You'll want to know: dich
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[GE-LEX-DICH-01]; assesses=[GE-LEX-MICH-01, GE-LEX-DU-SIE-02, GE-GRAMMAR-AKKUSATIV-NUR-MASKULIN-01] -->

> **dich** — "you", when you are the one being done to.

Said *dikh*, with the soft ich-sound of *ich*, *mich* and *nicht*.

> **Ich sehe dich.** — "I see you."

> **Ich höre dich.** — "I hear you."

It is *mich*'s partner exactly: **ich → mich**, **du → dich**. One vowel apart,
one job apart, and the pattern is visible without being taught.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GE-LEX-DICH-01, GE-ETYMON-DIR-THEE-02, GE-ETYMON-DU-03, GE-LEX-DEIN-01] -->

English once had this whole row and let it collapse. *Thou* was the doer,
*thee* was both the done-to and the to-whom, *thine* the possessive. German
kept four distinct shapes where English kept three and then lost all of them:

> **du** · **dich** · **dir** · **dein**

Every one begins with the *d* that was English *th*, so the row is predictable
from a rule you have had since the second chapter.
