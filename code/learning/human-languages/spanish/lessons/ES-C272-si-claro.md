# sí, claro — when the words say yes and the speaker means no

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Harder than an implicature: a sentence whose meaning is the OPPOSITE of its words.

## You'll want to know first
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[] -->

- [es que…](./ES-C272-es-que.md) — meaning more than you say.

## Sounds you'll need
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[] -->

- Everything here is in the delivery. Said briskly and warmly, *sí, claro* is
  plain agreement. Said slowly, flatly, with the *claro* dragged out, it is the
  opposite. → [reference](../pronunciation-reference.md)

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-SI-CLARO]; assesses=[] -->

**sí, claro** — "yes, of course" — or "yeah, right", depending entirely on how
it is said.

*Claro* is Latin **clarus**, "bright, clear" — the same word as English **clear**
and the name **Clara**. To say *claro* is to say *that is obvious*.

Which is exactly what makes it available for irony. A word meaning "obviously"
is the perfect thing to say about something you find not obvious at all, and the
gap between the word and the tone is where the sarcasm lives.

## What you've built
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-SEMANTIC-IRONY]; assesses=[] -->

Irony is not a Spanish trick; English does the same with *sure* and *of course*.
What is worth knowing is that **the marker is carried in the voice, not in the
words** — so a written *sí, claro* is genuinely ambiguous, and Spanish readers
resolve it from context exactly as you do in English.

If you are not certain which one you are hearing, you are not failing at Spanish.
The sentence is ambiguous by design.
