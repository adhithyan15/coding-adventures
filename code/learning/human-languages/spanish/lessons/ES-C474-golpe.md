## You'll want to know: el golpe
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-C474-PAGO-04]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C474-PAGO-03] -->

**el golpe** — the blow, the knock.

> No te lo cobran de **golpe**.

The literal sense is a hit: *un golpe en la puerta*, a knock at the door.

But the phrase is what you need: **de golpe** — all at once, suddenly.

## Grammar Lens: the phrase a payment plan is defined against
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C474-PAGO-04, ES-LEX-C474-PAGO-02] -->

Spanish has two ways to say *all at once*, and a notice about money will use
one to rule out the other.

| phrase | what it pictures |
|---|---|
| **de golpe** | in one blow — suddenly, often unwelcome |
| *de una vez* | in a single go — neutral |

*De golpe* carries the impact. *Se me olvidó de golpe* — it went out of my head
all at once. *Subió de golpe* — it shot up.

So *no de golpe* about a fee is doing more than saying *in instalments*: it is
saying you will not be hit with it. That is why the reassurance lands, and why
a question about it may use the calmer *de una vez* instead.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C474-PAGO-04] -->

Latin **colaphus**, a blow with the fist, borrowed from Greek **kólaphos**, a
box on the ear.

The road from *colaphus* to *golpe* is worth a look, because two things
happened: the **c** softened to **g**, and the middle syllable fell out
entirely — *co-la-phus* worn down to *gol-pe*. That is ordinary wear, and it
left a word that no longer looks Greek at all.

English has the same blow in **coup**, taken from French rather than Spanish
and kept intact: a *coup* is one sharp strike, which is what a *golpe* is.
Spanish says **golpe de estado** where English says *coup d'état* — the same
two words, twice.

French went further and made a quantity out of it. **Beaucoup** is *beau* plus
*coup*, a **beautiful blow**, and it means *a lot* — so the same punch that
gives Spanish its *golpe* gives French its word for plenty.
