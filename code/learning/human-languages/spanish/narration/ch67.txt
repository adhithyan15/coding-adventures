Spanish, chapter 67: Tener in the Plural, and the Stem-Change Rule.
2 lessons. All 2 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 2.
tenemos — and the stem change disappears.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say "I have" and "you have." (Tengo, tienes.) And "we eat." (Comemos.)

Grammar Lens: the break is gone.
tenemos — we have., tenéis — you all have.
Not tienemos. Look at what happened: tienes had the broken stem ie, and here it is plain e again — the ordinary -er endings, on the ordinary stem.
You already know why. It is the same reason -er and -ir looked identical in the singular and parted in the plural: stress.
Tienes stresses the stem, and a stressed e in that position breaks into ie. Tenemos stresses the ending, so the stem is unstressed and its vowel stays whole.
The stem change was never a property of the verb. It is a property of where the stress lands, and these two forms are where the stress lands elsewhere.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: "tienes" then "tenemos" — and hear the stress move off the stem]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: "comemos, tenemos" — the same endings]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[question — say your answer, then pause 8 seconds]
Say we have.
[question — say your answer, then pause 10 seconds]
Why does tienes break its stem but tenemos does not?

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
What is "we have"? (Tenemos.) Where did the ie go? (Nowhere — it only ever appears when the stem is stressed.) Next: the form where the stress comes back.


Lesson 2 of 2.
tienen — the break comes back.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say "we have", then "you have" to a friend. (Tenemos, tienes.) Which one has the broken stem?

Grammar Lens: the rule, stated once and for all.
tienen — they have, and ustedes tienen, you all have.
The ie is back. And now you have seen it appear, vanish and return, which is enough to state the rule outright:
A stem change happens exactly where the stem is stressed, and nowhere else.
That is four forms with the break — setting aside the yo form, which has an oddity of its own — and two without: nosotros and vosotros. Always those two. In every stem-changing verb in the language.
Learners are usually told to memorise a shape called "the boot" — the four stressed forms drawn as an outline around a table. You do not need the boot. You need to know where Spanish puts its stress, which you already do.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: "tienes, tenemos, tienen" — break, no break, break]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: the two forms that never break, for any verb]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[question — say your answer, then pause 8 seconds]
Say they have.
[question — say your answer, then pause 10 seconds]
In any stem-changing verb, which two forms never break?
[question — say your answer, then pause 8 seconds]
What actually decides whether the stem breaks?

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
What is "they have"? (Tienen.) State the stem-change rule. (The stem breaks exactly where it is stressed.) Which two forms never break? (Nosotros and vosotros.)
