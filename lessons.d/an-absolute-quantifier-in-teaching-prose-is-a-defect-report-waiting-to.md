# An absolute quantifier in teaching prose is a defect report waiting to be filed

Of roughly twenty authoring errors caught in review of Spanish chapters
460-464, a third were one word long. The word was *always*, *never*, *the
one*, *all of them*, *most*, or *without exception*:

- "A *z* before *e* becomes *c* in Spanish, **always**" — *zen*, *zeta*,
  *enzima*, *nazi*.
- "Spanish does not write **ze** … it applies **without exception**" — same
  counterexamples, asserted twice in one chapter.
- "*La guía* is **the one** that complicates it" — *la policía*, in the
  table row directly above, does the identical thing.
- "Four verbs, one shape, and you now have **all of them**" — true of the
  course, false of Spanish, and the learner will meet *me encanta* next
  week.
- "which is how **most** modern profession words were built" — `-dor` and
  `-ero` are at least as productive as `-ista`.
- "this is **the one** pattern where the article must be learned with the
  word" — every example given ends in `-a` or `-o` and follows the ordinary
  rule.

None of these is an etymology mistake or a research failure. In every case
the correct hedged sentence was already known and the absolute was reached
for because it reads more confidently. It reads more confidently precisely
because it claims more, and the extra claim is the false part.

The corpus already knows how to do this. `ES-C57-f-a-h` writes "So this is
a **lead**, not a guarantee." `ES-C394-trabajo` spends a lesson grading
attested forms against asterisked ones. Both were available as models.

Practical rule before pushing lesson prose: grep your own diff for
`always|never|every|all of them|the one|most|without exception|no
exception`, and for each hit either produce the counterexample and hedge,
or narrow the scope until the absolute is true — "verb endings never put an
*e* after a *z*" is both absolute and correct, where the unscoped version
is not.
