# putō — I think that …

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[LA-LEX-COGITO-01, LA-LEX-C171-ACT171-05] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say "I think", the verb of turning things over. (*Cōgitō.*) Now
"I believe". (*Crēdō.*)

[PAUSE 1s] The chapter on *cōgitō* named a lighter verb beside it, closer
to "I reckon". This lesson puts it to work.

## You'll want to know: putō
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[LA-LEX-C283-PUTO-01]; assesses=[LA-LEX-C68-AEGER-01] -->

**putō, putāre** — "to think, to reckon, to suppose". It is the everyday
verb of opinion: *cōgitō* is thinking hard; *putō* is what you think.

> **Ita putō.** — I think so; that is how I reckon it.

Its perfect is regular: **putāvī**, I thought.

## Grammar Lens: no word for "that"
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[LA-GRAMMAR-C283-ACC-INF-02]; assesses=[LA-LEX-C68-AEGER-02, LA-GRAMMAR-C59-OBJECT-M-02] -->

English says "I think **that** my friend is ill". Latin has no word there.
It puts the person in the **accusative** and the verb in the
**infinitive**:

> **Putō amīcum aegrum esse.** — I think my friend is ill.

[PAUSE 2s] Word for word, "I think friend-*um* to be ill". The *-um* of
*amīcum* marks who the thought is about, and *esse* carries the verb.
Even the adjective joins the accusative: *aegrum*, not *aeger*.

> **Crēdō tē fessum esse.** — I believe you are tired.
> **Putō pluere.** — I think it is raining.

[PAUSE 1s] *Pluere* has no subject at all, as *pluit* had none. This
pattern, accusative and infinitive, carries what someone thinks, believes
or says all through Latin.

## The word, taken apart: putō
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[LA-ETYMON-C283-PUTO-03]; assesses=[LA-ETYMON-COGITO-02, LA-LEX-C167-ACT167-04, LA-LEX-C166-ACT166-03] -->

The *cōgitō* lesson told the story: **putō** first meant "to prune", then
"to settle an account", then "to think" (Lewis & Short trace it to a root
meaning "to clean"). You own two of its compounds already: *computō*, "to
calculate", is *com-* + *putō*, reckoning together, and *disputō*, "to
discuss", began in the merchants' sense of going over a sum item by item.

The Roman writer Aulus Gellius made a point of it. He read the word for "I
think" as a pruner's word: cutting away the false opinions until what is
left is true (Lewis & Short quote the passage under *puto*).
