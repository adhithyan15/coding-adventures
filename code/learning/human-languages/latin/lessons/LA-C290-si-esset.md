# sī hīc essēs — if it were so

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[LA-GRAMMAR-C289-SI-REAL-01, LA-GRAMMAR-C290-SUBIUNCTIVUS-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say "if it rains, we stay in the farmhouse". (*Sī pluit, in vīllā
manēmus.*) Then "I would like to see you". (*Velim tē vidēre.*)

[PAUSE 1s] The first may well come true. Now a condition you know is false,
about right now.

## Grammar Lens: the infinitive plus an ending
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[LA-GRAMMAR-C290-IRREALIS-01]; assesses=[LA-LEX-SUM-01, LA-LEX-HABEO-01, LA-GRAMMAR-C287-AFFECTUS-01] -->

> **Sī hīc essēs, laetus essem.** — If you were here, I would be happy.

[PAUSE 2s] You are not here, and both halves say so by their mood. **essem**
and **essēs** are the **imperfect subjunctive** of *sum*, and they are made
the easiest way Latin has: take the infinitive, *esse*, and add the
endings: **essem**, **essēs**, **esset**, **essēmus**, **essētis**,
**essent**.

[PAUSE 2s] The other verbs follow the same recipe: *habēre*, **habērem**, I would have; *posse*,
**possem**, I could; *velle*, **vellem**, I would want.

> **Sī pecūniam habērem, nāvem emerem.** — If I had money, I would buy a
> ship.

[PAUSE 1s] *Emerem* is *emere* with *-m*. In English the "if" half sounds
past, "if I had"; Latin agrees, and calls this the imperfect.

## Why it's said this way: real or not
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[LA-GRAMMAR-C289-SI-REAL-01, LA-LEX-C289-SI-01, LA-LEX-C100-VERBA6-02] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Set the two side by side:

> **Sī pluit, in vīllā manēmus.** — If it rains, we stay in. (It may.)
> **Sī nōn plueret, nāvigārēmus.** — If it were not raining, we would be
> sailing. (It is.)

[PAUSE 2s] Plain verbs leave the question open. The subjunctive tells the
listener the "if" is false right now, and the speaker knows it.
