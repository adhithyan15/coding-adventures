Mandarin Chinese, chapter 439: Country Life.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
dàotián — rice paddy; paddy field.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Mandarin for a part of a whole, then the Mandarin for all of it.

You'll want to know: dàotián.
dàotián — "rice paddy; paddy field".
wǒ yéye nǎinai zhù zài dàotián pángbiān — My grandparents live next to the rice paddies.
Its characters come later; for now it is a word for the ear.

The word, taken apart.
dàotián is built from dào 'rice in the field' and tián 'a field', both inherited from Middle Chinese, dawX and den (Guangyun, in Baxter's transcription), which glosses the first as rice and the second, after an older book, as land that has been ploughed.

Lesson 3 of 5.
nóngjiālè — farm stay; country guesthouse.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Mandarin for a rice paddy, then the Mandarin for an orchard.

You'll want to know: nóngjiālè.
nóngjiālè — "farm stay; country guesthouse".
wǒmen zhù zài yì jiā nóngjiālè — We're staying at a farm guesthouse.
Its characters come later; for now it is a word for the ear.

The word, taken apart.
nóngjiālè is built from nóng 'farming', 家 'a family' and 乐 'joy', all inherited from Middle Chinese, nowng, kae and lak (Guangyun, in Baxter's transcription), which glosses the first as working the fields. The name for a farm that takes guests is modern.

Its characters come later; for now it is a word for the ear.

The word, taken apart.
yāzi is built from yā 'a duck' and the noun ending zi. The first is inherited from Middle Chinese 'aep (Guangyun, in Baxter's transcription), glossed as a water bird.

Lesson 5 of 5.
sànxīn — to take one's mind off things; to unwind.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Mandarin for a farm stay, then the Mandarin for a duck.

You'll want to know: sànxīn.
sànxīn — "to take one's mind off things; to unwind".
lèi de shíhou wǒ xǐhuan qù nóngcūn sànxīn — When I'm tired, I like to go to the countryside to unwind.
Its characters come later; for now it is a word for the ear.

The word, taken apart.
sànxīn is built from sàn 'to scatter' and 心 'the heart', both inherited from Middle Chinese, sanH and sim (Guangyun, in Baxter's transcription), which glosses the first 'to break apart' and 'to spread': cares scattered from the heart.
