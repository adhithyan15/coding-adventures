Latin, chapter 311: Damnātus aut absolūtus.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
damnō, damnāre — to condemn, to find guilty.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to prove, then the Latin for information.

You'll want to know: damnō, damnāre.
damnō, damnāre — "to condemn, to find guilty".
Iūdicēs eum fūrtī damnāvērunt. The judges found him guilty of theft.
damnō, damnāre, damnāvī, damnātum, with the genitive of the charge. From damnum, 'loss' (taught). English damn.

The word, taken apart.
damnō is built on damnum, "loss, damage" (EtymDB): to inflict a loss, a penalty. English damn and condemn come from its family.

Lesson 2 of 5.
absolvō, absolvere — to acquit; to set free; to finish.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for information, then the Latin for to condemn.

You'll want to know: absolvō, absolvere.
absolvō, absolvere — "to acquit; to set free; to finish".
Reus absolūtus est et laetus domum rediit. The defendant was acquitted and went home happy.
absolvō, absolvere, absolvī, absolūtum: ab + solvō (taught), to loosen from. English absolve.

The word, taken apart.
absolvō joins ab, "from", and solvō, "to loosen" (EtymDB): to set loose from a charge. English absolve comes from it.

Lesson 3 of 5.
nocēns, nocentis — guilty; harmful.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to condemn, then the Latin for to acquit.

You'll want to know: nocēns, nocentis.
nocēns, nocentis — "guilty; harmful".
Nocēns poenam dare dēbet. The guilty man must pay the penalty.
One ending, genitive nocentis: the present participle of a verb meaning 'to harm'.

The word, taken apart.
nocēns is the present participle of noceō, "to harm" (Lewis & Short): doing harm, and so guilty.

Lesson 5 of 5.
culpa, culpae — fault, blame; guilt.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for guilty, then the Latin for innocent.

You'll want to know: culpa, culpae.
culpa, culpae — "fault, blame; guilt".
Nōn mea culpa erat, sed tua. It was not my fault, but yours.
Feminine. In culpā esse: to be to blame. English culpable comes from it.

The word, taken apart.
culpa is inherited from Proto-Italic kʷolpā, "a wrong, a mistake" (EtymDB). English culpable and culprit come from its family.
