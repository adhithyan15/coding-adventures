Lesson 1 of 5.
きそく (kisoku) — a rule; a regulation.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Japanese for after all, then the Japanese for freedom.

You'll want to know: きそく.
きそく — kisoku — "a rule; a regulation".
あぶないから、この きそくが あります。 — abunai kara, kono kisoku ga arimasu — "This rule is there because it is dangerous."

The word, taken apart.
きそく (kisoku) is Sino-Japanese (kango): two Chinese characters read with their on'yomi, readings borrowed from Chinese. They are ki, "standard" (Middle Chinese kjwie) and soku, "rule" (tsok) (KANJIDIC2; Middle Chinese in Baxter's transcription).

Lesson 2 of 5.
やぶる (yaburu) — to break a rule; to break (a promise); to tear.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Japanese for freedom, then the Japanese for a regulation.

You'll want to know: やぶる.
やぶる — yaburu — "to break a rule; to break (a promise); to tear".
やくそくを やぶったから、ともだちが おこりました。 — yakusoku o yabutta kara, tomodachi ga okorimashita — "I broke my promise, so my friend got angry."
Polite やぶります, yaburimasu; a godan verb. Past (polite): かれが やくそくを やぶりました。, kare ga yakusoku o yaburimashita.

The word, taken apart.
やぶる (yaburu) is a native Japanese word (wago), inherited rather than borrowed from Chinese, and its older origin is not settled. Its kanji was attached to it later as a kun'yomi, a native reading given to a Chinese character (JMdict, KANJIDIC2).
