# als and wenn — once, or every time

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GE-GRAMMAR-WAEHREND-HINTERGRUND-02, GE-LEX-WANN-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say "while I was reading". (*Während ich las.*) Then ask "when
are you coming?" (*Wann kommst du?*)

[PAUSE 1s] *Wann* asks. Inside a story, German has two other words for
"when", and it chooses by counting.

## Grammar Lens: one occasion, or every occasion
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[GE-GRAMMAR-ALS-EINMAL-01, GE-GRAMMAR-WENN-IMMER-02]; assesses=[GE-GRAMMAR-PRAETERITUM-STARK-01, GE-LEX-C62-FAMILIE-01, GE-LEX-C139-VERB139-02] -->

For **one** occasion in the past, German says **als**:

> *Als ich in Berlin war, sah ich meine Großmutter.* — When I was in Berlin
> (that one time), I saw my grandmother.

For something that happened **every time**, it says **wenn**, "whenever":

> *Wenn ich in Berlin war, besuchte ich meine Großmutter.* — Whenever I
> was in Berlin, I visited my grandmother.

English says "when" for both. Both German words work like *weil*: the
verb goes to the **end** of its clause, *als ich in Berlin* **war**.

## Why it's said this way: verb, comma, verb
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GE-LEX-WEIL-01, GE-GRAMMAR-VERB-LETZT-01, GE-GRAMMAR-SEIN-ICH-WAR-01] -->

In the first sentence two verbs meet at the comma: *… in Berlin* **war**,
**sah** *ich*. The whole *als* clause is the first piece of the sentence,
and the main verb comes second, so it follows the comma at once, with the
subject behind it.

Turn the sentence round and the meeting disappears: *Ich sah meine
Großmutter, als ich in Berlin war.*

With the present, *wenn* means "when" or "whenever": *Wenn ich in
Berlin bin, besuche ich sie.* *Als* belongs to one occasion that is over.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GE-ETYMON-WANN-WHEN-02] -->

**wenn** and **wann** were one word in Old High German, *wanne* or *wenne*;
EtymDB 2.1 traces *wann* to Proto-Germanic *\*hwannē*, the parent of
English *when*. It lists no parent for *wenn*, so the split of one old word
into a question word and a joining word is given as the usual account.

**als** ← Old High German *alsō* (EtymDB 2.1), worn down to one syllable.
EtymDB gives no meaning for it; by the usual account it was *al* + *sō*,
"all so, just so".

(***Wenn.***) A question about time? (***Wann.***) Where does the verb go

when the *als* clause goes first? (**The main verb.**)
