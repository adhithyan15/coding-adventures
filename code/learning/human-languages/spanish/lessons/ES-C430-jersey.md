# el jersey — an island, worn

## You'll want to know: el jersey
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-C430-VIDA-03]; assesses=[] -->

**el jersey** — the jumper, the pullover, the sweater.

Said the Spanish way: *jer-SÉI*, with the **j** as the throaty *jota* you already
have from *jamón*.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C430-VIDA-03] -->

**Jersey** is an island in the Channel, between England and France. Its knitters
were famous, and the heavy woollen garment they made took the island's name in
English. Spanish borrowed the English word entire, island and all.

That makes it one of a small set of Spanish clothing words that name a **place**
rather than a shape or a material:

| garment | the place |
|---|---|
| el jersey | a Channel island |
| los tejanos | Texas |
| la chaqueta | a French peasant named Jacques |

Latin America mostly says **el suéter** instead — also English, borrowed a
different way, and spelled to fit Spanish.

## Grammar Lens: what a borrowed word has to learn
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C430-VIDA-03, ES-LEX-C430-VIDA-02] -->

A word taken from another language still has to obey the accent rules of this
one. *Jersey* is stressed on its last syllable, and a word ending in **-y**
takes no written accent, so the singular is bare.

The plural is where it shows:

| number | form |
|---|---|
| singular | el jersey |
| plural | los jerséis |

The **y** turns into an **i**, the ending becomes **-s**, and now an accent is
needed to hold the stress where it was. Spanish did not leave the word foreign;
it made it follow the rules.
