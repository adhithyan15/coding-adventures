Lesson 1 of 9.
पानीयम् (pānīyam) — "water," the thing to be drunk.

Before we start: you can do this one in the car, but part of it needs your eyes. For that part you will want your eyes, for letter shapes on the page. You can listen to everything else now. I will say so again when we reach it, but leave this section until you have stopped: The letters in this word.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say "Sanskrit is dear to me." (Saṁskṛtaṁ mama priyam.) Every word so far has named an action. Now for the nouns you would actually reach for — starting with the one you would ask for first.

You'll want to know: पानीयम्.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
Sanskrit: पानीयम्. sound: pānīyam. meaning: water.
Sanskrit: —. sound: pibati. meaning: he, she, or it drinks.
पानीयम् (pānīyam) is neuter, and its final -म् marks that — a whole family of everyday nouns ends this way, and it will keep coming back this chapter.

The letters in this word.
[once you have stopped driving — this part needs your eyes or your hands; if you are driving, skip ahead to the next part]
पा is pā, the long-ā you have said since नाम. नी stacks न na under the long-ī sign ी. यम् closes it: y, the inherent a, then म् with its vowel stripped by the halant — pā-nī-ya-m.

The word, taken apart — the thing to be drunk.
पानीयम् (pānīyam) is built on the root पा (pā), "to drink," plus -अनीय (-anīya), a suffix meaning "fit to be ed." Literally: the thing that is to be drunk.
The root is PIE peh3-, "to drink." Latin took it into potare, and from that English has potion and poison — a drink, and a drink gone wrong.
Eastward, पानीयम् (pānīyam) wore down through Prakrit pāṇīya into the everyday water-word of the daughter languages: Hindi पानी (pānī), Gujarati pāṇī, and, in the Bangladesh register of Bengali, pani — every one तद्भव, worn down by ordinary speech, not carried over whole. Sanskrit's own oldest water-words, jala and udaka, took a different road entirely; this is not that word.

Script you'll notice: स.
[once you have stopped driving — this part needs your eyes or your hands; if you are driving, skip ahead to the next part]
स — sa.
It is a consonant, and in this script a consonant is never bare: it comes with an a already in it. So it is not s, it is sa.
What it is made of:
a descending left stem curling through the central hook and diagonal tail.
a left-to-right middle crossbar.
a top-to-bottom right stem.
the top shirorekhā.
You already say these, and every one of them has स somewhere inside it:
नमस्ते namaste — hello (lit. "a bow to you").
नमस्कारः namaskāraḥ — hello (lit. "the making of a bow").
अस्ति asti — is.
मम नाम … अस्ति mama nāma … asti — my name is…

Writing: स.
1. start just below the headline, descend the left stem, curve left around the central hook, and continue diagonally down-right through the tail without lifting.
2. lift at the central junction and draw the middle crossbar left-to-right.
3. without lifting, climb up the right stem to the headline.
4. descend the right stem top-to-bottom.
5. lift and draw the top shirorekhā left-to-right.
Pen lifts: 2. The pen comes up 2 times and no more.
The source shows four movements; most native writers draw स in three strokes (71% of HP Labs India's native-writer samples). This path keeps every movement in order and direction, and lifts only before the crossbar and the headline.
This is one attested teaching order and not a national standard — handwriting here is taught with school-to-school variation. Source: JackPotte, ‘Devanagari s स (sa).gif’, strokes 1–4, Wikimedia Commons, 29 March 2009.

Lesson 3 of 9.
क्षीरम् (kṣīram) — "milk," and the dessert it left behind.

Before we start: you can do this one in the car, but part of it needs your eyes. For that part you will want your eyes, for letter shapes on the page. You can listen to everything else now. I will say so again when we reach it, but leave this section until you have stopped: The letters in this word.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say "water," and the root it is built on. (Pānīyam; पा, "to drink.") Not every drink-word in Sanskrit has so tidy an answer.

You'll want to know: क्षीरम्.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
Sanskrit: क्षीरम्. sound: kṣīram. meaning: milk.
Sanskrit: क्षीर. sound: kṣīra. meaning: milk — the bare stem, still living inside Hindi khīr.
Neuter again, marked by the same -म् as पानीयम् (pānīyam).

The letters in this word.
[once you have stopped driving — this part needs your eyes or your hands; if you are driving, skip ahead to the next part]
क्ष is a new stacked pair: क k fused under ष, the hissed retroflex s — together they say kṣa, one push, not two. Then the long-ī sign ी, र ra, and the neuter म् you already know: kṣī-ra-m.

The word, taken apart — a disputed root, and a live dessert.
Where पानीयम् (pānīyam)'s history is settled, क्षीरम् (kṣīram)'s is not. One account traces it to an old Proto-Indo-European word for milk; another argues it was borrowed from a substrate language spoken where Indo-Iranian speakers first arrived in the region. Neither side has won.
What is clear is the family it sits in: Avestan xšuuidi- and Persian šir say roughly the same word, but as relatives, not children — all three come down from one shared Proto-Indo-Iranian ancestor, the way gṛhṇāti and English grab are cousins rather than the same word wearing different clothes.
The word's liveliest descendant is not a drink. Hindi खीर (khīr) is a sweet rice pudding cooked in milk — worn down, तद्भव — and English borrowed that dessert whole, as kheer.

Lesson 4 of 9.
अन्नम् (annam) — "food," the eaten thing.

Before we start: you can do this one in the car, but part of it needs your eyes. For that part you will want your eyes, for letter shapes on the page. You can listen to everything else now. I will say so again when we reach it, but leave this section until you have stopped: The letters in this word.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say "milk" and "water." (Kṣīram, pānīyam.) One more thing you would ask for, and this one carries a root you already own.

You'll want to know: अन्नम्.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
Sanskrit: अन्नम्. sound: annam. meaning: food, grain.
Sanskrit: खाद्यम् अन्नम्. sound: khādyam annam. meaning: edible food — खादति's own adjective on it.
Neuter, and -म् again — the third noun running.

The letters in this word.
[once you have stopped driving — this part needs your eyes or your hands; if you are driving, skip ahead to the next part]
अ opens bare, no consonant to carry it. न्न doubles न na onto itself, held a beat longer than one n would be. म् closes it, the same stripped-vowel neuter ending: an-na-m.

The word, taken apart — the eaten thing.
Chapter 7 taught खादति, the everyday word for eating, and set beside it its untaken cousin: अद्, PIE ed-, the very root of English eat, which Sanskrit keeps alive as अत्ति.
अन्नम् (annam) is built on that same अद्, plus the old participle suffix -न — "eaten." The d and the n fuse into the doubled न्न you just sounded out. Food, named this way, is not a thing but an event: the thing that has been eaten.

Grammar Lens: three genders, one small system.
Every noun in this chapter has ended in -म्, and that is not coincidence: Sanskrit sorts every noun into one of three genders — masculine, feminine, and neuter — the same system that put a gendered form on the numerals back in Chapter 6, where the daughter languages inherited specifically the neuter forms, trīṇi and catvāri. This book teaches only the plain nominative singular of each noun, never the full run of cases and numbers behind it — but the gender is worth carrying, because it is not optional, and it is not guessable from meaning alone.

Script you'll notice: ◌ी.
[once you have stopped driving — this part needs your eyes or your hands; if you are driving, skip ahead to the next part]
◌ी — ī.
It is a vowel sign. It is not a letter and never stands alone: it attaches to a consonant and replaces the a built into it. Replaces, not adds — the a is gone.
Where it sits: a curl to the right with a tail over the top bar.
Worked through: न + ◌ी = नी — nī.
You already say these, and every one of them has ◌ी (ī) somewhere inside it:
पानीयम् pānīyam — water — "the thing to be drunk," and the exact word your neighbors' पानी grew from.

Writing: ◌ी — follow the numbered strip.
Follow the numbered strip: start where movement 1 begins, and let each panel show you the next movement before your pen makes it. Then write ◌ी (ī) yourself — slowly, and larger than it is printed. The short bar on top stays grey in the strip: in a word it is part of the headline, which you draw last, once, across the whole word.
The strip shows where to start the character and which way to travel, in one attested order. That is taught with real variation from school to school, so the strip names its source beneath it: treat it as a sound way in, not the only one.

Warm-up.
[once you have stopped driving — this part needs your eyes or your hands; if you are driving, skip ahead to the next part]
Read अस्ति and say its meaning: asti, “is.” Point to स, the virama ◌्, and त. Every sign and the word itself are already known; this lesson only makes their join visible.

Writing — observe and trace the conjunct.
Watch the pieces close up: स (sa) + ◌् + त becomes स्त (sta). The virama removes the a from स (sa), so the consonants touch: s followed by ta.
Keep स्त (sta) visible. Trace the printed join once with a fingertip, then once with a pencil on tracing paper. Preserve the headline and the smaller first consonant; do not add an a between the two sounds.

Wrap-up recall.
What does the join inside अस्ति (asti) say? (s + ta, with no vowel after the first consonant.)
