Latin, chapter 287: How It Felt.
3 lessons. All 3 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 3.
laetus eram — how it felt.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say "happy", then "sad". (Laetus. Trīstis.) Then "I was", the open past. (Eram.)

Grammar Lens: the feeling agrees with who felt it.
A feeling in a story is a state, so it usually takes the imperfect, and the adjective matches the person who felt it:
Laetus eram. — I was happy (a man speaking). Laeta eram. — I was happy (a woman speaking). Sollicitī erāmus. — We were worried. Trīstis erat. — He was sad, or she was: trīstis serves both.
[pause 2 seconds]
Anxius, sollicitus and īrātus follow laetus: -us for a man, -a for a woman, -ī for several. Trīstis has one form for a man and a woman, and trīstēs for several.

Why it's said this way: the cause, with quod.
[pause 2 seconds]
The cause of a feeling takes the quod you met as a reader, and here both verbs are scene:
Laeta eram, quod caelum serēnum erat. — I was happy, because the sky was clear. Trīstis eram, quod pluēbat. — I was sad, because it was raining.
[pause 2 seconds]
Latin can also put the feeling in a verb: gaudēbam, "I was glad", beside laetus eram; timēbam, "I was afraid". The adjective describes the person; the verb tells what the person was doing inside.

The word, taken apart: anxius and sollicitus.
Two of these feelings are bodily, and their own lessons said how. anxius is built on the verb for "to squeeze, to choke" (Lewis & Short): worry as a tight throat. sollicitus is built from sollus, "whole", and cieō, "to stir up" (Lewis & Short): stirred through and through. English took them over as anxious and solicitous.

Lesson 2 of 3.
spērō — I hope.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say "I was happy". (Laetus eram.) Then the noun for "hope". (Spēs.)
[pause 1 second]
A feeling looks back. A hope looks forward, and Latin has a verb for it built on the noun you own.

You'll want to know: spērō.
spērō, spērāre — "to hope". It takes three shapes.
Pācem spērō. — I hope for peace. Ut spērō, valēs. — As I hope, you are well. Spērō tē valēre. — I hope you are well.
[pause 2 seconds]
The first hopes for a thing, in the accusative. The second tucks ut spērō, "as I hope", into a sentence; Cicero writes it in his letters. The third is the accusative and infinitive of putō: tē, then valēre.
[pause 1 second]
When the hope is about later, Latin prefers an infinitive made for the future, a form still to come. For now, hope for things, and for how things are.

Grammar Lens: cupiō, volō + infinitive.
What you long to do goes with cupiō, "I long", or volō, "I want", and a plain infinitive:
Rōmam vidēre cupiō. — I long to see Rome. Mercātor esse volō. — I want to be a merchant.
[pause 2 seconds]
Mercātor stays nominative: it is the same person as the one who wants. And optō, "to wish for, to choose", hopes for a thing, like spērō: nāvem optō.

The word, taken apart: spērō.
spērō is the verb of spēs, "hope", and EtymDB 2.1 traces it, like the noun, to Proto-Indo-European speh₁-, "to prosper, to turn out well". Put dē-, "away", in front and you have the verb for "to give up hope" (Lewis & Short), which reached English as despair and desperate.

Lesson 3 of 3.
Review — how it felt, and what you hope for.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
A woman says "I was worried". (Sollicita eram.) Then say "I hope for peace". (Pācem spērō.)

What you've built.
Two lessons, and an experience has a before and an after. Before: a feeling with eram, agreeing with the one who felt it, and its cause with quod. After: spērō for what you hope for, and cupiō or volō with an infinitive for what you long to do.
Sarcinam āmīsī, et trīstis eram. Sed nunc laetus sum, et Rōmam vidēre cupiō. — I lost my pack, and I was sad. But now I am happy, and I long to see Rome.
[pause 2 seconds]
An event, a feeling in the past, a feeling now, and a wish.
