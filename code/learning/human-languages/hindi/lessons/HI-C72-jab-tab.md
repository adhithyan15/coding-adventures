# जब … तब … (*jab … tab …*) — when, then

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[HI-JOIN-KAB-01, HI-LEX-C40-DEIXIS-01, HI-LEX-C40-DEIXIS-02] -->

[PAUSE 3s] Retrieve the two items before this one by name: **क्योंकि**, and
**कब**. Then say the pair the deixis lesson gave you — **यह** and **वह**, *this* and
*that*, the **य-** shape and the **व-** shape. That machine is about to run
again.

## Grammar Lens: three first letters, one word
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[HI-JOIN-JAB-TAB-01]; assesses=[HI-GRAMMAR-C40-DEIXIS-SYSTEM, HI-CONCEPT-C05-MAINHINDIBOLTAHUN-01, HI-LEX-LISTEN, HI-GRAMMAR-VERB-FINAL-01] -->

> **जब मैं हिंदी बोलता हूँ, तब आप सुनते हैं.**
> — *jab maiṁ hindī boltā hūṁ, tab āp sunte haiṁ.* —
> **When I speak Hindi, then you listen.**

Look at the three words side by side:

| | |
|---|---|
| **कब** | when? — the question |
| **जब** | when — the clause that sets the time |
| **तब** | then — the clause that answers it |

One word, three first letters. **क-** asks, **ज-** points back at something,
**त-** points at it again. It is exactly the machine the deixis lesson taught with
**यह** and **वह**, and Hindi is unusually tidy about it: once you have seen the
pattern once you can hear which job a word is doing from its first letter alone.

Hindi says **both halves**. English drops the *then* and leaves *when I speak
Hindi, I feel happiness*; Hindi keeps **तब**, and it earns its place — it is what
tells the listener the first clause has finished, which matters far more in a
language where every clause ends on a verb.
