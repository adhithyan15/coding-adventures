The same writing, with a clock — the same writing against a clock, and the TORFL rule that makes the weakest of five papers the one that decides.

Before we start: this one needs your hands, so it is not a driving lesson. You will want a pen and something to write on. You can listen to all of it now and come back to the parts that need looking at once you have stopped.

Warm-up.

A task you can only do untimed is not yet a task you can do.

Writing — timed production.
What the target publishes. Russian's A1 exam is the state test TORFL at its Elementary Level, ТЭУ. The Pushkin Institute describes it as five separate subtests — Lexis and Grammar, Reading, Writing, Listening and Speaking — taking about three and a half hours in all.
What this book does not quote is the writing paper's own minutes or length. It has not yet transcribed the current paper into task-shapes/a1.json, so check the provider's current materials before the exam you book.
[pause 2 seconds]
So the clock here is this book's, not the exam's, and it is short. Set a three-minute timer. Write by hand to one reader you name: who you are, what you do, what you like. Lines you own, a dot at the end of each.
[pause 2 seconds]
When the three-minute timer rings, stop, even mid-word.

Wrap-up recall.
[pause 2 seconds]
Spend at most one minute checking, and do not rewrite the timed lines. Hurry shows in the hand first: check л against м, and the bar over т, before you check anything else.
[pause 2 seconds]
Now the rule worth carrying out of this lesson, because it changes how you study rather than how you write today.
[pause 2 seconds]
TORFL scores each subtest on its own, and this book's target is 66 percent in every one of the five. Some TORFL materials describe a narrower allowance for one subtest; this book does not plan around it.
[pause 2 seconds]
So the paper that decides your result is the one you are worst at, and there are five of them, not four. Strong reading cannot carry weak writing.
