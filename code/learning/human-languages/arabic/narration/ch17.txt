Arabic, chapter 17: Asking Someone's Age.
2 lessons. You can do the first 1 of them in the car; after that you will want to have stopped.


Lesson 1 of 2.
كم عمرك؟ — asking age with a noun, not a verb.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
You've seen Romance languages have their years, Germanic languages be their years, and Classical Latin itself use a third construction ("born" + accusative of duration). Arabic does something structurally different again: it doesn't use a verb for this at all.

You'll want to know — عمر — life, lifetime, age.
عمر (ʿumr) = "age, lifetime, life span" — from the root ع-م-ر (ʿ-m-r), broadly tied to the idea of living, dwelling, flourishing (the same root behind the personal name ʿUmar, and words for "building, habitation"). Age in Arabic isn't something you have or are — it's expressed as a possessed noun: "your ʿumr," "my ʿumr."

Grammar Lens: كم عمرك؟ — no verb needed.
كم عمرك؟ — "How old are you?" (to a man) — literally "how much [is] your ʿumr." (kam ʿumruka?)
عمري عشرون سنة. — "I am twenty years old." — literally "my ʿumr [is] twenty years." (ʿumrī ʿishrūna sana.)
Notice there's no verb "to be" at all in the present tense here — Arabic simply juxtaposes two nouns ("my age" / "twenty years"), exactly the way it forms ordinary present-tense sentences without is/are. So this is a genuinely fourth shape for the concept: not Romance's "I have twenty years," not Germanic's "I am twenty years [old]," not Latin's "born twenty years [so far]," but "my age [is] twenty years" — age as a possessed noun-quantity, stated without any verb at all.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: "ʿumr" — age, lifetime, life span]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: "kam ʿumruka?" — how old are you? (to a man)]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: "ʿumrī ʿishrūna sana" — my age is twenty years, no verb at all]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
What does عمر literally mean, and what root is it from? ("Age, lifetime" — root ع-م-ر, tied to living/flourishing.) Does Arabic use a verb to state age, the way Romance ("have"), Germanic ("be"), or Latin ("born" + duration) do? (No — it juxtaposes "my age" and the number, with no verb needed at all.) What does كم عمرك؟ literally ask? ("How much [is] your ʿumr?")


Lesson 2 of 2.
The letters that wrote my name — the writing set that built اسمي and بخير, retrieved cold — one bowl with four dot patterns, a letter with three jobs, and the marks everyday text leaves off.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, for letter shapes on the page. You can listen to everything else now — leave the sections called Script: one bowl, four letters, Script: the letter with three jobs, Script: the marks everyday text leaves off and Script: خير, taken apart until you have stopped, and I will say so again when we reach it.

Warm-up.
[pause 4 seconds]
Write اسمي and خير by hand, right to left, without looking below. Both were drawn stroke by stroke in the writing set and neither has been asked for since.

Script: one bowl, four letters.
[a table of 4 rows, read aloud]
letter: ب bāʾ. dots: one below. where it sits: inside بخير.
letter: ن nūn. dots: one above. where it sits: inside أنت.
letter: ت tāʾ. dots: two above. where it sits: inside أنت, تشرفنا.
letter: ث thāʾ. dots: three above. where it sits: —.
One shallow boat-bowl, drawn the same way every time. Only the dots change, and the dots are the letter. Two of these four build the word for you: an alif with a hamza seat, then nūn, then tāʾ.

Script: the letter with three jobs.
ي yāʾ is the same bowl body with two dots below — a tooth when joined. It does three separate jobs, and you have used all three:
consonant y, as in yawm, a day.
the long vowel ī, a seat for the ee sound.
the suffix ـي, glued on to mean my.
That third job is what turns اسم into اسمي. Four letters, right to left: alif, sīn, mīm, yāʾ.

Script: the marks everyday text leaves off.
The ḥarakāt are three small marks written around a consonant, not letters of their own: fatḥa above for a, kasra below for i, ḍamma above for u. Arabic is an abjad, so ordinary text leaves them out — and one of them carries the whole difference between أنتَ and أنتِ.
Hamza is not one of them. It writes a real consonant: the glottal catch in the middle of English uh-oh. Written alone it is ء; riding an alif seat it is أ, which is how the word for you begins.

Script: خير, taken apart.
خ khāʾ belongs to the hook-and-tail family — a rounded hook flowing into a tail that drops below the line, with one dot above. Its plain cousin ح has no dot and ج takes one below.
[once you have stopped driving — write: خير right to left — khāʾ with its dot, into yāʾ, then rā, and lift]
[once you have stopped driving — write: glue بـ on the front — بخير, its one dot below, joining straight into the khāʾ]
[once you have stopped driving — write: اسمي — alif, sīn, mīm, yāʾ]
Nothing joins after ر, which is why the pen lifts there.

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 4 seconds]
Name the one feature that separates ب ن ت ث. (The dots — one below, one above, two above, three above.) Give yāʾ's three jobs. (Consonant y, long vowel ī, the suffix meaning my.) Say whether hamza is a vowel mark. (No — a glottal stop.) And say what بـ adds to خير. (In — in goodness.)
