# येतो / येते (yeto / yete) — "I'll take my leave"

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[MR-PHRASE-YETO-YETE-01]; assesses=[MR-LEX-NAMASKAR-01, MR-LEX-NAHI-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] The Marathi goodbye — which, like the Dravidian languages, refuses to
say a bare "I'm leaving." And it changes with the speaker's gender.

## The letters in this word
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[MR-PHRASE-YETO-YETE-01] -->

*(Skim if you read Devanagari.)* **ये** (*ye*) + **तो** (*to*) → *yeto*; **ये** +
**ते** (*te*) → *yete*.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[MR-PHRASE-YETO-YETE-01] -->

Marathi, like the Dravidian languages to its south, will not say a bare "I'm
leaving." The everyday goodbye is **येतो** (*yeto*) — literally "I **come**
[again]," a promise of return, never a final departure. (Fuller: *yeto,
namaskār*.)

## Grammar Lens: Marathi verbs carry gender
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[MR-PHRASE-YETO-YETE-01] -->

*yeto* is what a **man** says; a **woman** says **येते** (*yete*). The verb
itself changes with the speaker's gender — something Hindi does only in the past
tense, but Marathi does in the **present** too. (And Marathi keeps **three**
genders — masculine, feminine, and neuter — where Hindi kept only two.) You'll
meet this at every verb.
