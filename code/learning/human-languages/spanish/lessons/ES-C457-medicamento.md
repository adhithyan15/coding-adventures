## You'll want to know: el medicamento
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-C457-GUARDIA-01]; assesses=[ES-LEX-MEDICO] -->

**el medicamento** — the medicine, the drug: the formal word, and the one on
the packaging.

*Un medicamento con receta* — a prescription medicine. *Este medicamento no lo
tenemos* — we don't have that one in.

In speech people say **la medicina** as readily, and for a single pill
**la pastilla**. *Medicamento* is what the box, the leaflet and the pharmacist
will use.

## Grammar Lens: -mento and -miento are the same ending
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C457-GUARDIA-01, ES-LEX-C353-WHERE-23] -->

You know **-miento** from *el seguimiento*. **-mento** is the same Latin
ending arriving by the other road:

| worn down by speech | lifted from the page |
|---|---|
| el segui**miento** | el medica**mento** |
| el pensa**miento** | el docu**mento** |

Both are masculine, so nothing is lost either way. What the spelling tells you
is the **register**: a *-miento* noun is usually everyday, a *-mento* noun
usually formal or technical. The pattern held for *rueda* and *rotativo*, and
it holds here.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C457-GUARDIA-01, ES-LEX-MEDICO] -->

Latin **medicus**, a doctor — and behind it **medēri**, to heal, to look
after.

So *el médico* and *el medicamento* are one word: the healer, and the thing
that does the healing. The family runs wide — *la medicina*, *remedio*, and
English **remedy**, **medical**, **meditate**.

That last one is worth a second, and worth handling carefully. An older root
does sit behind *medeor*, and **modus**, **moderate** and **meditate** are
independent evidence for it. What you should not do is gloss that root as
"measure" and then offer a doctor's judgement as the proof — that argues in a
circle, as the lesson on *el médico* has already said. The cousins are real;
the story about why is not established.
