Hindi, chapter 91: Character, Not Mood.
8 lessons. You can do the first 3 of them in the car; after that you will want to have stopped.


Lesson 1 of 8.
बुरा (burā) — bad, and the phrase you will hear it in first.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
You have had acchā for a long time and have never had its opposite. Say it, then notice that you cannot yet say the other thing.

You'll want to know: बुरा.
बुरा (burā) — "bad."
It ends in -आ, so by now you know without being told: burā, burī, bure. The shape rule you learned about size words works on character words too, because it was never about meaning.

The word, taken apart.
बुरा (burā) is the word inside one of the most-heard sentences in everyday Hindi:
बुरा मत मानो — burā mat māno — "don't take it badly."
Literally: do not consider it bad. It is what a speaker adds after saying something blunt, and it is closer to English "no offence" than to any apology. You will hear it long before you need to produce it, so recognising it is worth more right now than being able to build it.
A second everyday one: बुरा लगना — burā lagnā, "to feel hurt," literally to strike one as bad. Hindi describes being offended as something the remark does, not something you do.

Grammar Lens: a pair that behaves identically.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
adjective: acchā. masculine: acchā. feminine: acchī.
adjective: बुरा (burā). masculine: बुरा (burā). feminine: बुरी.
Both inherited, both ending in -आ, both bending. The pair is the easiest one in the chapter, and the rest of the chapter is where it stops being easy: four of the six words ahead do not bend.

Lesson 2 of 8.
होशियार (hośiyār) — holding your wits, which is a compliment and a warning at once.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say आसान. It ends in a consonant and never bends. The word in this lesson ends in a consonant too — so predict what it will do before reading on.

You'll want to know: होशियार.
होशियार (hośiyār) — "clever, sharp, alert."
It ends in र, a consonant, so it does not bend. Ek hośiyār laṛkā, ek hośiyār laṛkī — the adjective is the same shape for both.

The word, taken apart.
Persian, and built from two pieces:
होश (hoś) — consciousness, one's senses, one's wits.
-यार (-yār) — holding, keeping.
So a hośiyār person is one who keeps hold of their senses. Not one who knows a great deal — one who is awake.
That is why the word does double duty. Called out sharply, होशियार (hośiyār)! means "look out!" — the Indian Railways used it as the standard shouted warning. The same word praises a bright child and stops a man walking into a moving cart, because both are about being alert.
होश on its own is worth keeping. Hoś meṅ ānā is "to come to one's senses," and hoś uṛnā is literally "for one's wits to fly away" — to be stunned.

Grammar Lens: the first one that does not bend.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
adjective: बुरा (burā). masculine: बुरा (burā). feminine: बुरी.
adjective: होशियार (hośiyār). masculine: होशियार (hośiyār). feminine: होशियार (hośiyār).
The difference is not that one is about character and the other about worth. It is the last letter, and nothing else. बुरा (burā) is inherited and ends in -आ; होशियार (hośiyār) is Persian and ends in a consonant.

Lesson 3 of 8.
मेहनती (mehnatī) — an ending that makes a person out of a thing.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say होशियार (hośiyār). It ends in a consonant and stays put. This word ends in -ई, which is a third shape, and it also stays put — for a different reason.

You'll want to know: मेहनती.
मेहनती (mehnatī) — "hard-working, industrious."
It is built out of a noun you can see inside it: मेहनत (mehnat), "hard work, toil," plus the ending -ई.

The word, taken apart.
मेहनत is Arabic miḥna, which did not start out meaning honest labour. It meant a trial, an ordeal, a testing — the thing that puts a person through it. Work became the name for the ordeal, which is a judgement about work that several languages independently agreed on.
The -ई on the end is one of the most useful endings in Hindi. Attach it to a noun and you get the person characterised by that thing:
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
noun: मेहनत — toil. the person: मेहनती (mehnatī) — one who toils.
noun: शरम — shame. the person: शर्मीला (śarmīlā) — one who feels it.
noun: ज़रूरत — need. the person: ज़रूरी — necessary.
It is the same job English does with -y in wealthy, greedy, speedy — and, in fact, the very same job Persian does with -ī in Hindī, "belonging to Hind."

Grammar Lens: the third shape, and why it also stays put.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
ending: -आ. example: बुरा (burā). changes for a woman?: yes, to -ई.
ending: consonant. example: होशियार (hośiyār). changes for a woman?: no.
ending: -ई. example: मेहनती (mehnatī). changes for a woman?: no.
The reason the third row does not move is worth seeing rather than memorising: the feminine ending an -आ adjective changes to is -ई. A word that already ends in -ई has nowhere to go. It is not an exception — it is the rule arriving at a word that is already there.

[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
What did Arabic miḥna mean before it meant work? (A trial or ordeal.) Why does an -ई adjective not change for a woman? (-ई is already the feminine ending; there is nowhere further to go.)


Lesson 4 of 8.
शर्मीला (śarmīlā) — the ending that turns a feeling into a character.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, because the lesson points at something written down. You can listen to all of it now and come back to the parts that need looking at once you have stopped.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say मेहनती (mehnatī). You saw an ending build a person out of a noun. The same thing happens here with a different ending, and this one bends.

You'll want to know: शर्मीला.
शर्मीला (śarmīlā) — "shy, bashful."
Built from शरम / शर्म (śaram), "shame, modesty," plus the ending -ईला.

The word, taken apart.
The ending -ईला is not the same as the -ई of mehnatī, and the difference is exactly the difference this chapter is about.
-ई says has this thing. -ईला says is made of this thing — it builds a settled quality, a disposition rather than an episode:
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
noun: शरम — shame. with -ईला: शर्मीला (śarmīlā) — shy by nature.
noun: रंग — colour. with -ईला: रंगीला — colourful, flamboyant.
noun: नशा — intoxication. with -ईला: नशीला — intoxicating.
That is why शर्मीला is the right word for an exam question about character. Someone can feel shame at a moment; a śarmīlā person is one for whom it is a standing trait. This is settled character, not mood — the distinction the whole chapter turns on.
शर्म itself is Persian, and it is the same word as English shame: both descend from the same ancient root for covering oneself.

Grammar Lens: back to the bending shape.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
adjective: बुरा (burā). masculine: बुरा (burā). feminine: बुरी.
adjective: मेहनती (mehnatī). masculine: मेहनती (mehnatī). feminine: मेहनती (mehnatī).
adjective: शर्मीला (śarmīlā). masculine: शर्मीला (śarmīlā). feminine: शर्मीली.
-ईला ends in -आ, so the rule applies as usual and the word bends. The long ending does not protect it: look at the last letter, always.

Lesson 5 of 8.
मिलनसार (milansār) — the opposite of shy, built out of a word you have had for ages.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say शर्मीला (śarmīlā). Now think of the opposite kind of person — the one who is easy with strangers. Hindi builds that word out of something you learned in order to say goodbye.

You'll want to know: मिलनसार.
मिलनसार (milansār) — "sociable, friendly, easy to get on with."

The word, taken apart.
Two pieces, and you already have the first:
मिलन (milan) — a meeting, from मिलना, "to meet" — the verb inside phir milenge, the farewell you learned early.
-सार (-sār) — a Persian ending meaning inclined toward, full of.
So a milansār person is inclined toward meeting. Hindi names the trait after the act rather than after the feeling, which is a plainer way to describe a person than English manages: sociable goes back to Latin socius, a companion, and is a noun about a relationship; milansār is a verb about what someone actually does.
The -सार ending is rare enough to be worth flagging as a small set rather than a rule to apply. You will meet शर्मसार (śarmsār), "ashamed" — built on the same śaram as the last lesson.

Grammar Lens: a consonant ending, so it stands still.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
adjective: शर्मीला (śarmīlā). masculine: शर्मीला (śarmīlā). feminine: शर्मीली.
adjective: होशियार (hośiyār). masculine: होशियार (hośiyār). feminine: होशियार (hośiyār).
adjective: मिलनसार (milansār). masculine: मिलनसार (milansār). feminine: मिलनसार (milansār).
The pair शर्मीला (śarmīlā) / मिलनसार (milansār) is the sharpest demonstration in the chapter: two opposite traits, and one bends while the other does not. Meaning has nothing to do with it. Only the last letter does.

Lesson 6 of 8.
गंभीर (gambhīr) — deep, which is how three separate languages say serious.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, because the lesson points at something written down. You can listen to all of it now and come back to the parts that need looking at once you have stopped.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
You learned that मोटा describes a deep voice. Hold on to that idea of depth — this lesson takes it somewhere else entirely.

You'll want to know: गंभीर.
गंभीर (gambhīr) — "serious, grave, solemn."
It ends in र, a consonant, so it does not bend.

The word, taken apart.
Sanskrit गम्भीर, and its first meaning was deep — of water, of a well, of a voice.
What is striking is how many unrelated languages reached for depth or weight when they needed a word for gravity of manner:
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
language: Hindi. word: गंभीर (gambhīr). first meaning: deep.
language: Latin. word: gravis. first meaning: heavy.
language: English. word: profound. first meaning: deep.
A serious person, in all three, is one you cannot see the bottom of. Hindi still uses the literal sense freely: a gambhīr river is a deep one, and a gambhīr illness is a grave one.
One useful distinction, and it is the same one English draws. A gambhīr person is solemn and weighty. A gambhīr situation is dangerous. The word does not decide which; the noun does.

Grammar Lens: all six, sorted by ending.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
ending: -आ. the words: बुरा (burā), शर्मीला (śarmīlā). feminine: बुरी, शर्मीली.
ending: consonant. the words: होशियार (hośiyār), मिलनसार (milansār), गंभीर (gambhīr). feminine: unchanged.
ending: -ई. the words: मेहनती (mehnatī). feminine: unchanged.
Two bend and four do not, and you can sort them without knowing what a single one means.

Lesson 7 of 8.
सात शब्द स्वभाव के (sāt śabd svabhāv ke) — seven words, three endings, and one pair of opposites.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, because the lesson points at something written down. You can listen to all of it now and come back to the parts that need looking at once you have stopped.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Name as many of the seven as you can before reading the table. Aim for five.

Grammar Lens: the ending decides, and the meaning never does.
All seven, sorted by what their last letters allow:
[a table of 7 rows, read aloud]
word: acchā. meaning: good. feminine: acchī.
word: बुरा (burā). meaning: bad. feminine: बुरी.
word: शर्मीला (śarmīlā). meaning: shy. feminine: शर्मीली.
word: होशियार (hośiyār). meaning: clever. feminine: होशियार (hośiyār).
word: मिलनसार (milansār). meaning: sociable. feminine: मिलनसार (milansār).
word: गंभीर (gambhīr). meaning: serious. feminine: गंभीर (gambhīr).
word: मेहनती (mehnatī). meaning: hard-working. feminine: मेहनती (mehnatī).
Read the third column against the first. Where the two differ, the word moves for a woman; where they are identical, it does not. Three move and four stay.

Grammar Lens: where each word came from.
The split is not random, and seeing why makes the endings predictable:
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
source: inherited from Sanskrit. the words: acchā, बुरा (burā), गंभीर (gambhīr). ends in: mostly -आ.
source: Persian. the words: होशियार (hośiyār), मिलनसार (milansār), शर्मीला (śarmīlā). ends in: mostly a consonant.
source: Arabic through Persian. the words: मेहनती (mehnatī). ends in: -ई.
Inherited words are the ones that kept the -आ ending, because they are the ones that went through the whole sound history of the language. Borrowed words arrived later, in whatever shape the lending language had them, and mostly that shape ended in a consonant.
गंभीर (gambhīr) is the exception that proves it is a tendency rather than a law: it is inherited from Sanskrit and still ends in a consonant. So the origin explains the pattern — the ending is what you act on.

Lesson 8 of 8.
वह कैसी है? (vah kaisī hai?) — the question the seven were assembled to answer.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say होशियार (hośiyār) and मेहनती (mehnatī). Two words no reference letter could do without, and the track had neither until now.

Grammar Lens: the question word bends too.
कैसा is itself an -आ word, so the question agrees before the answer does:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
about: a man. question: वह कैसा है? a possible answer: वह गंभीर (gambhīr) है।.
about: a woman. question: वह कैसी है? (vah kaisī hai?) a possible answer: वह शर्मीली है।.
Notice what that means in practice. The gender is announced in the question, before any adjective arrives. A listener who catches only kaisī already knows a woman is being asked about — and knows that any bending adjective in the answer must end in -ई.

Reading.
Read aloud. Every word has been taught:
— आपकी बहन कैसी है? — वह होशियार (hośiyār) है। वह मेहनती (mehnatī) भी है। वह शर्मीली है। — आपका भाई कैसा है? — वह मिलनसार (milansār) है। वह गंभीर (gambhīr) भी है।.
Five adjectives in that exchange, and only शर्मीली had to change its ending. The other four were decided by their own last letters rather than by who was being described — which is the entire point of the chapter, put into one conversation.
