# tener — "to have," your first irregular verb

## Warm-up

[PAUSE 2s] Every verb so far has been **regular** — *hablo, como, vivo*, all
tidy. **tener** ("to have") is your first **irregular** one, and it breaks the
rule in the two ways Spanish verbs most often do. Meet the exceptions now; you'll
see them again and again.

## Sounds you'll need

- `diphthong-ie` — the stem grows a *-ie-*: **tienes** = *TYE-nes*.

## The word, taken apart

**tener** comes from Latin **tenēre**, *"to hold, to keep, to grasp."* Having is,
at root, *holding*. That root has a firm grip on English:

- **tenant** (one who *holds* land), **tenure**, **tenacious** (*holding* on),
  **tenable**, **tenet** (a *held* belief).
- with prefixes: **contain** ("hold together"), **retain** ("hold back"),
  **maintain** ("hold in hand"), **sustain** ("hold up"), **detain**, **obtain**.

## Grammar Lens: the two irregularities

*tener* misbehaves in exactly the two classic Spanish ways:

1. **The *yo* form is odd**: not *"teno"* but **tengo** (a *-g-* appears). Several
   common verbs do this (*hago, pongo, salgo, vengo*).
2. **The stem vowel breaks *e → ie*** in the stressed forms — a **stem-changer**:

| yo | **tengo** | (the *-go* form) |
| tú | **tienes** | (e → ie) |
| él/ella/usted | **tiene** | (e → ie) |
| nosotros | **tenemos** | (back to *e* — unstressed) |
| ellos/ustedes | **tienen** | (e → ie) |

Notice *tenemos* keeps the plain *e* — the vowel only cracks to *ie* when it's
**stressed**. That "**boot**" pattern (all forms but *nosotros/vosotros*) is the
single most common irregularity in Spanish; *tener* is your model for it.

## Wrap-up Recall

[PAUSE 3s] What Latin verb is *tener* from, and its English cousins? (*tenēre*,
"to hold" — tenant, retain, contain.) Its two irregularities? (The *-go* yo-form
*tengo*; the *e→ie* stem-change *tienes/tiene/tienen*.) Which form keeps the plain
*e*? (*tenemos* — it's unstressed.) Next: use it to give your **age**.
