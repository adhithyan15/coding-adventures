Spanish, chapter 461: El donativo.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
donar — to give it away.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say perdón and la sangre. There is a verb of giving hiding in the first, and the second is the commonest thing it is used about.

You'll want to know: donar.
donar — to donate.
Donar sangre — to give blood, the sign outside every mobile clinic. Donar ropa — to donate clothes. La donación is the donation and el donante the donor.
It is not the everyday verb for giving. Dar is that. Donar is giving to an institution or a cause, and it carries no expectation of return.

Grammar Lens: donar is not regalar.
Three verbs of giving, and the difference is who receives:
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
what is happening: handing anything to anyone. the verb: dar.
what is happening: making somebody a present. the verb: regalar.
what is happening: giving to a cause or a body. the verb: donar.
Te doy el taladro — I'm passing it to you. Te regalo el taladro — it is yours, a gift. Dono el taladro al centro — the neighbourhood centre has it now.
Use donar about a person and it sounds like a charitable act rather than a kindness, which is sometimes exactly the joke a Spanish speaker is making.

The word, taken apart.
Latin donare, to give — and here is the payoff on a word you learned in your first weeks.
Perdón is per- plus donare: to give thoroughly, to give completely away. Forgiving somebody is giving the offence away rather than holding it, and the metaphor is the same in English — for-give is built the identical way, for- being an intensifier, so pardon and forgive are the same idea twice.
The family also gives el don, a gift or a talent, and la dosis, through Greek — a dose is what is given to you.

Lesson 2 of 5.
peligroso — dangerous.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say el taladro and el accidente. One can lead to the other, and the sign warning you sits between them.

You'll want to know: peligroso.
peligroso — dangerous.
Peligro. No pasar.
El peligro is the noun and the single word on the sign; peligroso is the adjective. Una zona peligrosa — a dangerous area. Es peligroso dejarlo ahí — it's dangerous to leave it there.
Estar en peligro is to be in danger, and fuera de peligro — out of danger — uses the frame you already have from fuera de servicio.

Grammar Lens: -oso makes an adjective of a noun.
-oso means "full of" or "given to", and it turns nouns into adjectives all over the language:
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
noun: el peligro. adjective: peligroso.
noun: el cuidado. adjective: cuidadoso — careful.
noun: la fama. adjective: famoso — famous.
English has the same ending in dangerous, famous and curious, from the same Latin -osus, so the pair usually translates straight across.
It agrees like any adjective — peligrosa, peligrosos — and the noun keeps its own gender: el peligro is masculine, una zona peligrosa feminine.

The word, taken apart.
Latin periculum, a trial or a risk — from a root meaning to try, the one also behind experiri, to test by going through.
So a peligro is something you have to try your luck against, and English peril is the same word, kept closer to the Latin. Experience and experiment are relatives too: all of them are attempts, and only some are survived.
From periculum to peligro the r and the l swapped places — the metathesis you met in milagro from miraculum. Both words came through speech, and both got their letters shuffled on the way; a word that arrived by book kept them, which is why experiencia looks so different.

Lesson 3 of 5.
rechazar — to turn it down.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say aceptar and admitir. The word coming next undoes the first, and it is made of the same Latin.

You'll want to know: rechazar.
rechazar — to turn down, to reject, to refuse.
Rechazar una oferta — to turn down an offer. Han rechazado la solicitud — the application has been rejected. El banco rechazó la tarjeta — the card was declined.
El rechazo is the noun: a refusal, and in medicine the rejection of a transplant.

Grammar Lens: the three verbs of the counter, completed.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
the move: you agree to what is offered. the verb: aceptar.
the move: the institution lets it through. the verb: admitir.
the move: it is turned back. the verb: rechazar.
Rechazar is the mirror of both of the others, and which one it mirrors depends on who is doing it. A shop rechaza your card where it might have aceptado it; an office rechaza your form where it might have admitido it.
That is why the verb turns up so often in the passive-looking se form on notices — no se aceptan, no se admiten — and in the plain active when a person does it: rechacé la oferta.
Note the spelling: rechacé, with a c. A z before e becomes c in Spanish, always, which is why empezar gives empecé.

The word, taken apart.
re- plus an old Spanish verb chazar, to drive back — and behind that Vulgar Latin captiare, to chase, built on capere, to take.
So rechazar and aceptar are cousins, and the pair is worth holding: ad- plus capere is taking a thing towards you, and re- plus captiare is driving it back. One Latin verb of taking, pointed two ways by two prefixes.
The same captiare gave Spanish cazar, to hunt, and French chasser, which English borrowed as chase — and, through a second borrowing, as catch. To reject something is to chase it back the way it came.

Lesson 4 of 5.
(donar, peligroso, rechazar) — from cold.

Warm-up.
[pause 3 seconds]
Close the lessons before this one. Say all three: to donate, dangerous, to turn down.

Grammar Lens: one verb of taking, pointed two ways.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
word: aceptar. the prefix, and where it points: ad-, towards you — you take it.
word: rechazar. the prefix, and where it points: re-, back — you drive it away.
Both are Latin capere, to take. The prefix does everything, exactly as it did for meter, el permiso, permitir and admitir off mittere.
That is now two families in three chapters where the prefix carries the whole meaning. When a Spanish verb starts with a-, re-, per-, ad- or des-, the part after it is usually a word you can already find.

Grammar Lens: two words hiding in your earliest vocabulary.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
new word: donar. where it was already: perdón — per-donare, to give thoroughly.
new word: peligroso. where it was already: el peligro, and periculum behind it.
Perdón is the one to keep. You learned it in the first weeks as a thing you say when you bump into somebody, and it turns out to be built on giving — forgiveness is handing the offence over rather than keeping it. English does the identical thing in for-give.

Why it's said this way.
Se rechazan los que estén rotos o sean peligrosos is the sentence to take apart, and the subjunctive is doing the same job it did on the swimming-course notice.
Los que estén rotos, not están. The poster is not saying that some of the toys are broken; it is describing a category that may or may not turn up — whichever ones happen to be broken. The indicative would point at particular toys the writer had in mind, which on a poster addressed to a whole building would be an odd thing to imply.
Note sean as well: ser for a lasting property, because a toy that is dangerous for a toddler is dangerous by design, not by circumstance. And estén for rotos, because being broken is a state a thing arrived at. One sentence, both verbs, each right for its own reason.

Then check yourself: did you use the subjunctive for the things that might turn up, and ser against estar for the two reasons?
