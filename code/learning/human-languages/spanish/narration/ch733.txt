Spanish, chapter 733: Gripe y resfriado.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
la gripe — the flu.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Spanish for to fall ill, then the Spanish for to get worse.

You'll want to know: la gripe.
la gripe — "the flu".
Estoy en cama con gripe. I am in bed with the flu.
In Mexico and Colombia people say la gripa.

The word, taken apart.
gripe was borrowed from French grippe (EtymDB), from gripper "to seize", which comes from Frankish grīpan: an illness that grips you.

Lesson 2 of 5.
el resfriado — the cold (illness).

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Spanish for to get worse, then the Spanish for the flu.

You'll want to know: el resfriado.
el resfriado — "the cold (illness)".
Tengo un resfriado fuerte. I have a bad cold.
In Spain also el catarro; in parts of South America el resfrío.

The word, taken apart.
resfriado is built on resfriarse "to catch cold", from frío "cold", which is usually traced to Latin frīgidus.

Lesson 3 of 5.
constipado — having a cold, bunged up.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Spanish for the flu, then the Spanish for the cold.

You'll want to know: constipado.
constipado — "having a cold, bunged up".
Estoy constipado y no puedo respirar bien. I have a cold and cannot breathe properly.
A false friend, mainly in Spain: estar constipado is to have a cold. Constipated is estreñido.

The word, taken apart.
constipado comes from Latin cōnstīpātus "crowded or pressed closely together" (EtymDB), from stīpāre "to pack": a head packed tight.

Lesson 4 of 5.
contagiar — to infect, to pass on.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Spanish for the cold, then the Spanish for having a cold.

You'll want to know: contagiar.
contagiar — "to infect, to pass on".
No quiero contagiarte el resfriado. I do not want to give you my cold.
Contagiarse — to catch something. It is also used for feelings: contagia su alegría.

The word, taken apart.
contagiar is built on contagio, usually described as a learned borrowing from Latin contāgium "contact, infection", from tangere "to touch".
