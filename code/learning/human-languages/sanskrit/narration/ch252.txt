Lesson 1 of 5.
वल्ली (vallī) — a vine.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Sanskrit for nature, then the Sanskrit for mud.

You'll want to know: वल्ली.
वल्ली — vallī — "a vine".

The word, taken apart.
vallī may be borrowed from Dravidian: CDIAL 11429 says "possibly", comparing Tamil vaḷḷi, "creeper", and DEDR 5316 sets the Dravidian words beside it. The goddess Vaḷḷi of Tamil tradition bears the same name.

Lesson 2 of 5.
कण्टकः (kaṇṭakaḥ) — a thorn.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Sanskrit for mud, then the Sanskrit for a vine.

You'll want to know: कण्टकः.
कण्टकः — kaṇṭakaḥ — "a thorn".

The word, taken apart.
kaṇṭaka is a thorn or anything pointed, already in the Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa (CDIAL 2668). MW gives no derivation, so its origin is uncertain. It lives on as Punjabi kaṇḍā and as the Hindi word for thorn.

Lesson 3 of 5.
कलिका (kalikā) — a bud.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Sanskrit for a vine, then the Sanskrit for a thorn.

You'll want to know: कलिका.
कलिका — kalikā — "a bud".

The word, taken apart.
kalikā is kali, "unblown bud", with the suffix -kā, first found in Kālidāsa (CDIAL 2934). Its origin is uncertain, but it lives on as Hindi kalī, "bud".

Lesson 4 of 5.
मल्लिका (mallikā) — jasmine.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Sanskrit for a thorn, then the Sanskrit for a bud.

You'll want to know: मल्लिका.
मल्लिका — mallikā — "jasmine".

The word, taken apart.
mallikā names the Arabian jasmine (MW). CDIAL 9913 compares Tamil mullai, "jasmine", and DEDR 4987 sets the Dravidian words beside it, so a Dravidian source is possible but not settled. Hindi mallī, "jasmine", comes from it.

Lesson 5 of 5.
तुलसी (tulasī) — holy basil.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Sanskrit for a bud, then the Sanskrit for jasmine.

You'll want to know: तुलसी.
तुलसी — tulasī — "holy basil".

The word, taken apart.
tulasī is the holy basil, first in the Bhāgavata Purāṇa (CDIAL 5885). DEDR 3357 sets Tamil tuḷaci beside it and says some Dravidian forms were borrowed back from Sanskrit; its first origin is uncertain. It lives on as Hindi tulsī.
