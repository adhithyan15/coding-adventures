Gujarati, chapter 36: And, Or, But.
5 lessons. You can do the first 2 of them in the car; after that you will want to have stopped.


Lesson 1 of 5.
અને (ane) — and.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
From the chapter behind you, the two sentences that get you out of trouble. Then, from the food chapter far behind you, tea and milk.
[your turn — recall: māf karo, samajto nathī — then chā, dūdh]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
Both of those drinks were taught in the same chapter, on facing pages. Until this lesson there has been no way to ask for both of them in one breath.

The word, taken apart.
ચા અને દૂધ — chā ane dūdh — tea and milk.
અને (ane) stands between the two things it joins, exactly where English puts and. That is the whole of it for now.
It did not start as a joining word. અને (ane) comes from Sanskrit અન્ય (anya), "other" — so the word that now means and began as the word for the other one. Naming a second thing and calling it "the other" is how a great many languages built their and, and Gujarati still shows the seam.

[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: chā alone, then the pair. Hear what the middle word buys you.]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — build: pick any two things you can already name, and join them.]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Join two drinks in one phrase. Where does અને (ane) stand relative to the things it joins? (Between them.) What did it mean before it meant and? ("Other".)


Lesson 2 of 5.
અને (ane), joining two sentences — and, joining two whole sentences — the first two-clause sentence in this book.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
One lesson back: the joining word, and where it stands. Five lessons back, at the top of the last chapter: the word that replaces chhe.
[your turn — recall: ane, and nathī]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

The pattern, taken apart.
The same અને (ane) that joined two nouns joins two whole sentences, and it changes nothing about either one:
(first sentence) અને (ane) (second sentence).
Nothing is added to the verbs, nothing moves, and no ending appears. This is why and is the joining word to learn first: it is the only one that costs the sentences on either side of it absolutely nothing.
And it composes with what you learned last chapter — the second half is free to be a negative:
… અને (ane) … નથી — "… and … is not".
That is a two-clause sentence, and it is the first one this book has been able to make.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — build: two short sentences you can already say, joined with ane.]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — build: the same again, with nathī closing the second half.]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — contrast: the two sentences said separately, then joined. Same words.]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
What changes about a sentence when અને (ane) joins it to another? (Nothing at all.) Make one two-clause sentence whose second half is negative.


Lesson 3 of 5.
અને (ane) reaches the page — and, reaching the page on three signs taught long ago.

Before we start: this one needs your hands, so it is not a driving lesson. You will want a pen and something to write on and your eyes, for letter shapes on the page. You can listen to everything else now — leave the section called Script — three signs, none of them new until you have stopped, and I will say so again when we reach it.

Warm-up.
[pause 3 seconds]
Say the joining word before anything is uncovered. Then a long reach back — read the word for city, written many chapters ago and not read since.
[your turn — read: શહેર]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
That word has been waiting a long time for a second look; this is it.

Script — three signs, none of them new.
Uncover અને (ane) once. Name its pieces in order:
અ — the independent a, ન — na, ે — the e stroke above.
Every one of the three was taught long before this chapter. This is worth saying plainly: the word this book most needed was never a writing problem. It was never written down.

Writing — guided copy, model in view.
Keep the model in view and copy અને (ane) once. Check the ે sits above and to the right of ન, not over અ.

Wrap-up Recall.

Lesson 4 of 5.
અથવા (athavā) — or.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
One lesson back you wrote the joining word. Five lessons back, in the middle of the last chapter, you learned to say you had not followed.
[your turn — recall: અને (ane), and samajto nathī]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

The word, taken apart.
અને (ane) offers you both. અથવા (athavā) offers you one of two:
ચા અથવા દૂધ — chā athavā dūdh — tea or milk.
It stands in the same place ane does, between the two things, and it asks the listener to pick.
Inside it are two older words: Sanskrit અથ (atha, "then, moreover") and વા (vā, "or"). Gujarati fused a linking word and a choosing word and kept both.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: chā athavā dūdh — then chā ane dūdh. One asks, one gives.]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — contrast: the two joining words back to back, twice.]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — build: offer someone a choice of two things you can name.]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Offer a choice of two drinks, then offer both. Which word did each need? (athavā to choose, ane to give both.)


Lesson 5 of 5.
પણ (paṇ) — but, and also.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
The two joining words so far: one gives both, one asks you to choose. Say them.
[your turn — recall: ane, athavā]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

The word, taken apart.
પણ (paṇ) sets one thing against another. It joins two clauses the way ane does, and it warns that the second will pull against the first:
(first sentence) પણ (paṇ) (second sentence) — "… but …"
It works especially well with what you learned last chapter, because the sharpest contrast a beginner can draw is between something that is and something that નથી.

Grammar Lens: the same word, moved, means "also".
Put પણ (paṇ) between two clauses and it is but. Put it directly after a noun and it becomes also:
ચા પણ — chā paṇ — "tea too".
Nothing about the word changes. Only where it stands changes, and position alone carries the difference. This is the first time in this book that a word's job has depended on its place in the sentence rather than on its ending.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: two clauses joined with paṇ, the second one negative.]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: chā paṇ — the "also" reading, straight after a noun.]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — contrast: paṇ between clauses, then paṇ after a noun. Same word.]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Say tea too. Now say a sentence that uses પણ (paṇ) to mean but. What decides which of the two you get? (Where the word stands — after a noun, or between two clauses.) Next chapter: the word that lets you say what somebody thinks.
