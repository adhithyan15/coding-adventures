[PAUSE 2s] What decides the ending on a Spanish verb? (Its **subject**.)

## What you've built
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-GRAMMAR-PRESENT-ACTUAL-AND-DURATIVE, ES-GRAMMAR-QUANTIFIER-AGREES-WITH-NOUN, ES-GRAMMAR-PROPER-NOUN-NO-ARTICLE] -->

Four chants, and not one of them taught you anything you could not already do.

[PAUSE 2s] **First.** *Como* means *I eat* **and** *I'm eating*. One form, two
readings, and the long form is for insisting.

[PAUSE 2s] **Second.** The ending agrees with the subject — which is *why* the
pronoun can go, rather than a separate habit.

[PAUSE 2s] **Third.** *Mucha agua*, but *trabaja mucho*. It agrees on a noun
and freezes on a verb.

[PAUSE 2s] **Fourth.** *Veo a María*, not *a la María*. A name already points at
one thing, so the article has no work to do.

## Grammar Lens: why naming it was worth a chapter
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-GRAMMAR-AGREEMENT-EXPLAINS-PRO-DROP, ES-GRAMMAR-QUANTIFIER-FROZEN-AFTER-VERB, ES-GRAMMAR-TITLE-TAKES-ARTICLE] -->

[PAUSE 2s] You have obeyed all four of these for hundreds of chapters without
being told any of them. So what changed?

[PAUSE 2s] **Two things.** A rule you can state is a rule you can **check**
when you are unsure — and doing something correctly by reflex is no help at all
when somebody asks you directly why.

[PAUSE 2s] And naming one rule often explains another. Once *the ending agrees
with the subject* is said out loud, dropping the pronoun stops being a quirk to
memorise and becomes an obvious consequence. Three of this book's earliest
lessons quietly make more sense than they did yesterday.
