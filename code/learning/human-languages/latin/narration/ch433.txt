Latin, chapter 433: Beast of burden, Nag, She-mule, Pack saddle, To load.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
iūmentum, iūmentī — a beast of burden, a draught animal.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to jump down, then the Latin for to kick.

You'll want to know: iūmentum, iūmentī.
iūmentum, iūmentī — "a beast of burden, a draught animal".
Iūmenta sarcinās mercātōrum per montēs portant. Pack animals carry the merchants' bags over the mountains.
Second declension, neuter: iūmentum, iūmentī n. Any animal that pulls or carries: horse, mule, ass or ox.

The word, taken apart.
iūmentum is usually traced to iungō 'to yoke' (Lewis & Short, from an older iugimentum): an animal put under the yoke. EtymDB lists it without a parent, so the line is hedged.

Lesson 2 of 5.
caballus, caballī — a nag, a pack horse.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to kick, then the Latin for a beast of burden.

You'll want to know: caballus, caballī.
caballus, caballī — "a nag, a pack horse".
Caballus vetus et lentus est, sed sarcinās portat. The nag is old and slow, but it carries the bags.
Second declension, masculine: caballus, caballī m. An everyday, low word; the fine word is equus. French cheval and English cavalry come from it.

The word, taken apart.
caballus is usually traced to Gaulish caballos 'pack horse' (EtymDB); Lewis & Short also call it perhaps Celtic and compare Greek kaballēs. French cheval and, through Italian, English cavalry go back to it.

Lesson 3 of 5.
mūla, mūlae — a she-mule.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a beast of burden, then the Latin for a nag.

You'll want to know: mūla, mūlae.
mūla, mūlae — "a she-mule".
Mūla parva est sed fortis. The she-mule is small but strong.
First declension, feminine: mūla, mūlae f., the female of mūlus. Romans said a mule never foals, so cum mūla pepererit meant 'never'.

The word, taken apart.
mūla is the feminine of mūlus 'mule' (Lewis & Short). EtymDB traces mūlus to PIE mukslós.

Lesson 4 of 5.
clītellae, clītellārum — a pack saddle (for an ass or mule).

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a nag, then the Latin for a she-mule.

You'll want to know: clītellae, clītellārum.
clītellae, clītellārum — "a pack saddle (for an ass or mule)".
Asinus clītellās portat et in clītellīs saccī sunt. The donkey carries a pack saddle, and on the pack saddle there are sacks.
First declension, feminine plural: clītellae, clītellārum f. One thing, plural form.

The word, taken apart.
clītellae is inherited: EtymDB traces it to Proto-Italic kleitrelā. Lewis & Short give the sense but no origin.

Lesson 5 of 5.
onerō, onerāre — to load, to burden.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a she-mule, then the Latin for a pack saddle.

You'll want to know: onerō, onerāre.
onerō, onerāre — "to load, to burden".
Servī mūlōs sarcinīs onerant. The slaves load the mules with baggage.
onerō, onerāre, onerāvī, onerātum, with the thing in the ablative. English onerous and exonerate come from the same root.

The word, taken apart.
onerō is built on onus, oneris 'a load, a burden', from PIE h₃énh₂os (EtymDB; Lewis & Short). English onerous and exonerate come from the same family.
