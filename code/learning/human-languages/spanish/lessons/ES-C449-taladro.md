## You'll want to know: el taladro
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-C449-TALLER-04]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C444-CASA-02] -->

**el taladro** — the drill.

*¿Me dejas el taladro?* — can you lend me the drill? *Taladrar* — to drill:
*hay que taladrar la pared*, we need to drill the wall.

The word covers the machine and, in a workshop, the bit as well. *Un taladro
para madera* is a wood bit; *un taladro sin cable* is a cordless drill.

## Grammar Lens: lending, in Spanish
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C449-TALLER-04, ES-LEX-MANO-01] -->

Asking to borrow a tool is where two verbs get tangled, because Spanish has
no verb for *borrow* at all:

| what you want | what Spanish says |
|---|---|
| lend me the drill | *¿me **dejas** el taladro?* |
| can I borrow it? | *¿me lo **prestas**?* |

Both put the **lender** in the subject position. There is no sentence shaped
like *I borrow the drill from you*; you turn it round and ask the other
person to do the lending. *Dejar* is the everyday one and *prestar* the
slightly more formal.

Hand the tool back with *aquí tienes* and you have the whole exchange.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C449-TALLER-04, ES-LEX-C449-TALLER-03] -->

Latin **taratrum**, a borer — itself taken from Gaulish, the Celtic language
spoken in France before Latin arrived. The Romans met a better tool than their
own and kept the name with it.

From *taratrum* to *taladro* an **r** turned into an **l**, and notice that
*taratrum* has no *l* to begin with. The word had **two r's**, which is
awkward, so one of them gave way — a repeated sound in one short word is
unstable, and changing one of the pair is the easiest repair.

That is not what happened to *milagro* and *peligro*. Those came from
*miraculum* and *periculum*, which **did** have both letters, and the two
traded places. Two different habits, worth telling apart.
