\chapter*{Preface}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Preface}

This is the Latin volume of a wider project --- and it plays a special role.
Latin is a \textbf{taproot}: the ancestor of the Spanish, French, Italian, and
Portuguese tracks, and the single largest source of English's vocabulary after
English's own Germanic core. Almost every lesson here pays a double dividend ---
you learn a Latin word \emph{and} you see why a dozen English words, and their
Romance cousins, look the way they do.

Latin uses the alphabet you are reading, so there is no script to decode; the
work is the words and the \textbf{endings}. Latin carries meaning in its endings
rather than in word order or helper-words, which is why it can drop pronouns and
still be perfectly clear (\textit{ag\=o} already means ``I do''). Each chapter
introduces that machinery one piece at a time, exactly where a real phrase needs
it --- never as a front-loaded grammar table.

We use \textbf{classical} pronunciation (Caesar's \textit{v} was a \emph{w},
his \textit{c} always hard) and mark long vowels with a \textbf{macron}
(\=a \=e \=\i \=o \=u), as teaching texts do, though Romans themselves wrote none.
Roots are traced back toward Proto-Indo-European where the trail is clear, and
forward into English and the Romance languages --- every form supplied in full.

Same three commitments as the other volumes: one word per lesson, gone deep; the
widest \emph{honest} web of connections; the reason, not just the rule. Released
under CC~BY-SA~4.0.
