# तव नाम किम्? — and the reason you might not say it that way

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[SA-LEX-TVAM, SA-GRAMMAR-POLITENESS-BY-PERSON] -->

[PAUSE 3s] Two words for **you**. Say the familiar one. Then say the one you
would use on meeting a stranger, and say what is strange about it.

## The exchange
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[SA-PATTERN-WHATS-YOUR-NAME, SA-GRAMMAR-RESPECTFUL-QUESTION, SA-LEX-ANANDAH, SA-CULTURE-SANSKRIT-ANSWERING] -->

[PAUSE 1s] Six turns, and the only thing that moves is how much respect is in
the air.

- [YOU SAY: the familiar question — *tava nāma kim?*]
- [YOU SAY: the same question to someone you have not met — *bhavataḥ nāma kim?*]
- [YOU SAY: the answer, with your own name in it — *mama nāma … asti*]
- [YOU SAY: agree that you understood — *ām*]
- [YOU SAY: that meeting them is a joy — *ānandaḥ*]
- [PAUSE 2s]

## Why it's said this way
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[SA-GRAMMAR-POLITENESS-BY-PERSON, SA-GRAMMAR-RESPECTFUL-QUESTION] -->

*Bhavān* is not a second-person word wearing a polite coat. It is a
**third-person** word — *your honour* — and it drags the whole sentence with it:
the verb turns third-person, and *tava* becomes *bhavataḥ*.

That is worth holding beside the way you already know a language to do this:

| | familiar | respectful |
|---|---|---|
| **the word for you** | *tvam* | *bhavān* |
| **grammatical person** | second | **third** |
| **your, in the question** | *tava* | *bhavataḥ* |

Making respect out of the third person is a different device from making it out
of the plural. Both end in the same place — you avoid pointing straight at the
person — and only one of them changes the verb.

## Across the family
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[SA-CULTURE-ANJALI-BOW, SA-CULTURE-SANSKRIT-THANKS, SA-CULTURE-WELCOMING-A-GUEST, SA-CULTURE-SANSKRIT-ANSWERING] -->

Four things the greetings carry that no translation of them carries:

- the greeting **is a bow** — the hands come together, and the word says so
- thanks is spoken rarely between intimates, because it puts distance in
- welcoming someone in is a duty owed to a guest, not a pleasantry
- **ām** agrees; it does not merely acknowledge that you heard

Each of these is about what the word *does* between two people. Say the wrong
one at the wrong moment and the grammar stays perfect.
