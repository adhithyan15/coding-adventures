# Unit 3: *Ser* — Identity & Origin

## Warm-up

[PAUSE 2s] Recall out loud: *yo, tú, él/ella, nosotros, ellos*. Good — today
those pronouns get their first verb: *ser*, "to be" (for identity and
origin — there's a second "to be," *estar*, coming in Unit 5, for state and
location. Keeping them apart is the whole game; Unit 6 tackles the contrast
directly).

## New Material

**soy** — I am
> Etymology: this is where it gets interesting. The Spanish infinitive *ser*
> actually descends from Latin *sedēre* ("to sit" — cognate with English
> *session*, *sedentary*, *sedate*, *sediment*). But its present-tense
> conjugations — *soy, eres, es, somos, son* — descend from a *different*
> Latin verb, *esse* ("to be" — cognate with English *is*, *am*, *essence*,
> *entity*). Spanish *ser* is a fusion of two separate Latin verbs into one.
> *soy* ← Latin *sum* ("I am").

**eres** — you (informal) are
> ← Latin *es* ("you are"), same *esse* family as *soy*.

**es** — he/she/it is, you (formal) are
> ← Latin *est* ("he/she/it is").

**somos** — we are
> ← Latin *sumus* ("we are").

**son** — they are, you all are
> ← Latin *sunt* ("they are").

## Grammar Lens

*Ser* is what's called a **linking verb** (or "copula") — a verb that
doesn't describe an action, it just connects a subject to a description of
it: "Ana **is** tall," not "Ana **runs**." English has exactly one linking
verb doing this job: "to be" (*am, is, are, was, were*). Spanish also uses
"to be" for this — except Spanish actually has **two** separate verbs
where English has one: *ser* (this unit) and *estar* (next unit). English
speakers have to consciously learn to ask "which kind of 'is' is this?" —
a question that doesn't exist in English at all, because English never
had to split the two meanings apart. Unit 6 lays out exactly how to tell
them apart.
