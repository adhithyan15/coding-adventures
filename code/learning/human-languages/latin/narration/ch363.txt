Latin, chapter 363: Granting and Denying.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
concēdō, concēdere — to grant, to concede; to give way.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to prove wrong, then the Latin for a guess.

You'll want to know: concēdō, concēdere.
concēdō, concēdere — "to grant, to concede; to give way".
Hoc tibi concēdō, sed nōn omnia. I grant you this, but not everything.
concēdō, concēdere, concessī, concessum. Dative of the person, accusative of the point: hoc tibi concēdō. English concede and concession.

The word, taken apart.
concēdō is built from con- and cēdō "to go, to yield" (Lewis & Short): to step aside for someone. English concede comes from it.

Lesson 2 of 5.
dēnegō, dēnegāre — to deny flatly; to refuse.

You'll want to know: dēnegō, dēnegāre.
dēnegō, dēnegāre — "to deny flatly; to refuse".
Pater mihi pecūniam dēnegāvit. Father refused me the money.
dēnegō, dēnegāre, dēnegāvī, dēnegātum. Stronger than negō: a firm no. English deny comes through French from dēnegāre.

The word, taken apart.
dēnegō is built from dē- and negō "to say no" (EtymDB; Lewis & Short). English deny comes from it through Old French.

Lesson 3 of 5.
cōnfiteor, cōnfitērī — to confess, to admit.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to grant, then the Latin for to deny flatly.

You'll want to know: cōnfiteor, cōnfitērī.
cōnfiteor, cōnfitērī — "to confess, to admit".
Culpam meam cōnfiteor. I admit my fault.
Deponent: cōnfiteor, cōnfitērī, cōnfessus sum. A fuller form of fateor, to admit. English confess.

The word, taken apart.
cōnfiteor is built from con- and fateor "to admit" (EtymDB; Lewis & Short), the vowel weakened inside the compound. English confess comes from its participle cōnfessus.

Lesson 4 of 5.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to deny flatly, then the Latin for to confess.

You'll want to know: obiciō, obicere.
obiciō, obicere — "to throw in the way; to throw in someone's face".
Multī eī stultitiam obiciunt. Many people reproach him with folly.
obiciō, obicere, obiēcī, obiectum: dative of the person, accusative of the charge. English object and objection.

The word, taken apart.
obiciō is built from ob "in the way" and iaciō "to throw" (EtymDB; Lewis & Short). English object comes from its participle obiectum.

Lesson 5 of 5.
contendō, contendere — to insist, to maintain; to strive; to hurry.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to confess, then the Latin for to throw in the way.

You'll want to know: contendō, contendere.
contendō, contendere — "to insist, to maintain; to strive; to hurry".
Contendit sē innocentem esse. He insists that he is innocent.
contendō, contendere, contendī, contentum. With the accusative and infinitive, to insist; with ad, to hurry to a place. English contend.

The word, taken apart.
contendō is built from con- and tendō "to stretch" (EtymDB; Lewis & Short): to stretch every nerve, for a goal or for a claim. English contend comes from it.
