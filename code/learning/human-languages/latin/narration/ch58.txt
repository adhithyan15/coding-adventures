Latin, chapter 58: Reading — Words, Lines, and a First Passage.
3 lessons. All 3 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 3.
(reading) — six words, each one already a claim.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Every word below you have met. Not one is new.
[pause 1 second]
What is new is that nothing surrounds them.

Reading.
aqua liber schola magister discipulus oculus.
[pause 3 seconds]
Read down the list once, without stopping.
[pause 3 seconds]
Again, and notice that five of the six end in -us, -a or -er, and that you know what that ending means.

The word, taken apart.
[pause 2 seconds]
An English word standing alone tells you almost nothing about its job. Book could be the thing doing or the thing done to.
[pause 2 seconds]
Liber cannot. That ending says it is the one doing, before any sentence has been built around it. A Latin noun carries its job with it.
[pause 2 seconds]
That is what makes Latin readable in a way its word order is not. The endings do the work English does with position, and they do it one word at a time.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: the six words down the list, once, without stopping]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[repeat that twice]
[question — say your answer, then pause 12 seconds]
Which word is the teacher?

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 2 seconds]
How many of the six were new? (None.)
[pause 2 seconds]
What does a Latin ending tell you that an English word cannot? (Its job — before the sentence exists.)


Lesson 2 of 3.
(reading) — six lines, two of them one word.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
The last lesson gave you words alone. These are whole lines.
[pause 1 second]
You have said every one of them.

Reading.
Salvē! Valē! Nōmen meum est Marcus. Ego sum discipulus. Iterum! Bonus est.
[pause 3 seconds]
Read down once, without stopping.
[pause 3 seconds]
Again — and notice that two of the six are a single word, and that both of them are things you say to somebody.

The word, taken apart.
[pause 2 seconds]
Salvē and Valē are commands. Be well. Be strong. A Roman greeting and a Roman farewell are both orders, which is why neither needs anything around it.
[pause 2 seconds]
Bonus est has no subject on the page at all. Latin puts the person in the verb, so est is already he is or it is, and the sentence is complete with two words.
[pause 2 seconds]
That is the second thing reading Latin asks of you: expect the subject to be missing, and expect the verb to have it.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: the six lines, once, in order, without stopping]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[repeat that twice]
[question — say your answer, then pause 12 seconds]
Which line would you read as you leave?

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 2 seconds]
How many of the six were new? (None.)
[pause 2 seconds]
Where is the subject of Bonus est? (Inside the verb.)


Lesson 3 of 3.
(reading) — the first passage.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Six words, then six lines. Now twelve of them together.
[pause 1 second]
Forty-two words, and not one of them is new.

Reading.
Salvē! Nōmen meum est Marcus. Ego sum discipulus. Magister meus bonus est. Liber meus bonus est. Ego legō et bibō. Amīcus meus est discipulus. Schola bona est. Prīmus liber bonus est. Sextus liber bonus est. Iterum legō, quia liber bonus est. Valē!
[pause 3 seconds]
Read it once without stopping. Do not translate as you go — let the sentences arrive.
[pause 3 seconds]
Now read it again, and look at where est falls.

The word, taken apart.
[pause 2 seconds]
Nearly every line ends in est. That is not a habit of this passage; it is how Latin was written. The verb goes last, and the reader holds everything before it open until it arrives.
[pause 2 seconds]
Two lines break the pattern on purpose. Nōmen meum est Marcus puts est in the middle, because the name is the point and Latin moves what matters. And the last full line is the longest, because quia hangs a whole reason off a sentence that was already complete.
[pause 2 seconds]
Notice also bonus, bona, bonus — the same word three times, changing to match what it describes. You are reading agreement, not being told about it.
[pause 2 seconds]
This is the first whole passage the book has asked you to hold at once, and it works because everything in it was paid for.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: the passage aloud, once, without stopping]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[repeat that twice]
[question — say your answer, then pause 15 seconds]
Why does he read again?

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 2 seconds]
Where does a Latin sentence usually keep its verb? (Last.)
[pause 2 seconds]
Why is it schola bona and not schola bonus? (The adjective agrees — and you read it before anyone explained it.)
