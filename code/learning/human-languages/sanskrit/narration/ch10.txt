Lesson 1 of 5.
पानीयम् (pānīyam) — "water," the thing to be drunk.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, for letter shapes on the page. You can listen to everything else now — leave the section called The letters in this word until you have stopped, and I will say so again when we reach it.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say "Sanskrit is dear to me." (Saṁskṛtaṁ mama priyam.) Every word so far has named an action. Now for the nouns you would actually reach for — starting with the one you would ask for first.

You'll want to know: पानीयम्.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
Sanskrit: पानीयम्. sound: pānīyam. meaning: water.
Sanskrit: पिबति. sound: pibati. meaning: he, she, or it drinks.
पानीयम् (pānīyam) is neuter, and its final -म् marks that — a whole family of everyday nouns ends this way, and it will keep coming back this chapter.

The letters in this word.
पा is pā, the long-ā you have said since नाम. नी stacks न na under the long-ī sign ी. यम् closes it: y, the inherent a, then म् with its vowel stripped by the halant — pā-nī-ya-m.

The word, taken apart — the thing to be drunk.
पानीयम् (pānīyam) is built on the root पा (pā), "to drink," plus -अनीय (-anīya), a suffix meaning "fit to be ed." Literally: the thing that is to be drunk.
The root is PIE peh3-, "to drink." Latin took it into potare, and from that English has potion and poison — a drink, and a drink gone wrong.
Eastward, पानीयम् (pānīyam) wore down through Prakrit pāṇīya into the everyday water-word of the daughter languages: Hindi पानी (pānī), Gujarati pāṇī, and, in the Bangladesh register of Bengali, pani — every one तद्भव, worn down by ordinary speech, not carried over whole. Sanskrit's own oldest water-words, जल and उदक, took a different road entirely; this is not that word.

Lesson 2 of 5.
क्षीरम् (kṣīram) — "milk," and the dessert it left behind.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, for letter shapes on the page. You can listen to everything else now — leave the section called The letters in this word until you have stopped, and I will say so again when we reach it.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say "water," and the root it is built on. (Pānīyam; पा, "to drink.") Not every drink-word in Sanskrit has so tidy an answer.

You'll want to know: क्षीरम्.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
Sanskrit: क्षीरम्. sound: kṣīram. meaning: milk.
Sanskrit: क्षीर. sound: kṣīra. meaning: milk — the bare stem, still living inside Hindi khīr.
Neuter again, marked by the same -म् as पानीयम् (pānīyam).

The letters in this word.
क्ष is a new stacked pair: क k fused under ष, the hissed retroflex s — together they say kṣa, one push, not two. Then the long-ī sign ी, र ra, and the neuter म् you already know: kṣī-ra-m.

The word, taken apart — a disputed root, and a live dessert.
Where पानीयम् (pānīyam)'s history is settled, क्षीरम् (kṣīram)'s is not. One account traces it to an old Proto-Indo-European word for milk; another argues it was borrowed from a substrate language spoken where Indo-Iranian speakers first arrived in the region. Neither side has won.
What is clear is the family it sits in: Avestan xšuuidi- and Persian šir say roughly the same word, but as relatives, not children — all three come down from one shared Proto-Indo-Iranian ancestor, the way gṛhṇāti and English grab are cousins rather than the same word wearing different clothes.
The word's liveliest descendant is not a drink. Hindi खीर (khīr) is a sweet rice pudding cooked in milk — worn down, तद्भव — and English borrowed that dessert whole, as kheer.

Lesson 3 of 5.
अन्नम् (annam) — "food," the eaten thing.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, for letter shapes on the page. You can listen to everything else now — leave the section called The letters in this word until you have stopped, and I will say so again when we reach it.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say "milk" and "water." (Kṣīram, pānīyam.) One more thing you would ask for, and this one carries a root you already own.

You'll want to know: अन्नम्.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
Sanskrit: अन्नम्. sound: annam. meaning: food, grain.
Sanskrit: खाद्यम् अन्नम्. sound: khādyam annam. meaning: edible food — खादति's own adjective on it.
Neuter, and -म् again — the third noun running.

The letters in this word.
अ opens bare, no consonant to carry it. न्न doubles न na onto itself, held a beat longer than one n would be. म् closes it, the same stripped-vowel neuter ending: an-na-m.

The word, taken apart — the eaten thing.
Chapter 7 taught खादति, the everyday word for eating, and set beside it its untaken cousin: अद्, PIE ed-, the very root of English eat, which Sanskrit keeps alive as अत्ति.
अन्नम् (annam) is built on that same अद्, plus the old participle suffix -न — "eaten." The d and the n fuse into the doubled न्न you just sounded out. Food, named this way, is not a thing but an event: the thing that has been eaten.

Grammar Lens: three genders, one small system.
Every noun in this chapter has ended in -म्, and that is not coincidence: Sanskrit sorts every noun into one of three genders — masculine, feminine, and neuter — the same system that put a gendered form on the numerals back in Chapter 6, where the daughter languages inherited specifically the neuter forms, trīṇi and catvāri. This book teaches only the plain nominative singular of each noun, never the full run of cases and numbers behind it — but the gender is worth carrying, because it is not optional, and it is not guessable from meaning alone.

Lesson 4 of 5.
अ — one character, met inside words you already say.

Before we start: this one needs your hands, so it is not a driving lesson. You will want a pen and something to write on and your eyes, for letter shapes on the page. You can listen to everything else now — leave the sections called Script you'll notice: अ and Guided Practice until you have stopped, and I will say so again when we reach it.

Warm-up.
[pause 1 second]
Before the new one: न — what does it do?
[pause 2 seconds]
One character this time. Just one — and you have been saying it for pages without knowing which mark on the page it was.

Script you'll notice: अ.
अ — a.
It is an independent vowel — the shape the vowel a takes when a word begins with it, rather than the sign it becomes inside a word.
What it is made of:
a joined upper-and-lower left body.
a middle shoulder.
a right vertical stem.
the top shirorekhā.
You already say these, and every one of them has अ somewhere inside it:
अस्ति asti — is.
अहम् aham — I.
अहं संस्कृतं वदामि ahaṁ saṁskṛtaṁ vadāmi — I speak Sanskrit.
अस्ति, भवति asti, bhavati — is, and becomes.

Writing: अ.
1. curve around the upper bowl, then continue down and around the lower bowl without lifting.
2. lift and sweep the middle shoulder right.
3. lift and draw the right stem top-to-bottom.
4. lift and draw the top shirorekhā left-to-right.
Pen lifts: 3. The pen comes up 3 times and no more.
Verified modern printed teaching form; other Devanagari traditions split the body into more strokes.
This is one attested teaching order and not a national standard — handwriting here is taught with school-to-school variation. Source: Saurmandal, ‘Devanagari अ stroke order.svg’, frames 1–4, Wikimedia Commons, 5 August 2023.

Lesson 5 of 5.
◌ि — one character, met inside words you already say.

Before we start: this one needs your hands, so it is not a driving lesson. You will want a pen and something to write on and your eyes, for letter shapes on the page. You can listen to everything else now — leave the sections called Script you'll notice: ◌ि and Guided Practice until you have stopped, and I will say so again when we reach it.

Warm-up.
[pause 1 second]
Before the new one: स — what does it do?
[pause 2 seconds]
One character this time. Just one — and you have been saying it for pages without knowing which mark on the page it was.

Script you'll notice: ◌ि.
◌ि — i.
It is a vowel sign. It is not a letter and never stands alone: it attaches to a consonant and replaces the a built into it with i. Replaces, not adds — the a is gone.
Where it sits: a curl placed BEFORE (to the left of) the consonant, though pronounced after.
Worked through: श + ◌ि = शि — śi.
You already say these, and every one of them has ◌ि somewhere inside it:
अस्ति asti — is.
किम् kim — what.
न चिन्ता na cintā — no worry / it's nothing / you're welcome.
गच्छामि gacchāmi — I go (a way to take leave).

Writing: ◌ि — copy what you see.
Put your pen on ◌ि and follow its line. Copy the shape you can see — slowly, and larger than it is printed.
This book does not yet tell you where to start the character or which way to travel. That is a real thing, taught with real variation from school to school, and it is not written down here until it can be written down with a source. Copying what is in front of you needs no such source, and it is how the shape gets into your hand in the meantime.
