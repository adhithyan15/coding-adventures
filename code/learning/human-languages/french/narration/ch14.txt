French, chapter 14: To Have, and How Old You Are.
2 lessons. The first lesson already needs your eyes or your hands, so save this one for when you have stopped.


Lesson 1 of 2.
avoir — "to have".

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, because the lesson points at something written down. You can listen to all of it now and come back to the parts that need looking at once you have stopped.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
The verb French leans on hardest. It's irregular, it's everywhere, and — you have already met its root without knowing it.

The forms.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
j'ai, nous avons, blank.
tu as, vous avez, blank.
il/elle a, ils/elles ont, blank.
Say the singular column: ai, as, a — three forms, and they all sound the same. The written endings differ; your ear can't tell them apart. Only the pronoun tells you who.
Note j'ai, not je ai: before a vowel, je loses its e. That's the elision you already do in l'eau (Ch. 11).

The root you already own.
avoir from Latin habēre. And in Chapter 5 you learned habiter ("to live somewhere") from habitāre — which is just habēre run through a "keep doing it" pattern: to keep having a place. Same root, both verbs.
The family in English is large because English borrowed it whole:
[a table of 4 rows, read aloud]
English word: habit. inside it: what you have regularly.
English word: inhabit. inside it: to have a place — = habiter.
English word: exhibit. inside it: to hold out.
English word: prohibit. inside it: to hold back.

How far it wore down.
Latin habeō ("I have") becomes French j'ai. Four syllables of Latin reduced to a single vowel sound. French does this more than any of its sisters — compare Italian ho, Spanish he, and Chapter 11's aqua becomes eau.

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Give the six forms of avoir. (J'ai, tu as, il a, nous avons, vous avez, ils ont.) Which three sound identical? (Ai, as, a.) What root is avoir from? (Latin habēre.) Lesson 2 of 2.
j'ai vingt ans — having your years.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Ask a French speaker their age and the answer uses avoir. Not "I am twenty" — "I have twenty years." English can't do this; Spanish and Italian do exactly the same thing.

The phrase.
Quel âge as-tu? — "What age do you have?" J'ai vingt ans. — "I have twenty years."
Two things to notice:
The verb is avoir, never être. Je suis vingt ans is simply wrong.
ans is compulsory. English drops it ("I'm twenty"); French cannot.

an from annus.
an ("year") from Latin annus, which gives English annual, anniversary, annals, and per annum. You have already met its descendants: Chapter 9's month names came from the same calendar.
Careful with the sound: an is nasal, and in vingt ans the silent t of vingt wakes up and links — vin-t-an. That's liaison, French's habit of reviving a dead consonant when a vowel follows.

Who else does it this way.
[a table of 6 rows, read aloud]
language: French. "I am 20": j'ai vingt ans — have.
language: Spanish. "I am 20": tengo veinte años — have.
language: Italian. "I am 20": ho venti anni — have.
language: Portuguese. "I am 20": tenho vinte anos — have.
language: German. "I am 20": ich bin zwanzig Jahre alt — be.
language: English. "I am 20": I am twenty — be.
A clean split: Romance has its years; Germanic is its years. Age as something you carry versus age as something you are.

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Which verb does French use for age? (Avoir — never être.) What does j'ai vingt ans literally say? ("I have twenty years.") Can you drop ans? (No — French requires it.) Where does an come from? (Latin annus becomes annual, anniversary.) What happens to the t of vingt before ans? (It links — liaison.) Which languages side with English here? (German — and only German, of the five.)
