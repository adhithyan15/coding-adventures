Latin, chapter 322: Fuga.
6 lessons. All 6 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 6.
fuga, fugae — flight, running away.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for an uproar, then the Latin for a shout.

You'll want to know: fuga, fugae.
fuga, fugae — "flight, running away".
Hostēs sē in fugam dedērunt. The enemy took to flight.
Feminine, from fugiō (taught). In fugam dare: to put to flight.

The word, taken apart.
fuga belongs with fugiō, "to flee" (Lewis & Short), and matches Greek phygē, "flight". English fugitive comes from the same family.

Lesson 2 of 6.
effugiō, effugere — to escape.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a shout, then the Latin for flight.

You'll want to know: effugiō, effugere.
effugiō, effugere — "to escape".
Paucī ex incendiō effūgērunt. Few escaped from the fire.
effugiō, effugere, effūgī: ex + fugiō, also with the accusative: perīculum effugere.

The word, taken apart.
effugiō is built on fugiō, "to flee", with ex-, "out" (Lewis & Short): to flee out of danger.

Nauta ex undīs ad lītus ēvāsit. The sailor got out of the waves to the shore.
ēvādō, ēvādere, ēvāsī, ēvāsum: ē + vādō, 'to go'. English evade.

The word, taken apart.
ēvādō is built on vādō, "to go, to rush", with ex-, "out" (EtymDB). English evade comes from it.

Lesson 4 of 6.
pereō, perīre — to perish, to die; to be lost.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to escape, then the Latin for to get away.

You'll want to know: pereō, perīre.
pereō, perīre — "to perish, to die; to be lost".
In illō incendiō multī periērunt. Many died in that fire.
pereō, perīre, periī, peritum, like eō (taught). In comedy a slave cries periī!, 'I am done for!'

The word, taken apart.
pereō is built on eō, "to go", with per-, here "to destruction" (Lewis & Short): to go to ruin. English perish comes from it through French.

Lesson 5 of 6.
custōs, custōdis — a guard, a keeper.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to get away, then the Latin for to perish.

You'll want to know: custōs, custōdis.
custōs, custōdis — "a guard, a keeper".
Custōdēs portae dormiēbant, et fūr intrāvit. The guards of the gate were asleep, and the thief got in.
Masculine or feminine, 3rd declension. A satirist asked who would guard the guards themselves. English custody is of its family.

The word, taken apart.
custōs is linked by Lewis & Short with a root meaning "to cover, to hide"; the link is not certain. English custody comes from its family.
