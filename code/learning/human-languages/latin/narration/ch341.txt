Latin, chapter 341: Obsidiō.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
obsideō, obsidēre — to besiege; to sit at.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a breastplate, then the Latin for a round shield.

You'll want to know: obsideō, obsidēre.
obsideō, obsidēre — "to besiege; to sit at".
Hostēs oppidum trēs mēnsēs obsēdērunt. The enemy besieged the town for three months.
obsideō, obsidēre, obsēdī, obsessum: ob + sedeō (taught), to sit down in front of. English obsess.

The word, taken apart.
obsideō is built on sedeō, "to sit", with ob, "in front of" (Lewis & Short): to sit down before a town. English obsess comes from its participle.

obsidiō, obsidiōnis — "a siege".
Obsidiō longa erat, et cīvēs famē labōrābant. The siege was long, and the citizens were suffering from hunger.
Feminine, 3rd declension, from obsideō.

The word, taken apart.
obsidiō is built on obsideō, "to besiege" (EtymDB), with the ending -iō.

oppugnō, oppugnāre — "to attack, to assault".
Mīlitēs mūrōs oppidī oppugnābant. The soldiers were attacking the walls of the town.
oppugnō, oppugnāre, oppugnāvī, oppugnātum: ob + pugnō (taught). To attack, not yet to win.

The word, taken apart.
oppugnō is built on pugnō, "to fight", with ob-, "against" (EtymDB). English oppugn comes from it.

Lesson 5 of 5.
ariēs, arietis — a battering ram; a ram.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to attack, then the Latin for to take by storm.

You'll want to know: ariēs, arietis.
ariēs, arietis — "a battering ram; a ram".
Ariete portam frēgērunt. They broke the gate with a battering ram.
Masculine, 3rd declension. First the male sheep, then the beam with an iron ram's head that broke gates.

The word, taken apart.
ariēs is inherited; EtymDB traces it to a Proto-Indo-European name for a domestic animal, with cousins in Greek eriphos, "kid".
