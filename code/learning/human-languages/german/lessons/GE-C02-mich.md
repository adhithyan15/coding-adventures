# mich — "me," the same word as *ich*, doing a different job

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GE-LEX-ICH-02] -->

[PAUSE 2s] English does not say "gladdens I." It says "gladdens **me**."
German makes the same move, and the next phrase you learn needs it.

## You'll want to know: mich
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[GE-LEX-MICH-01]; assesses=[GE-SOUND-ICH-LAUT-01] -->

> **mich** — "me," the object form of *ich*.

Said *mikh*: the final **ch** is the soft ich-sound you already met in *ich*,
not the throat-scrape of *Nacht*.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[GE-ETYMON-MICH-02]; assesses=[GE-LEX-MICH-01, GE-LEX-ICH-02] -->

**mich** ("me," the object form) ← Proto-Germanic **\*mek**, from PIE **\*me-** —
the *same* first-person root as English **me**, **my**, **mine** (and the French
*me* in *je m'appelle*). So German *mich*, English *me*, and French *me* are
three cousins of one ancient word for "me." And just as English shifts **I**
(subject) → **me** (object), German shifts **ich** → **mich**.
