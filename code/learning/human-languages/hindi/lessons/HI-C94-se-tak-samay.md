# सुबह से शाम तक — the same frame, smaller units

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[HI-CONCEPT-C28-SUBAH-01, HI-CONCEPT-C29-SHAAM-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say *morning* and *evening*, the two Persian loanwords you met early
on. Then guess how a span between them is built.

## You'll want to know: सुबह से शाम तक
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[HI-LEX-C94-SPAN-03]; assesses=[] -->

**सुबह से शाम तक** — from morning until evening.

If you guessed, you guessed right. Nothing about the frame changed.

## The two words
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[HI-LEX-C94-SPAN-03, HI-LEX-C94-SPAN-02] -->

| what is spanned | Hindi |
|---|---|
| days | सोमवार से शुक्रवार तक |
| parts of a day | सुबह से शाम तक |
| clock hours | दस बजे से पाँच बजे तक |

Three different units, one frame. **से** and **तक** do not care what they are
attached to, and that is worth noticing because English does not always manage
it either — *from ten to five* is fine, but *from Monday until Friday* and
*until the house* pull on different senses of the same word.

## Grammar Lens: the clock row is two packages, not four words
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[HI-LEX-C94-SPAN-03, HI-LEX-C88-TIME-01] -->

**दस बजे से** is one package and **पाँच बजे तक** is another. Each already had
its own marker — **बजे** — before the span marker arrived, so the clock row
stacks **two** postposition-like endings on one side.

Read it inside out:

**दस** → **दस बजे** (*at ten*) → **दस बजे से** (*from ten*)

Nothing is being negotiated between them. Each step adds one word to the
right of what came before, which is the direction this language always builds
in.
