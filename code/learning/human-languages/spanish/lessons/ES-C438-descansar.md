# descansar — to un-tire yourself

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-CANSADO, ES-LEX-C438-CUERPO-03] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say **cansado**, the word for a body with nothing left in it. Say
**torcerse**. You have twisted your ankle and you are tired. Name what the
doctor is about to tell you to do.

## You'll want to know: descansar
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-C438-CUERPO-04]; assesses=[ES-LEX-CANSADO] -->

**descansar** — to rest.

*Tiene que descansar dos días* — you have to rest for two days. *Descanse* on a
sign at a viewpoint or a clinic is an invitation to sit. The related noun
**el descanso** is what a timetable calls a break, and what a football match
calls half-time.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C438-CUERPO-04, ES-ETYMON-CAMPSARE] -->

**des-** plus **cansar**, and you already own the second half.

*Cansado* came from Latin **campsāre**, which was a sailor's word: to turn a
ship round a headland. Turning the ship round, over and over, was the hard work
that wore a crew out, and the word slid from the manoeuvre to the exhaustion it
caused.

**des-** is the undoing prefix. Put it on **cansar** and you get the reversal:

| word | what it does to the tiredness |
|---|---|
| cansar | puts it there |
| descansar | takes it away |

## Why it's said this way
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C438-CUERPO-04] -->

English **rest** names a condition you are in. Spanish **descansar** names an
action you perform on a condition: you go in and remove the tiredness.

That is not a curiosity, it changes how the word behaves. Because it is a
reversal and not a state, it takes a duration the way any action does —
*descansé media hora*, I rested for half an hour — where English has to reach
for *had a rest* to make *rest* countable.

The prefix is productive, so the move is reusable. **des-** on a verb you
already know gives you its undoing, and you can often guess the result before
anyone teaches it to you.
