# la madera — the stuff a tree is for

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C310-ASK-03, ES-LEX-SILLA, ES-LEX-ARBOL] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say **la mesa**, **la silla** and **el árbol**. Two of those are made
of the third. Name what they are made of.

## You'll want to know: la madera
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-C444-CASA-02]; assesses=[ES-LEX-ARBOL] -->

**la madera** — wood, as a material.

*Una mesa de madera* — a wooden table. Spanish says **de madera**, of wood,
where English turns the noun into an adjective.

The tree is *el árbol*, the wood you build with is *la madera*, and a wood you
walk in is *el bosque* — three words where English stretches one.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C444-CASA-02] -->

**madera** is Latin **māteria**, and *māteria* meant **timber** before it meant
anything else.

Behind it sits **māter**, mother. A tree's *māteria* was its trunk — the part
that puts out the branches, the mothering stock. Only later did Roman
philosophers borrow the word for *the stuff a thing is made of* in general.

So the abstract sense came **second**. Matter was named after wood, not the
other way round.

## Why it's said this way
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C444-CASA-02, ES-LEX-C444-CASA-01] -->

That one Latin word reached Spanish **twice**, and this is the third pair of its
kind you have met.

| the word | the road it took |
|---|---|
| **la madera** | carried in every mouth, worn down |
| **la materia** | lifted off the page by scholars |

*Māteria* spoken for a thousand years became **madera** and kept the concrete
sense: the planks. *Māteria* read off the page centuries later came back as
**la materia** and kept the abstract one: matter, subject-matter, a school
subject.

You have seen the shape before — **nadar** beside **la natación**, **hacer**
beside **la factura**, and now **madera** beside **materia**. The worn twin is
the everyday one; the learned twin is the one on the timetable, the bill or the
syllabus. When two Spanish words look loosely alike and one is plainer than the
other, this is the first thing to suspect.
