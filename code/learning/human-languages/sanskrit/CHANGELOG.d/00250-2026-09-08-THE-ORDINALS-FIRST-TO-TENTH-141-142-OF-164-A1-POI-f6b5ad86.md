- **The chapter opens at FIFTH, and the reason is Sanskrit's own.** **-म** is the
  ending that generalizes: it makes 5, 7, 8, 9 and 10 with nothing else changing
  at all. A rule has to be visible before a departure from it can be READ as a
  departure, so chapter 62 teaches the block of five that share one ending —

- **Chapter 63 works OUTWARD from the hole rather than counting down**, and each
  step is licensed by the one before it:

  | ch. | order | why that order |
  |---|---|---|
  | 62 | **पञ्चमः सप्तमः अष्टमः नवमः दशमः** | the ending that generalizes, seen five times |
  | 63 | **षष्ठः चतुर्थः तृतीयः द्वितीयः प्रथमः** | the hole, then the pair it belongs to, then the pair before that, then the word that belongs to nothing |

  **षष्ठः** is a single odd word until **चतुर्थः** makes **-थ** a pair;
  **तृतीयः** is a third oddity until **द्वितीयः** makes **-तीय** a pair; and
  **प्रथमः** could only be recognised as a DIFFERENT KIND of word once nine
  words of the same kind had gone past. Numerical order would have taught all
  four groups in the first four lessons and named none of them.
- **The grouping is READ OFF A DICTIONARY, not asserted.** Macdonell's *A
  Practical Sanskrit Dictionary* prints the join inside every one of the ten
  headwords — *pañca-má*, *ṣaṣ-thá*, *dvi-tīya*, *pra-thamá* — and the
  chapter-63 payoff prints that table with his page numbers so a reader can
  check it. **प्रथमः** gets a note the other nine do not: *[= pra-tama, spv.
  foremost]*. It is not *one-th*; it is **प्र**, "forward", with a superlative
  ending, and there is no numeral in it. English did the identical thing:
  *first* is the superlative of *fore*.
- **One collision named rather than glossed over.** **नव** "new" (chapter 15)
  and the **नव** under **नवन्** "nine" (chapter 19) are two different words that
  wore down to the same shape. **नवमः** is built on the second. Latin has the
  same pair — *novus* beside *novem* — which is why the collision is inherited
  rather than a Sanskrit accident.
