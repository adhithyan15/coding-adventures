Lesson 2 of 7.
作文 (zuòwén) — essay; composition (at school).

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Mandarin for to play chess, then the Mandarin for measure word for essays.

You'll want to know: 作文.
作文 — zuòwén — "essay; composition (at school)".
wǒ zài xiě zuòwén — I'm writing an essay.
The new character, in pieces: 作 (zuò) — seven strokes: the person piece 亻; then a short stroke falling to the left, a bar, a long vertical, and two short bars to the right of it. The next lesson puts it in your hand.

The word, taken apart.
作文 (zuòwén) is built from 作 (zuò) 'to make' and 文 'writing', both inherited from Middle Chinese, tsak and mjun (Guangyun, in Baxter's transcription), which glosses the first 'to do' and 'to rise', and the second 'a piece of writing'.

做 你.
[once you have stopped driving — read: each one aloud, with nothing said first — zuò, nǐ]

[once you have stopped driving — this part needs your eyes or your hands; if you are driving, skip ahead to the next part]
Script — the person piece, then a post with two short bars.
作 (zuò).
Two pieces, left before right.
The left piece, 亻, two strokes, as in 你 and 做: a stroke falling to the left, then a vertical down to the foot.
The right piece, five strokes. A short stroke falling to the left at the top. A bar to the right from its middle. A long vertical down from the bar to the foot. Then two short bars against the right side of the vertical, the upper one first.
2 + 5 = seven.

Guided Practice.
[once you have stopped driving — write: 作 (zuò), all seven, with the model in front of you]
[once you have stopped driving — cover: write it again from memory, and count the pieces, not the strokes]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
How many strokes in 作? (Seven.) Which other character starts with the same piece and is said zuò? (做.) What are the last two strokes? (Two short bars on the right of the vertical.)

Lesson 4 of 7.
写作 (xiězuò) — to write essays and stories; writing.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Mandarin for measure word for essays, then the Mandarin for a school essay.

You'll want to know: 写作.
写作 — xiězuò — "to write essays and stories; writing".
我很喜欢写作 wǒ hěn xǐhuan xiězuò — I really enjoy writing.
The new character, in pieces: 写 (xiě) — five strokes: a cover of a dot and a bar that turns down; under it a short bar, a stroke that comes down, turns right, turns down again and hooks, and a long bar at the foot. The next lesson puts it in your hand.

The word, taken apart.
写作 (xiězuò) is built from 写 (xiě) 'to write' and 作 (zuò) 'to make', both inherited from Middle Chinese, sjaeX and tsak (Guangyun, in Baxter's transcription), which glosses the first, among other senses, 'to copy out a book'.

[once you have stopped driving — read: each one aloud, with nothing said first — xué, zì]

[once you have stopped driving — this part needs your eyes or your hands; if you are driving, skip ahead to the next part]
Script — a cover, then a turning hook.
写 (xiě).
Two pieces, top before bottom.
The cover, two strokes. A short dot on the left, then a bar that runs right and turns down, ending in a small hook: the cover of 学.
Under the cover, three strokes. A short bar in the middle. Then a stroke that starts right under the cover, comes down, turns right, turns down again and hooks to the left. Then the long bar across the foot.
2 + 3 = five.

[once you have stopped driving — cover: write it again from memory, and count the pieces, not the strokes]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
How many strokes in 写 (xiě)? (Five.) Where have you made the cover before? (On 学, under its dots.) What comes last? (The long bar at the foot.)

Lesson 6 of 7.
生字 (shēngzì) — new character; a character not yet learned.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Mandarin for a school essay, then the Mandarin for to write essays and stories.

You'll want to know: 生字.
生字 — shēngzì — "new character; a character not yet learned".
这些生字我都会写 zhèxiē shēngzì wǒ dōu huì xiě — I can write all these new characters.

The word, taken apart.
生字 (shēngzì) is built from 生 and 字 'a character', both inherited from Middle Chinese, srjaeng and dziH (Guangyun, in Baxter's transcription), which glosses the first 'to grow'. Its sense 'raw, not yet known' is not given there, so how it came is not settled.
