Hindi, chapter 100: How Much, How Well, How Big.
7 lessons. All 7 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 7.
थोड़ा (thoṛā) — the honest yes.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Somebody asks whether you speak Hindi. You can say yes and you can say no. Try to say a little, which is the true answer, and find you cannot.

You'll want to know: थोड़ा.
थोड़ा (thoṛā) — a little, a bit, some.
हाँ, थोड़ी (hā̃, thoṛī)। — Yes, a little. That two-word answer is a listening item on a practice paper.

The word, taken apart.
It ends in -आ, so it bends like every other word that does:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
what is being hedged: a masculine thing. the form: थोड़ा (thoṛā).
what is being hedged: a feminine thing. the form: थोड़ी.
That is why the paper's answer is थोड़ी rather than थोड़ा (thoṛā) — the question was about हिंदी, and hindī is feminine. The hedge agrees with what it hedges, not with the person speaking.
This catches English speakers out, because a little in English is a fixed lump that never changes. Here it is an adjective, and adjectives agree.

Grammar Lens: where it stands.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
मैं थोड़ी हिंदी बोलता हूँ। means I speak a little Hindi.
हाँ, थोड़ी (hā̃, thoṛī)। means Yes, a little.
In front of the noun, like any adjective. And on its own as a complete answer, where the noun it agrees with is the one the question named.
A hedge is worth more at this level than it looks. It is the difference between claiming fluency and being believed, and an examiner hears the difference.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: "थोड़ा (thoṛā)" and then "थोड़ी"]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: "मैं थोड़ी हिंदी बोलता हूँ"]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: "हाँ, थोड़ी (hā̃, thoṛī)" — and say why it is not थोड़ा (thoṛā)]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: the same answer as a woman would say the whole sentence]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Why is the paper's answer थोड़ी? (Hindī is feminine, and the hedge agrees with what it hedges.) Does English change a little the same way? (No — it is a fixed lump there.)


Lesson 2 of 7.
अच्छा / बुरा (acchā / burā) — well and badly, from words you already own.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
You have अच्छा for good and बुरा for bad. English needs two different words to say well and badly. Guess what Hindi needs.

You'll want to know: the manner use.
अच्छा is also well. बुरा is also badly.
वह अच्छा बोलता है। — He speaks well. वह बुरा बोलता है। — He speaks badly.

The word, taken apart.
English keeps two shelves of words — good / well, bad / badly — and puts -ly on the second shelf. Hindi keeps one. The same word describes a thing or describes an action, and its position in the sentence says which:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
where it stands: beside a noun. what it describes: the thing — a good book.
where it stands: in front of a verb. what it describes: the action — speaks well.
So he is good and he speaks well use the same word in Hindi, and an English speaker's instinct to reach for a second form is the thing to unlearn.
अच्छा is also where you can now read the letter छ, which arrived a moment ago in the alphabet. Find it in the middle of the word.

Grammar Lens: it still agrees.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
speaker: a man. sentence: वह अच्छा बोलता है।.
speaker: a woman. sentence: वह अच्छी बोलती है।.
Even doing the work of an English adverb, it is still an adjective, so it still bends. English adverbs never do, which is the second half of the same instinct to unlearn.
The ordinary spoken way to say well is also अच्छी तरह, "in a good manner" — useful to recognise, and the bare adjective is enough to produce.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: "वह अच्छा बोलता है" — he speaks well]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: "वह बुरा बोलता है" — he speaks badly]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: the same pair as a woman would say them]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — look: at अच्छा and put your finger on the letter छ]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
How many words does Hindi need for good and well? (One.) What tells you which job it is doing? (Where it stands.) Does it still bend for gender when it means well? (Yes — it is still an adjective.)


Lesson 3 of 7.
से बड़ा (se baṛā) — bigger, with no word for -er.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
You have बड़ा and छोटा. Try to say that one thing is bigger than another, and notice English changed the adjective to do it.

You'll want to know: से बड़ा.
आदमी बच्चे से बड़ा (se baṛā) है। — The man is bigger than the child.
The adjective does not change. The thing compared against takes से.

The word, taken apart.
English has two machines for this — -er on short adjectives and more in front of long ones — and both are extra grammar. Hindi has neither:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
English: bigger than X. Hindi: X से बड़ा (se baṛā).
English: smaller than X. Hindi: X से छोटा.
The postposition you learned for from and by is doing this too, and the logic holds: the comparison proceeds from the thing measured against. Measured from that book, this one is big.
So there is no comparative form to learn in this language. There is one more use of a word you already have.

Grammar Lens: word order, and the reason the note was wrong.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
slot: first. what goes in it: the thing being described.
slot: second. what goes in it: what it is measured against, plus से.
slot: third. what goes in it: the adjective, then the verb.
Note what बच्चे से is doing there: that is the oblique from the last chapter, earning its keep the very next time a postposition turns up.
English puts than X after the adjective; Hindi puts it before. That reversal is the only thing to drill, and it follows from the rule that has held since the first sentence you read: the verb goes last, and everything else queues in front of it.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: "आदमी बच्चे से बड़ा (se baṛā) है"]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: "बच्चा आदमी से छोटा है" — the same fact from the other end]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: what happens to the adjective in a Hindi comparison (nothing)]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: which side of the adjective the compared thing sits on]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
What marks the thing compared against? (से.) Does the adjective change? (No.) Where does the compared thing stand, against English? (Before the adjective rather than after it.)


Lesson 4 of 7.
कितना (kitnā) — a word let out of one sentence.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say the question you learned for asking somebody's age. One word in it means something on its own, and you have never used it anywhere else.

You'll want to know: कितना.
कितना (kitnā) — how much, how many.
कितनी किताबें? — How many books?

The word, taken apart.
You met it inside the age question, where it was one syllable of a memorised lump. It is a word, and it behaves like every -आ word in this language:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
what is being counted: a masculine thing. the form: कितना (kitnā).
what is being counted: a feminine thing, or a plural. the form: कितनी.
कितनी किताबें is feminine and plural, so the question word bends to match its noun — which is the same agreement you have now seen on adjectives, verbs, the past copula, the continuous and the hedge.
Hindi does not split how much from how many. English needs two phrases because it sorts nouns into countable and uncountable; Hindi asks one question and lets the noun answer.

Grammar Lens: the shape of a quantity question.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
question: कितनी किताबें? answer: दो किताबें।.
question: कितनी हिंदी? answer: थोड़ी।.
The second pair is the one a listening paper sets: a quantity question, and a hedge for an answer. Both words in that exchange bend to the same feminine noun, which is why they rhyme.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: "कितनी किताबें?" — how many books?]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: an answer with a number]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: "कितनी हिंदी?" and answer it with the hedge]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: the age question, and name the word inside it you now own]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
How many words does Hindi use for how much and how many? (One.) Why is it कितनी in front of किताबें? (The noun is feminine and plural.) Where had you already been saying it? (Inside the age question.)


Lesson 5 of 7.
कितना अच्छा! (kitnā acchā!) — a question that has stopped asking.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
You have कितना (kitnā) for how much and अच्छा for good. Put them together and listen to what the pair wants to be.

You'll want to know: कितना अच्छा!
कितना अच्छा! (kitnā acchā!) — How good! How lovely!
कितनी अच्छी किताब! — What a good book!

The word, taken apart.
Nothing has been added. This is the question word in front of an adjective, with the question taken out of it.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
what you say: कितना (kitnā) अच्छा है? what it is: how good is it?
what you say: कितना अच्छा! (kitnā acchā!) what it is: how good!
The difference is the verb and the voice. Drop है, let the pitch fall at the end, and a question becomes an exclamation. English does the identical thing — how good is it? against how good! — so there is nothing new to translate, only a habit to notice.

Grammar Lens: it still agrees, both words at once.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
what is being admired: a masculine thing. what you say: कितना अच्छा! (kitnā acchā!)
what is being admired: a feminine thing. what you say: कितनी अच्छी!
Both words move together, because both are -आ words and both are agreeing with the same noun. That is why the phrase rhymes with itself in either gender, and the rhyme is a useful check: if the two endings do not match, one of them is wrong.
This is the commonest way a Hindi speaker pays a compliment, and it costs you nothing you did not already have.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: "कितना अच्छा! (kitnā acchā!)" — how good!]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: "कितनी अच्छी किताब!" — what a good book!]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: the question form of the same phrase]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: what you take away to turn the question into the exclamation]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
What is removed to make the exclamation? (The verb, and the rising voice.) How many words bend in कितनी अच्छी? (Both.) What does the rhyme tell you? (That the two endings agree — if they do not match, one is wrong.)


Lesson 6 of 7.
mātrā kī tālikā — degree, on one page.

Warm-up.
[pause 3 seconds]
Name the word that hedges an answer and the word that asks a quantity, and say what the two have in common.

Grammar Lens: the five.
[a table of 5 rows, read aloud]
what you want to do: hedge an answer. what you say: थोड़ा (thoṛā) / थोड़ी.
what you want to do: describe how an action went. what you say: अच्छा / बुरा (acchā / burā).
what you want to do: measure one thing against another. what you say: X से बड़ा (se baṛā).
what you want to do: ask a quantity. what you say: कितना (kitnā) / कितनी.
what you want to do: admire something. what you say: कितना अच्छा! (kitnā acchā!)
Every one of the five ends in -आ and bends. That is the thread through the chapter, and it means none of them is a new kind of word — they are adjectives doing five different jobs.

Grammar Lens: what Hindi does without.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
English has: good and well. Hindi has: one word.
English has: how much and how many. Hindi has: one word.
English has: bigger, more interesting. Hindi has: the adjective, unchanged.
Three places where English carries extra machinery and Hindi carries none. A learner coming the other way has to build those distinctions; you get to drop them.

Grammar Lens: से, doing a third job.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
use: from. example: घर से.
use: by way of. example: बस से.
use: measured against. example: आदमी से बड़ा (se baṛā).
One postposition, three jobs, and the same idea under all three: the thing proceeds from the noun. A comparison starts at the thing you measure against.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: all five rows of the first table]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: "आदमी बच्चे से बड़ा (se baṛā) है"]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: "कितनी अच्छी किताब!"]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: the three jobs से now does]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
What do all five words in this chapter have in common? (They end in -आ and bend.) Does a Hindi adjective change to make a comparison? (No.) How many jobs does से now do? (Three.)


Lesson 7 of 7.
हाँ, थोड़ी (hā̃, thoṛī) — two words, and one of them is the test.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
You have हाँ for yes and the hedge from this chapter. Put them together and you have a complete answer.

The exchange.
— क्या आप हिंदी बोलती हैं? — हाँ, थोड़ी (hā̃, thoṛī)।.
That answer is a listening item on the first practice paper. Two words, and the second one carries the whole thing: a bare hā̃ would claim fluency, and thoṛī is what makes the answer true.
The ending is not decoration either. It is थोड़ी and not थोड़ा (thoṛā) because hindī is feminine — so a listener who hears the wrong ending hears a speaker who has not noticed what they are talking about.

How to answer.
Say each of these out loud:
yes, a little.
I speak a little Hindi.
he speaks well.
what a good book!
[pause 5 seconds]
Every one of the four ends in a word that had to agree with something. Say them again and listen for the ending each time rather than for the word.

Grammar Lens: the chapter in one sentence.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
what a paper asks: how much do you speak? what it is testing: the hedge, and its ending.
what a paper asks: how well does he speak? what it is testing: an adjective doing an adverb's job.
what a paper asks: which is bigger? what it is testing: से, and an unchanged adjective.
Three questions, and none of them needs a word you did not already half have. This chapter added five words and no new machinery.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: "हाँ, थोड़ी (hā̃, thoṛī)"]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: why the ending is -ई and not -आ]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: "वह अच्छा बोलता है"]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: "आदमी बच्चे से बड़ा (se baṛā) है"]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Which of the two words in the answer carries the meaning? (थोड़ी — hā̃ alone would overclaim.) Why that ending? (Hindī is feminine.) What marks the thing a comparison measures against? (से.)
