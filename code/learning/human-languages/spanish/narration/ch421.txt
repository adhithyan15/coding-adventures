Spanish, chapter 421: The Actor and the Actress.
4 lessons. All 4 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 4.
el actor — the one who does, which is all the word has ever meant.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
You can name el teatro and la película. Say both. Now say the one thing neither of them can do without, and that you have never been given a word for.

You'll want to know first.
el teatro — the looking-place, where this person works in front of you.
la película — the film, where this person works on a strip of plastic instead.

Sounds you'll need.
stress-final — ac-TOR, stress on the last syllable. English says AC-tor and Spanish says the opposite end. Moving it is the whole difference between sounding Spanish and sounding foreign here. see reference.
r-tap — the final r is one flick of the tongue, not the English curl.

The word, taken apart.
el actor — "the actor." Latin actor, from the verb agere, "to drive, to do, to set in motion."
Latin built agent nouns with -tor: take a verb, add -tor, and you get the one who does it. The pattern is still visible in dozens of English words that were borrowed whole:
[a table of 4 rows, read aloud]
Latin verb: agere. meaning: to do. the one who does it: actor.
Latin verb: docere. meaning: to teach. the one who does it: doctor.
Latin verb: dirigere. meaning: to direct. the one who does it: director.
Latin verb: inspicere. meaning: to look into. the one who does it: inspector.
So actor is not a theatre word at all. It is doer, and Latin used it for anyone who acted in any sense — an agent, a driver of cattle, a man pleading a case in court. The stage borrowed a perfectly ordinary word and kept it.
Agere is still visible all over English, and it never stopped meaning "do": an agent is someone doing something on your behalf, an agenda is literally things that must be done, and an action is a doing.

Grammar Lens: the two workplaces.
The same person, two places you already have words for:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
where: el teatro. what is watched: the actor. live?: yes.
where: el cine. what is watched: la película. live?: no.
El actor is masculine and takes el. That is the easy half. The other half — what to call a woman who does the same work — is the next lesson, and Spanish answers it with an ending that almost nothing else in the language uses.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: "el actor" — ac-TOR, stress at the end]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: "el actor del teatro" — the actor from the theatre]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: which English word meaning "things to be done" comes from the same Latin verb (agenda)]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
What did Latin agere mean? (To do, to drive.) So what does the -tor ending add? (The one who does it.) Where does the stress fall in actor in Spanish? (On the last syllable: ac-TOR.)


Lesson 2 of 4.
la actriz — the ending that did not follow the usual rule.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
You have el actor. Say it. Now guess the feminine before reading on, and expect to be wrong — the obvious guess is not what Spanish does here.

You'll want to know first.
el actor — the doer, from agere, with the Latin agent-noun ending -tor.

Sounds you'll need.
stress-final — ac-TRIZ, stress on the last syllable, like actor. see reference.
r-tap — the r in -triz is a single tap, and it sits between two consonants, which is where it is easiest to over-roll.

The word, taken apart.
la actriz — "the actress." Not la actora, which is what the ordinary Spanish habit of swapping -o for -a would have produced.
Latin had its own feminine for -tor nouns: -trix. Actor / actrix. Spanish inherited that pair intact, turned the Latin -x into -z, and kept it.
The ending is nearly extinct. Spanish has only a small handful of -triz words left, and they are worth seeing together because the list is short enough to hold in your head:
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
masculine: el actor. feminine: la actriz. meaning: the actor.
masculine: el emperador. feminine: la emperatriz. meaning: the emperor.
masculine: el institutor. feminine: la institutriz. meaning: the governess.
English kept the same Latin ending in aviatrix and dominatrix, and in the mathematical matrix — originally mater plus -trix, "the womb", the thing from which other things are produced.
Everywhere else in Spanish, the feminine of a person-noun is the ordinary swap: el camarero / la camarera. -triz is the exception, and actriz is the one you will meet most.

Grammar Lens: two words, one root, three endings.
Everything on this row came out of one Latin verb:
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
word: acto. ending: —. what the ending does: the deed itself.
word: el actor. ending: -or. what the ending does: the man who does it.
word: la actriz. ending: -riz. what the ending does: the woman who does it.
Strip the endings and the same three letters are left standing: act-. The root does the meaning; the ending only says who.
Note the article changes with the person, not with the building: el actor del teatro, la actriz del teatro. The theatre stays masculine either way.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: "la actriz" — ac-TRIZ, stress at the end]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: "el actor y la actriz" — and hear that only the article and the ending moved]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: "la actriz del teatro"]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: the feminine of emperador, using the same ending (emperatriz)]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Why is it not la actora? (Spanish inherited the Latin feminine actrix whole, rather than applying its own -o to -a swap.) Which English word for a grid of numbers carries the same ending? (Matrix.) Which syllable is stressed in both actor and actriz? (The last.)


Lesson 3 of 4.
la escena completa — two places, one thing, two people.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Name the two people from the last two lessons, with their articles, before reading further.

Grammar Lens: places, things and people take different endings.
Five words, and the gender of each is decided by a different kind of evidence:
[a table of 5 rows, read aloud]
word: el teatro. article: el. what decides it: the -o ending of a place-word.
word: el cine. article: el. what decides it: inherited with the shortened word.
word: la película. article: la. what decides it: the -a ending of a thing-word.
word: el actor. article: el. what decides it: the person is male.
word: la actriz. article: la. what decides it: the person is female.
That is the useful split. For a place or a thing, the ending tells you the article and the world has no say in it. For a person, the world decides and the ending follows.
So la película is feminine for a reason that has nothing to do with anyone being female, while la actriz is feminine for exactly that reason.

Reading.
Read this aloud. Every word in it has appeared before:
El actor está en el teatro. La actriz está en el cine. La película es de la actriz.
Three short sentences, five known words, no new grammar.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: the two places with el (el teatro, el cine)]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: the thing with la (la película)]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: the two people, each with its own article (el actor, la actriz)]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: which of the five is feminine because a person is female, and which is feminine only because of its ending]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Which two of the five change their article according to who is being talked about? (El actor and la actriz.) Which three never change? (El teatro, el cine, la película.)


Lesson 4 of 4.
¿Quién está en la película? — the question the two new words were for.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say el actor and la actriz once each. You are about to use both inside a question rather than on their own.

Grammar Lens: naming a person answers a different question.
Two question words you already have, pointed at the same film:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
question: ¿Qué es? what it asks for: a thing. answer from this chapter: Es una película.
question: ¿Quién está en la película? what it asks for: a person. answer from this chapter: El actor. / La actriz.
Qué wants the thing word. Quién wants the person word. Until this chapter you could answer only the first of the two about anything on a screen or a stage, because the corpus had named the film and never named anyone in it.

Reading.
— ¿Vamos al cine? — Sí. ¿Qué película es? — Es la película de la actriz. — ¿Y el actor? — El actor está en el teatro.
Note the inverted marks opening each question, and that de la actriz is how Spanish says the actress's.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: "¿Quién está en la película?" and then answer it with each of the two people in turn]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: "la película del actor" and "la película de la actriz", and hear where de + el contracts and where it cannot]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: where each of the two people is, using el teatro and el cine]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Which question word asks for a person rather than a thing? (Quién.) What happens to de el in la película del actor? (It contracts to del.) Why does de la actriz not contract? (The contraction exists only for de + el.)
