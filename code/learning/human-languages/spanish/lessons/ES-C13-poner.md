# poner — putting or placing with three singular forms

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-GRAMMAR-YO-GO-CONTRAST-LEARNED, ES-HISTORY-YO-GO-CONVERGENCE, ES-GRAMMAR-HACER-PRESENT-SINGULAR] -->

[PAUSE 2s] You already know **hago, haces, hace** and the careful comparison
**tengo · hago · digo**. Add one verb on its own evidence: **poner**, “to put”
or “to place.”

## Sounds you'll need
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[] -->

The **g** in **pongo** has the familiar hard sound. Tap the final **r** of
**poner** lightly; do not turn it into the stronger written **rr** sound.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-PONER, ES-ETYMON-PONERE]; assesses=[] -->

**poner** descends from Latin **pōnere**, “to put or place.” English keeps the
same placing family in **position**, **compose** (“place together”), and
**deposit** (“place down”). The two shapes **pon-** and **pos-** are historical
relatives, so *poner* and *position* are cousins even though their endings
differ.

## Grammar Lens: only the three singular forms
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-GRAMMAR-PONER-PRESENT-SINGULAR]; assesses=[ES-LEX-PONER, ES-GRAMMAR-YO-GO-CONTRAST-LEARNED, ES-ETYMON-PONERE] -->

| person | present of **poner** |
|---|---|
| yo | **pongo** |
| tú | **pones** |
| él / ella / usted | **pone** |

Say the bounded set as **pongo · pones · pone**. The learned **yo** form joins
the known comparison because it ends in **-go**. That resemblance helps memory;
it still does not predict an unseen verb.

For now, practise the forms and their meaning without importing a new object.
Later lessons can add the nouns needed for expressions such as setting a table
or putting on clothing. Plural persons and reflexive **ponerse** also wait.
