Latin, chapter 410: Passing a Law.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
rogātiō, rogātiōnis — a bill (put to the people); a request.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for lawful, then the Latin for allowed.

You'll want to know: rogātiō, rogātiōnis.
rogātiō, rogātiōnis — "a bill (put to the people); a request".
Tribūnus rogātiōnem ad populum tulit. The tribune put a bill to the people.
rogātiō, rogātiōnis f., built on rogō, to ask: the magistrate asked the people whether they wished it. If they voted yes, the bill became a lēx.

The word, taken apart.
rogātiō is the noun built on rogō "to ask" (EtymDB; Lewis & Short): the question put to the people. English rogation comes from it.

Lesson 2 of 5.
prōmulgō, prōmulgāre — to publish, to make known (a bill).

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for allowed, then the Latin for a bill.

You'll want to know: prōmulgō, prōmulgāre.
prōmulgō, prōmulgāre — "to publish, to make known (a bill)".
Lēgem novam prōmulgāvit. He published the new law.
prōmulgō, prōmulgāre, prōmulgāvī, prōmulgātum. A bill was posted for public view before the vote. English promulgate.

The word, taken apart.
The origin of prōmulgō is not settled: Lewis & Short call it unknown. English promulgate comes from it.

Lesson 3 of 5.
abrogō, abrogāre — to repeal, to abolish.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a bill, then the Latin for to publish.

You'll want to know: abrogō, abrogāre.
abrogō, abrogāre — "to repeal, to abolish".
Populus lēgem veterem abrogāvit. The people repealed the old law.
abrogō, abrogāre, abrogāvī, abrogātum, ab + rogō: to ask the people to take a law away. English abrogate.

The word, taken apart.
abrogō is built from ab "away" and rogō "to ask" (EtymDB; Lewis & Short): to ask the people to take a law away. English abrogate comes from it.

Lesson 4 of 5.
sanciō, sancīre — to make sacred and binding; to ratify.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to publish, then the Latin for to repeal.

You'll want to know: sanciō, sancīre.
sanciō, sancīre — "to make sacred and binding; to ratify".
Lēgem iūre iūrandō sānxērunt. They made the law binding by an oath.
sanciō, sancīre, sānxī, sānctum: to make a thing sacred and binding. Sānctus, holy, is its participle. English sanction and saint.

The word, taken apart.
sanciō is inherited from a Proto-Indo-European root meaning "to make holy" (EtymDB), and sānctus "holy" is its participle (Lewis & Short). English sanction and saint come from it.
