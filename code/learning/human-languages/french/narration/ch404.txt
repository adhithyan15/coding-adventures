French, chapter 404: Un pneu à plat.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
la crevaison — the puncture, the flat tyre.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the French for the tank, then the French for to start up.

You'll want to know: la crevaison.
la crevaison — "the puncture, the flat tyre".
Nous avons eu une crevaison sur la petite route. We had a puncture on the little road.
Réparer une crevaison — to mend a puncture.

The word, taken apart.
crevaison is built on crever 'to burst' with the ending -aison (EtymDB). Crever is inherited from Latin crepāre 'to crack, to burst'.

What you've built.
One more word for telling, explaining and describing at B1.

Lesson 3 of 5.
le pneu — the tyre.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the French for the puncture, then the French for to get a flat tyre.

You'll want to know: le pneu.
le pneu — "the tyre".
Le pneu avant est à plat. The front tyre is flat.
Plural les pneus. Un pneu neige — a winter tyre.

The word, taken apart.
pneu is short for pneumatique 'filled with air', a learned word from Latin pneumaticus, from Greek pneûma 'breath, air' (Lewis & Short).

Lesson 4 of 5.
le cric — the jack (for lifting a car).

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the French for to get a flat tyre, then the French for the tyre.

You'll want to know: le cric.
le cric — "the jack (for lifting a car)".
Le cric est sous le tapis, à l'arrière de la voiture. The jack is under the mat at the back of the car.
The final c is pronounced.

The word, taken apart.
The origin of cric is not settled. It is usually traced to Middle High German kriec 'winch'; EtymDB does not list it.

Lesson 5 of 5.
gonfler — to pump up, to inflate; to swell.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the French for the tyre, then the French for the jack.

You'll want to know: gonfler.
gonfler — "to pump up, to inflate; to swell".
Au garage, il gonfle les pneus. At the garage he pumps up the tyres.
Informal: ça me gonfle — that gets on my nerves.

The word, taken apart.
gonfler goes back to Latin conflāre 'to blow together', from flāre 'to blow' (EtymDB), probably through a southern dialect.
