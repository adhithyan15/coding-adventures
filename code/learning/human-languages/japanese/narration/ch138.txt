You'll want to know: おきます.
おきます — okimasu — "I get up", or "she gets up", "we get up": Japanese leaves out who does it when that is clear. It says what someone does, as a habit or as a plan.
Every verb in this chapter ends in ます, masu. That ending makes the polite present, the form you use with anyone you do not know well. A word list gives the dictionary form instead, here おきる, okiru. To make the polite form of a verb in -iru or -eru like this one, take off the last る and add ます: oki-ru becomes oki-masu.
まいあさ おきます。 — maiasa okimasu — "I get up every morning."

You'll want to know: はたらきます.
はたらきます — hatarakimasu — "I work": to do a job, at a desk, in a shop or in a field. The noun for the job itself is しごと, which you already know.
Its dictionary form is はたらく, hataraku. Verbs that end in -u like this one change that last sound to -i before ます: hatarak-u becomes hatarak-i-masu. So く turns into き.
ぎんこうで はたらきます。 — ginkō de hatarakimasu — "I work at a bank." The particle で marks the place where something is done.

The dictionary form is あるく, aruku, and it works like はたらく: the last く becomes き, and ます follows. aruk-u becomes aruk-i-masu.

You'll want to know: かえります.
かえります — kaerimasu — "I go home", "I go back", to the place you belong to: your home, your town, your country.
Its dictionary form is かえる, kaeru. It ends in -eru, but it follows the -u pattern of あるく, not the pattern of おきる: the last る becomes り, and ます follows. So it is kaer-i-masu, never kaemasu. A few common verbs in -eru and -iru work this way, and a word list marks them.
うちに かえります。 — uchi ni kaerimasu — "I go home." The particle に marks where you are going.

You'll want to know: ねます.
ねます — nemasu — "I go to bed", "I sleep".
Its dictionary form is ねる, neru, and it works like おきる: take off る and add ます. ne-ru becomes ne-masu.
いま ねます。 — ima nemasu — "I am going to bed now."
Now you have a whole day, and the two ways to find the stem:
