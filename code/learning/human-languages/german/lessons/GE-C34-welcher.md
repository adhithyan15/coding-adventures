# welcher — "which," and the two pieces inside it

## You'll want to know: welcher
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[GE-LEX-WELCHER-01]; assesses=[GE-LEX-DER-DIE-DAS-02, GE-LEX-HUND-02, GE-LEX-KATZE-04, GE-LEX-BROT-01] -->

> **welcher** — "which", standing in front of a noun.

The ending copies the article the noun already takes, so you have nothing new to
learn once you know its gender: **welcher** for a *der*-word, **welche** for a
*die*-word, **welches** for a *das*-word.

> **Welcher Hund?** · **Welche Katze?** · **Welches Brot?**

Said *VEL-cher*, with the soft ich-sound of *ich* and *nicht* in the middle.

## The word, taken apart: what-like
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GE-LEX-WELCHER-01, GE-LEX-WAS-01, GE-ETYMON-WER-WHO-02] -->

**welcher** is two ancient pieces: ***hwa-*** — the *what* you already meet at
the front of every W-word — and ***-lîk***, "like, bodily form", the ancestor of
English **-ly** and of the word **like** itself. *What-like.*

English built its own word out of the very same two: *hwā* + *līc* gave
**hwilc**, which wore down to **which**. So both languages ask "which one" by
asking "what kind", and the shared seam is still visible if you set *wel-cher*
beside *wh-ich*.
