Latin, chapter 333: Diēs fēstus.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
fēstus, fēsta, fēstum — festive, holiday.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to seal, then the Latin for to leave behind.

You'll want to know: fēstus, fēsta, fēstum.
fēstus, fēsta, fēstum — "festive, holiday".
Diē fēstō nēmō in forō labōrābat. On the holiday nobody was working in the forum.
Diēs fēstus: a holiday, the opposite of a working day. English feast and festival come from it.

The word, taken apart.
fēstus belongs, says Lewis & Short, with fēriae, "holidays" (taught). English feast comes from it through French, and festival from its family.

Lesson 2 of 5.
sollemnis, sollemne — yearly, solemn; customary.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to leave behind, then the Latin for festive.

You'll want to know: sollemnis, sollemne.
sollemnis, sollemne — "yearly, solemn; customary".
Sacrificium sollemne quotannīs fīēbat. A solemn sacrifice was held every year.
Two endings. A rite that comes round every year, and so solemn and customary. English solemn.

The word, taken apart.
sollemnis is explained by Lewis & Short as sollus, "whole", and annus, "year": what happens every year; EtymDB derives it from sollus. English solemn comes from it through French.

Lesson 3 of 5.
pompa, pompae — a procession.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for festive, then the Latin for yearly.

You'll want to know: pompa, pompae.
pompa, pompae — "a procession".
Longa pompa per viās urbis prōcessit. A long procession moved through the streets of the city.
Feminine. Games, triumphs, weddings and funerals all had their pompa. English pomp.

The word, taken apart.
pompa was borrowed from Greek pompē, "a sending, a procession", from pempō, "to send" (EtymDB). English pomp comes from it through French.

Lesson 4 of 5.
epulae, epulārum — a feast, a banquet.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for yearly, then the Latin for a procession.

You'll want to know: epulae, epulārum.
epulae, epulārum — "a feast, a banquet".
Post sacrificium epulae magnae parātae sunt. After the sacrifice a great feast was prepared.
Feminine, plural; the singular is the neuter epulum, a public banquet.

The word, taken apart.
epulae is the plural that goes with epulum, "a banquet"; Lewis & Short call its origin doubtful and suggest, with hesitation, a link with edō, "to eat".

Lesson 5 of 5.
epulor, epulārī — to feast, to banquet.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a procession, then the Latin for a feast.

You'll want to know: epulor, epulārī.
epulor, epulārī — "to feast, to banquet".
Tōtam noctem amīcī epulābantur. The friends feasted all night long.
epulor, epulārī, epulātus sum, a deponent.

The word, taken apart.
epulor is built on epulum, "a banquet" (Lewis & Short): to hold a feast.
