Arabic, chapter 41: Calling Somebody, and Asking Again.
3 lessons. You can do the first 1 of them in the car; after that you will want to have stopped.


Lesson 1 of 3.
يا (yā) — calling somebody.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Chapter two taught you to give your name and to ask for somebody else's. Forty chapters later you still cannot call them by it.
[your turn — recall: the time joiner from the lesson before — ḥīnamā]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

You'll want to know: يا.
يا حسن — yā Ḥasan — Hasan! (calling him).
يا جار — yā jār — neighbour!
Arabic does not address a person with a bare name. حسن on its own is a statement about Hasan; يا (yā) حسن is speaking to him. The particle is not optional politeness — it is how the language marks the difference between naming somebody and calling them.
Two letters, both long since yours, and no ending changes on the name.

The word, taken apart.
يا (yā) is pan-Semitic and English has no particle of its own to match it. The one place English reaches for something similar is old translation — O Lord, O Israel — where the O is standing in for exactly this. It sounds archaic in English and is entirely everyday in Arabic.
It goes before a title or a kin term as readily as before a name: يا ابن, يا بنت, yā ṣadīqī.

[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: yā jār … yā ṣadīqī]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: the name alone, then called — Ḥasan … yā Ḥasan]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
What is the difference between حسن and يا (yā) حسن? (One names him; the other calls him.) Does the name change shape after it? (No.) And what does English use where Arabic uses يا (yā)? (Nothing, ordinarily — only O in old translation.)

Lesson 2 of 3.
كرّر من فضلك (karrir min faḍlik) — say it again, please.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, because the lesson points at something written down. You can listen to all of it now and come back to the parts that need looking at once you have stopped.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Chapter thirty-seven gave you the first thing a lost learner says. Saying it only helps if you can also ask for the sentence back.
[your turn — recall: the particle from the lesson before, and what it turns a name into — yā]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

You'll want to know: كرّر.
كرّر — karrir — repeat.
كرّر من فضلك — karrir min faḍlik — say it again, please.
The please is not new: chapter six taught it. What is new is the verb, and the shape it comes in — this is the first command in the book, and Arabic builds one by stripping the present tense back to its bare stem.
Look at the shadda. كرّر doubles its middle letter, which is the same Form II shape as fakkara in chapter thirty-two — a pattern that means do it thoroughly, do it repeatedly. On the root ك-ر-ر, "to return", that gives exactly do it again.

Grammar Lens: the repair kit, whole.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
when: you did not follow. say: lā afham.
when: you want it again. say: karrir min faḍlik.
when: you are grateful for it. say: shukran.
The inventory that measured this track said the repair kit was strong with one hole in it: sorry, excuse me, pardon, please and thank you were all taught, and I do not understand and say it again were not. Those two are what chapters thirty-seven and forty-one add, and between them they needed one new verb.

[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: the whole kit in order — lā afham … karrir min faḍlik … shukran]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: and get their attention first — yā Ḥasan, karrir min faḍlik]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Which chapter taught the please in this sentence? (Six.) Which earlier verb wears the same doubled shape? (fakkara, chapter thirty-two.) And what does that shape add to a root? (Doing it thoroughly, or again.)
