Latin, chapter 329: Mors.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
mors, mortis — death.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a married woman, then the Latin for a divorce.

You'll want to know: mors, mortis.
mors, mortis — "death".
Mors avī tōtam familiam trīstem fēcit. Grandfather's death made the whole family sad.
Feminine, 3rd declension, from the root of morior (taught). English mortal comes from its family.

The word, taken apart.
mors is inherited from Proto-Indo-European mértis, "death" (EtymDB). English mortal comes from its family, as does mortgage, a dead pledge, through French.

Lesson 2 of 5.
mortuus, mortua, mortuum — dead.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a divorce, then the Latin for death.

You'll want to know: mortuus, mortua, mortuum.
mortuus, mortua, mortuum — "dead".
Canis senex in angulō mortuus iacēbat. The old dog lay dead in the corner.
The past participle of morior: mortuus est, he died. As a noun, mortuus is a dead man.

The word, taken apart.
mortuus is the participle of morior, "to die" (EtymDB), and goes back to Proto-Italic mortwos, "dead"; Greek brotos, "mortal", is a cousin.

Lesson 3 of 5.
exspīrō, exspīrāre — to breathe one's last.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for death, then the Latin for dead.

You'll want to know: exspīrō, exspīrāre.
exspīrō, exspīrāre — "to breathe one's last".
Senex in lectō exspīrāvit. The old man breathed his last in bed.
exspīrō, exspīrāre, exspīrāvī, exspīrātum: ex + spīrō (taught), to breathe out. English expire.

The word, taken apart.
exspīrō is built on spīrō, "to breathe", with ex-, "out" (EtymDB): to breathe out the last breath. English expire comes from it.

Lesson 4 of 5.
obeō, obīre — to die; to go to meet.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for dead, then the Latin for to breathe one's last.

You'll want to know: obeō, obīre.
obeō, obīre — "to die; to go to meet".
Avia mea placidē in somnō obiit. My grandmother died peacefully in her sleep.
obeō, obīre, obiī, obitum, like eō. Mortem obīre: to meet death; alone, obiit means 'he died'. English obituary.

The word, taken apart.
obeō is built on eō, "to go", with ob, "towards" (EtymDB): to go to meet, and so to meet one's end. English obituary comes from its noun obitus, "death".

Lesson 5 of 5.
dēcēdō, dēcēdere — to depart; to die.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to breathe one's last, then the Latin for to die.

You'll want to know: dēcēdō, dēcēdere.
dēcēdō, dēcēdere — "to depart; to die".
Pater meus herī dēcessit. My father passed away yesterday.
dēcēdō, dēcēdere, dēcessī, dēcessum: to go away, from a province or from life. English decease.

The word, taken apart.
dēcēdō is built on cēdō, "to go, to withdraw", with dē, "away" (Lewis & Short). English decease comes from its noun through French.
