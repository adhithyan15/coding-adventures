Mandarin Chinese, chapter 124: Tall and Handsome.
5 lessons. You can do the first 3 of them in the car; after that you will want to have stopped.


Lesson 1 of 5.
个子 (gèzi) — height; build (of a person).

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Mandarin for warm-hearted, then the Mandarin for to care about.

You'll want to know: 个子.
个子 — gèzi — "height; build (of a person)".
wǒ gēge gèzi hěn gāo — My brother is tall.

The word, taken apart.
个子 (gèzi) is built from 个, the measure word, and the noun ending 子, both inherited from Middle Chinese, kaH and tsiX (Guangyun, in Baxter's transcription). How the pair came to mean a person's height is not settled.

What you've built.
One more word for describing things.

Lesson 2 of 5.
tóufa — hair (on the head).

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Mandarin for to care about, then the Mandarin for a person's height.

You'll want to know: tóufa.
tóufa — "hair (on the head)".
tā de tóufa hěn cháng — Her hair is long.
Its characters come later; for now it is a word for the ear.

The word, taken apart.
tóufa is built from tóu 'head' and fa, both inherited from Middle Chinese, duw and pjot (Guangyun, in Baxter's transcription), which glosses the second 'the hair of the head'. In the word it loses its tone.

Its characters come later; for now it is a word for the ear.

The word, taken apart.
shuài is inherited from Middle Chinese srwijH (Guangyun, in Baxter's transcription), glossed 'a commander of troops'. The sense handsome is not given there, and how it came is not settled.

Lesson 4 of 5.
nánkàn — ugly.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, because the lesson points at something written down. You can listen to all of it now and come back to the parts that need looking at once you have stopped.

You'll want to know: nánkàn.
nánkàn — "ugly".
wǒ de xié hěn nánkàn — My shoes are ugly.
Its characters come later; for now it is a word for the ear.

The word, taken apart.
nánkàn is built from nán 'hard' and 看 'to look at', both inherited from Middle Chinese, nan and khanH (Guangyun, in Baxter's transcription): hard to look at.
