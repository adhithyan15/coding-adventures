Lesson 1 of 5.
latus, lateris — side, flank.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for sweat, then the Latin for a beat.

You'll want to know: latus, lateris.
latus, lateris — "side, flank".
Dolor in latere sinistrō est. The pain is in the left side.
Neuter, third declension. Not the adjective lātus — wide. English lateral.

The word, taken apart.
latus is a native Latin noun; Lewis & Short compares Greek platus "broad", but the origin is not settled. English lateral comes from it.

Lesson 2 of 5.
coxa, coxae — hip.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a beat, then the Latin for side.

You'll want to know: coxa, coxae.
coxa, coxae — "hip".
Senex in coxā dolōrem sentit. The old man feels pain in his hip.
Feminine, first declension. French cuisse and Italian coscia — thigh — come from it.

The word, taken apart.
coxa is inherited from a Proto-Indo-European word for "hip, joint", and Old Irish cos "foot, leg" is a cousin (EtymDB). French cuisse "thigh" comes from it.

Lesson 3 of 5.
calx, calcis — heel.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for side, then the Latin for hip.

You'll want to know: calx, calcis.
calx, calcis — "heel".
Calx mea post longum iter dolet. My heel hurts after the long journey.
Feminine, third declension. Another calx means lime or chalk. English recalcitrant, kicking back, comes from this one.

The word, taken apart.
calx "heel" is a native Latin noun with no settled origin; Lewis & Short keeps it apart from calx "lime". English recalcitrant comes from it, through recalcitrō "to kick back".

Lesson 4 of 5.
planta, plantae — sole of the foot; a sprout.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for hip, then the Latin for heel.

You'll want to know: planta, plantae.
planta, plantae — "sole of the foot; a sprout".
Puer nūdīs plantīs in harēnā ambulat. The boy walks on the sand with bare soles.
Feminine, first declension. Both the sole of the foot and a young plant. English plant.

The word, taken apart.
planta is inherited from a Proto-Indo-European word for "sole of the foot", built on a root meaning "flat" (EtymDB). English plant comes from its second sense, a sprout.

Lesson 5 of 5.
umbilīcus, umbilīcī — navel; the knob of a book-roll.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for heel, then the Latin for sole of the foot.

You'll want to know: umbilīcus, umbilīcī.
umbilīcus, umbilīcī — "navel; the knob of a book-roll".
Īnfāns parvum umbilīcum habet. The baby has a little navel.
Masculine, second declension. Also the knob on the rod of a book-roll, so ad umbilīcum addūcere — to finish a book. English umbilical.

The word, taken apart.
umbilīcus is inherited from a Proto-Indo-European word for "navel", and Greek omphalos is a cousin (EtymDB; Lewis & Short). English umbilical comes from it, and navel is a distant Germanic cousin.
