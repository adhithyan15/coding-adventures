# Synthesis — what you like, and who is doing the liking

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-GUSTAR-07, ES-GRAMMAR-GUSTAR-INVERSION-09] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say "I like coffee" in Spanish. Then answer this: in that sentence,
which word is the **subject**?

## Grammar Lens: the sentence that will not let you be the subject
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-GRAMMAR-GUSTAR-INVERSION-09, ES-LEX-GUSTAR-07, ES-LEX-CAFE] -->

**Me gusta el café.** The coffee is the subject. The coffee does the pleasing.
You are the one it happens *to*.

English puts you in charge: *I like coffee* — I am the subject, the coffee just
sits there. Spanish reverses it.

> Two chapters ago, all three verbs were about **the one doing it**. That is
> why this chapter had to sit next to them.

Translate *I like* word by word and you build a sentence Spanish does not
have — which is why this is a chapter about saying an idea, not a word.

## The exchange
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-GUSTAR-07, ES-LEX-CAFE, ES-LEX-GUSTO, ES-GRAMMAR-GUSTAR-INVERSION-09] -->

> — ¿Te gusta el café?
> — Sí, **me gusta** mucho. ¿Y a ti?
> — A mí también. **Mucho gusto.**

That last line is a joke you can now get. *Mucho gusto* — the phrase you
learned long ago for "pleased to meet you" — is the **same word**. It has
always meant *much pleasure*. You have been saying *gustar*'s noun since your
third conversation in this book.

## What you've built
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-ETYMON-GUSTAR-08, ES-LEX-GUSTO, ES-LEX-TOMAR-01, ES-LEX-AYUDAR-05] -->

*Gustar* is Latin *gustāre*, **to taste** — English *gusto*, *disgust*.
Liking, in Spanish, started at the tongue.
