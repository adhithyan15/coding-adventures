# el cargo — what has been put on the bill

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C444-CASA-05, ES-LEX-C311-ASK-07] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say **el carrito** and **la cuenta**. A cart carries a load, and so
does a bill.

## You'll want to know: el cargo
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-C456-CUENTA-02]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C311-ASK-07] -->

**el cargo** — the charge: an amount put onto a bill or an account.

*Hay un cargo de doce euros* — there's a twelve-euro charge. *Un cargo por
servicio* — a service charge. *Sin cargo* — free of charge, the wording on a
receipt.

**Cargar** is the verb — *me han cargado dos veces*, they've charged me twice
— and it also means to load, and to charge a phone.

## Grammar Lens: cargo is not precio
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C456-CUENTA-02, ES-LEX-C456-CUENTA-01] -->

| what you mean | the word |
|---|---|
| what a thing costs | **el precio** |
| an amount added to your bill | **el cargo** |

*El precio de la ensalada* is on the menu; *un cargo en la cuenta* is
something a business has placed there, and you may be querying it. That is why
*cargo* turns up on disputes and *precio* does not: a *precio* is announced in
advance, a *cargo* appears afterwards.

*El cargo* has a third life as a post or office — *el cargo de director* — for
the same reason: a job is a load somebody carries.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C456-CUENTA-02, ES-LEX-C444-CASA-05] -->

Latin **carricare**, to load a cart, built on **carrus** — the cart itself,
which Spanish kept as *el carro* and shrank into **el carrito**.

So a charge on your bill is a **load put onto a cart**, and the picture holds
in every branch of the family: **la carga** is a load, **el cargamento** is
cargo, and **descargar** is to unload — or to download, which is unloading
onto your machine.

English has the same *carricare* twice, by two different doors: **charge**
came through French, and **cargo** was taken straight from Spanish in the
seventeenth century. Two English words, one Latin verb, and one of them is
this very Spanish noun.
