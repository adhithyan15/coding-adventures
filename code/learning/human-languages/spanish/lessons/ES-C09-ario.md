## Grammar Lens: belonging to, or the place for
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-FRIEND-ARIO-ARY, ES-ETYMON-ARIUS]; assesses=[] -->

> **Spanish *-ario* is English *-ary*.**

Latin **-ārius** meant *belonging to* — and then, by an easy step, *the place
where that thing is kept*.

> *diccionario* — the place the **words** are kept

Which is exactly what an English **dictionary** is, and neither language ever
said so out loud.

| Spanish | English |
|---|---|
| *necesario* | **necessary** |
| *ordinario* | **ordinary** |
| *primario* | **primary** |
| *diccionario* | **dictionary** |
| *aniversario* | **anniversary** |

## Grammar Lens: it does two jobs
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-GRAMMAR-NOUN-GENDER, ES-MORPH-BUENO-BUENA] -->

Unlike the other seven, *-ario* makes **both** nouns and adjectives, and the
sentence tells you which:

| | |
|---|---|
| *un diccionario* | a dictionary — a **thing** |
| *es necesario* | it is necessary — a **quality** |

When it is an adjective it ends in *-o*, so it shifts the way you already
expect: *necesario* / *necesaria*. When it is a noun it is masculine, and takes
*el* or *un*.

[PAUSE 2s] You do not have to decide in advance. The sentence decides.
