Lesson 5 of 10.
एक, दोन, तीन, चार, पाच — one to five.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Marathi and Hindi both use Devanagari and both descend from Sanskrit, so their numbers look almost identical. Almost is where the interest is.

You'll want to know: the five.
[a table of 5 rows, read aloud]
1. Marathi: एक. said: ek.
2. Marathi: दोन. said: don.
3. Marathi: तीन. said: tīn.
4. Marathi: चार. said: chār — but see below.
5. Marathi: पाच. said: pāch.

What you've built: three Marathi tells.
Set them against Hindi. Two differences are visible and one is audible:
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
number: 2. Hindi: दो do. Marathi: दोन don: final n.
number: 5. Hindi: पाँच pāṁch. Marathi: पाच pāch: no nasal mark.
number: 4. Hindi: चार chār. Marathi: चार tsār: same spelling, different sound.
Do not explain all three histories yet. First make the five forms automatic; the next lesson earns the deeper comparison.

Lesson 6 of 10.
Why Marathi two rhymes with three — why Marathi two rhymes with three, and why Hindi preserves a different piece of five.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Count once: ek, don, tīn, tsār, pāch. Which two words end in n? (Don and tīn.)

The word, taken apart — दोन borrowed from “three”.
The tempting guess is that Marathi दोन don kept something Hindi दो do lost. It did not.
Sanskrit's neuter word for three, त्रीणि trī́ṇi, became Prakrit tiṇṇi. In three, the ṇ genuinely belongs to the word. The doubled consonant is a Prakrit trade for the lost r.
The word for two was then reshaped to match its neighbour: Prakrit doṇṇi copied the shape of tiṇṇi. Marathi don's final n is therefore an innovation, a rhyme borrowed from the number next door. Hindi do simply never took it.
English numbers did this too: four acquired its initial f- by analogy with five. Familiar sequences pull neighbouring words toward one another.

What you've built: पाच and the nasal Hindi kept.
Marathi पाच pāch and Hindi पाँच pāṁch tell the opposite story. Hindi's chandrabindu ँ records the nasal of Sanskrit pañca; Marathi's spelling drops it.
So neither language is simply “older.” Marathi innovated in don, while Hindi retained an older feature in pāṁch. Comparing both descendants reveals the history that either one alone could hide.

Sounds you'll need: चार and the change its spelling hides.
Both write चार, but Marathi pronounces च before ā nearer ts: tsār. The letters stayed aligned while the sound moved.
