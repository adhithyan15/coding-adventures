## You'll want to know: avisar
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-C470-PARAGUAS-04]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C332-WORD-22] -->

**avisar** — to let know, to notify.

> Si lo habéis visto, **avisadme**, por favor.

You have met this verb once already without being given it.
[El aviso](./ES-C332-aviso.md) named it in passing — *"behind it is the verb
avisar, to notify"* — and then went back to the noun. Here it is as yours.

*Avísame* — let me know. *Avisar a alguien* — to tell somebody. *Avisar de
algo* — to give notice of something.

## Grammar Lens: it shows, it does not order
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C470-PARAGUAS-04, ES-LEX-DEJAR] -->

*El aviso* made a point that the verb inherits whole: an *aviso* is not an
instruction but a **making-visible**. Somebody puts a thing where you can see
it, and what you do next is yours.

So *avisar* is the weakest of the telling verbs, and that is its use.

| you want | you say |
|---|---|
| somebody to know, no more | **avisar** |
| somebody to do it | *decir* that they must |

*Avísame si lo encuentras* asks for information and nothing else. It is what
you say about a paraguas you left behind: you are not giving an order to a
neighbour, you are asking to be shown something.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C470-PARAGUAS-04] -->

*El aviso* already traced this: Latin **ad vīsum**, toward what has been seen,
on **vidēre**, to see. There is nothing to add to the derivation, so take
instead the thing it predicts.

Every relative of *avisar* is about **seeing**, not speaking:

- English **advise** is the same formation — to put something in somebody's
  view;
- **advice** is what you were shown;
- **evident**, **vision**, **provide**, **survey** are all seeings.

A word for telling, built entirely out of sight. That is why *avisar* feels
lighter than it looks: nobody in it is talking.
