6 lessons. All 6 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 6.
écouler — to go by (as s'écouler, of time); to sell off (goods).

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the French for to precede, then the French for to last.

You'll want to know: écouler.
écouler — "to go by (as s'écouler, of time); to sell off (goods)".
Trois ans se sont écoulés depuis son départ. Three years have gone by since he left.
Mostly used as s'écouler: time goes by, and water flows out (l'eau s'écoule). Without se, it means to sell off goods: écouler un stock.

The word, taken apart.
écouler is built on couler "to flow", which is inherited from Latin cōlāre "to strain, to filter" (Lewis & Short).

Lesson 2 of 6.
ajourner — to postpone, to adjourn.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the French for to last, then the French for to go by.

You'll want to know: ajourner.
ajourner — "to postpone, to adjourn".
Le match a été ajourné parce qu'il pleuvait. The match was postponed because it was raining.
Formal; in everyday speech, remettre à plus tard.

The word, taken apart.
ajourner comes from Old French ajorner (EtymDB), built on jor "day", from Latin diurnum "daily, a day's portion": to put to another day.

Lesson 3 of 6.
aboutir — to succeed, to come to a result; to lead to.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the French for to go by, then the French for to postpone.

You'll want to know: aboutir.
aboutir — "to succeed, to come to a result; to lead to".
Le projet a enfin abouti. The project finally succeeded.
Aboutir à — to lead to: la rue aboutit à la place.

The word, taken apart.
aboutir is built on à and bout "end": to reach the end. Bout comes from bouter "to push", which is usually traced to a Frankish verb; the origin is not settled (EtymDB lists no parent).

Lesson 4 of 6.
le contretemps — the hitch, the mishap.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the French for to postpone, then the French for to succeed.

You'll want to know: le contretemps.
le contretemps — "the hitch, the mishap".
Un petit contretemps nous a mis en retard. A small hitch made us late.
À contretemps — at the wrong moment, out of step.

The word, taken apart.
contretemps joins contre "against", from Latin contrā, and temps "time", from Latin tempus (Lewis & Short): something that goes against the timing.

Lesson 5 of 6.

You'll want to know: l'échéance.
l'échéance — "the deadline, the due date".
L'échéance approche et le travail n'est pas fini. The deadline is getting close and the work is not finished.
À brève échéance — in the short term.

The word, taken apart.
échéance is built on échoir "to fall due", which is inherited from a Latin compound of ex "out" and cadere "to fall" (Lewis & Short).
