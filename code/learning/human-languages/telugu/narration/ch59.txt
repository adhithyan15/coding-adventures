Telugu, chapter 59: Weather and Ground.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
గాలి (gāli) — wind, air.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: what did antē mean?

You'll want to know: గాలి.
గాలి (gāli) — "wind, air".
గాలి (gāli) is inherited, and it covers both the wind that blows and the air that is simply there. Telugu does not split them.
It reaches further than weather. గాలి (gāli) is breath, and it is rumour — గాలి (gāli) వార్త is a story with nothing holding it up, blown in from somewhere. Air, breath and hearsay under one word is a chain a great many languages make, and it usually starts with breathing.
A గాలి (gāli) getting up in the evening is the ordinary reason given for cutting a visit short.

What you've built.
The first reason to start saying goodbye.

Lesson 2 of 5.
మంచు (mañcu) — mist, dew, snow.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: what did gāli mean?

You'll want to know: మంచు.
మంచు (mañcu) — "mist, dew, snow".
మంచు (mañcu) is the cold wet thing in the air: mist on a winter morning, dew on the ground, and snow where there is any. One word for all three states, which suits a country that meets the third of them rarely.
This is worth sitting with. A language draws its lines where its speakers need them. Telugu splits its cooking vessels three ways and lumps mist, dew and snow together; a language spoken where snow falls would do the opposite.
మంచు (mañcu) కురుస్తోంది is "it is misting" on the coast and "it is snowing" in the hills, with no change to the word.

What you've built.
Two: what moves, and what settles.

Lesson 3 of 5.
ఇసుక (isuka) — sand.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: what did mañcu mean?

You'll want to know: ఇసుక.
ఇసుక (isuka) — "sand".
ఇసుక (isuka) is inherited and unremarkable, and that is the point: it is the ground beside every నది in the Telugu country.
The sand of a riverbed is where cattle are watered, where lorries come at night to dig, and where a village argument about who owns the river tends to start. A plain word can carry a lot of local weather.
ఇసుకలో నీళ్ళు, water in sand, is what you say about effort that soaks away.

What you've built.

Lesson 4 of 5.
బురద (burada) — mud.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: what did isuka mean?

You'll want to know: బురద.
బురద (burada) — "mud".
బురద (burada) is mud — what ఇసుక (isuka) becomes when the rain has been at it.
It does the same second job that mud does in English. బురద (burada) చల్లు, to throw mud, is to slander somebody, and the picture is exact enough that you can guess the phrase before you are told it.
బురద (burada) is also the honest reason a visit ends early in the wet months. The బాట between the గ్రామం and the నది turns to it.

What you've built.

Lesson 5 of 5.
నిప్పు (nippu) — fire, a live coal.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: what did burada mean?

You'll want to know: నిప్పు.
నిప్పు (nippu) — "fire, a live coal".
నిప్పు (nippu) is inherited, and it is the fire you can point at: the live coal, the burning thing in the hearth. అగ్ని, borrowed from Sanskrit, is fire as an idea and as a god, the one that carries offerings.
So Telugu keeps a working fire and a ceremonial one, in the same way it keeps a పొద్దు and a సూర్యుడు. That split has turned up so often in these chapters that you can start expecting it: the old word does the work, the borrowed one does the ceremony.
నిప్పు (nippu) is what a house is banked down around at night, and putting it out is the last thing done before sleep. A good place to say వెళ్ళొస్తాను.

What you've built.
Five: గాలి (gāli), మంచు (mañcu), ఇసుక (isuka), బురద (burada), నిప్పు (nippu). Weather enough to take your leave on.
