Latin, chapter 306: Caedēs.
6 lessons. All 6 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 6.
caedēs, caedis — slaughter, murder.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for fraud, then the Latin for a trick.

You'll want to know: caedēs, caedis.
caedēs, caedis — "slaughter, murder".
Post caedem tōta urbs tacita erat. After the murder the whole city was silent.
Feminine, 3rd declension, genitive plural caedium. Used of one killing or of a massacre.

The word, taken apart.
caedēs is built on caedō, "to cut, to cut down" (EtymDB), which is inherited from a root meaning "to cut, to hew".

Lesson 2 of 6.
homicīda, homicīdae — a murderer, a killer.

[pause 2 seconds]

You'll want to know: homicīda, homicīdae.
homicīda, homicīdae — "a murderer, a killer".
Homicīda ē carcere fūgit. The murderer escaped from prison.
Masculine or feminine, 1st declension. English homicide names both the deed and the doer.

The word, taken apart.
homicīda joins homō, "person", and -cīda, "killer", from caedō, "to cut down" (Lewis & Short). English homicide comes from it.

Sīcāriī cōnsulem in viā exspectābant. The assassins were waiting for the consul in the street.
Masculine. Murder trials at Rome were held inter sīcāriōs: among the assassins.

The word, taken apart.
sīcārius is built on sīca, "a curved dagger" (Lewis & Short): the dagger man.

You'll want to know: pugiō, pugiōnis.
pugiō, pugiōnis — "a dagger".
Pugiōnem sub veste habēbat. He had a dagger under his clothing.
Masculine, 3rd declension. A short stabbing blade that soldiers wore on the belt.

The word, taken apart.
pugiō is built, says Lewis & Short, on the root of pungō, "to prick, to stab"; an old explanation says the dagger strikes with the point.

Lesson 5 of 6.
occīdō, occīdere — to kill, to cut down.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for an assassin, then the Latin for a dagger.

You'll want to know: occīdō, occīdere.
occīdō, occīdere — "to kill, to cut down".
Latrō viātōrem occīdit et pecūniam rapuit. The bandit killed a traveller and snatched his money.
occīdō, occīdere, occīdī, occīsum, with a long ī. Do not confuse occidō, 'to fall, to set', of the sun.

The word, taken apart.
occīdō is built on caedō, "to cut down", with ob- (EtymDB, Lewis & Short): to strike down. Its look-alike occidō, "to fall", is built on cadō.
