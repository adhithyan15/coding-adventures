Latin, chapter 288: Bigger Than, and How Long.
3 lessons. All 3 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 3.
longior quam — bigger than, faster than.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say "long", then "fast". (Longus. Celer.)
[pause 1 second]
An experience is often a comparison: the road was longer than you thought, the ship faster than the cart.

Grammar Lens: -ior and quam.
Take the stem of the adjective and add -ior, "more": long-us, longior, longer. quam is "than":
Nāvis celerior est quam raeda. — A ship is faster than a carriage.
[pause 2 seconds]
For a neuter word the ending is -ius:
Mare altius est quam flūmen. — The sea is deeper than the river. Iter longius erat quam putāvī. — The journey was longer than I thought.
[pause 1 second]
Mare and iter are neuter, so altius, longius. Nāvis is feminine, and -ior serves a man or a woman alike: celerior.
[pause 1 second]
Facilis gives facilior, easier. An adjective with a vowel before -us, like idōneus, takes magis, "more", in front instead: magis idōneus quam, more suitable than.

You'll want to know: maior, minor, melior.
Three common adjectives have a comparative of their own, learned whole:
magnus — maior, bigger. parvus — minor, smaller. bonus, good — melior, better.
Rōma maior est quam oppidum meum. — Rome is bigger than my town.

The word, taken apart: maior and melior.
The -ior ending is inherited: EtymDB 2.1 builds maior from the root of magnus, Proto-Indo-European megh2-, "great", with a comparative ending -yos. melior is traced to a Proto-Indo-European melyos, "better", with no link to bonus at all, which is why it has to be learned whole. English took major, minor and, through French, ameliorate.

Lesson 2 of 3.
abhinc — ago, and for how long.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say "for a long time". (Diū.) Then "a year". (Annus.)
[pause 1 second]
Diū says long without saying how long. Latin can count it out, and the case it counts in is the accusative.

Grammar Lens: the accusative of how long.
Trēs annōs Rōmae habitāvī. — I lived in Rome for three years. Duās hōrās ambulāvimus. — We walked for two hours.
[pause 2 seconds]
No word for "for": the stretch of time stands in the accusative, annōs, hōrās, and the verb says what filled it. Duās is duo agreeing with hōrās.

You'll want to know: abhinc.
abhinc — "ago". It counts back from now, and the count goes in the accusative too:
Abhinc trēs annōs Rōmam vēnī. — I came to Rome three years ago. Abhinc decem diēs nāvis solvit. — The ship sailed ten days ago.
[pause 2 seconds]
Put the two together and a life has a shape: abhinc for when it began, the accusative for how long it lasted. Cicero uses abhinc with the accusative in his speeches and letters (Lewis & Short).

The word, taken apart: abhinc.
abhinc is built from ab, "from", and hinc, "from here, from this point" (EtymDB 2.1), and hinc in turn comes from hīc, "here". So the word is "from this point", counted backwards into the past. Lewis & Short note that some early writers also used it forwards, "from now on", a sense that did not last.

Lesson 3 of 3.
Review — what it was like.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say "the sea is deeper than the river". (Mare altius est quam flūmen.)

What you've built.
Two chapters, and an experience can be told whole. How it felt, with eram and quod. What you hope for, with spērō and cupiō. What was bigger, faster or better, with -ior, quam, maior, minor and melior. And time counted out: the accusative for how long, abhinc for how long ago.
Abhinc duōs annōs Rōmam vēnī. Decem diēs ibi mānsī. Rōma maior erat quam putāvī, et laetus eram. — I came to Rome two years ago. I stayed there ten days. Rome was bigger than I thought, and I was happy.
