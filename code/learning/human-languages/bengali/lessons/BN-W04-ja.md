# জ — the consonant ja, and the eight this chapter added

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[BN-SCRIPT-CHA-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Write **চ**, and say where on the roof of the mouth it is made.

## Script
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[BN-SCRIPT-JA-01]; assesses=[BN-SCRIPT-CHA-01, BN-SCRIPT-AA-MATRA-01] -->

> জ

**jô** — the *j* of English *jam*. Same place as **চ**, the flat of the tongue on
the roof of the mouth, with the voice on.

Another two cells of another square, and by now the move should be predictable:
meet a consonant, ask where it is made and whether the voice is running, and the
letter has a family rather than being a shape on its own.

This letter carries a root worth knowing. Sanskrit *jñā*, "to know," is spelled
with it, and the verbs chapter followed that root out to English **know** and to
the *k* that English still writes and no longer says. The Bengali verb for
knowing facts opens on this shape.

With the mātrā:

> **জ** + **া** → **জা**

That is the chapter's eighth and last consonant, and it closes a grid rather than
lengthening a list. Every one of the eight was met as a cell — a place in the
mouth, with the voice and the breath switched on or off. Say them back by place
rather than by shape:

- **at the lips**: **প** voice off, **ব** voice on, **ভ** voice on with breath
- **on the teeth**: **দ** voice on, **ধ** voice on with breath
- **on the roof**: **চ** voice off, **জ** voice on
- **at the throat**: **খ**, voice off and breathy — **ক**'s partner from the
  first script chapter

The cells nobody has filled are letters this book has not needed yet, not gaps in
the script. A reader who can recite that list by place is filling in a grid; a
reader holding eight shapes is memorising eight shapes. That difference is the whole reason the chapter kept
asking *where* and *with what breath* instead of *what does this look like*.
