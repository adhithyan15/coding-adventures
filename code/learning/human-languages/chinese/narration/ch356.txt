Lesson 1 of 6.
jiàoxun — hard lesson learned.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Mandarin for to deny, then the Mandarin for to someone's face.

You'll want to know: jiàoxun.
jiàoxun — "hard lesson learned".
wǒ juéde zhè shì yí ge hěn hǎo de jiàoxun — I think that's a good lesson to learn.
Its characters come later; for now it is a word for the ear.

The word, taken apart.
jiàoxun is built from jiào and xùn, both inherited from Middle Chinese, kaewH and xjunH (Guangyun, in Baxter's transcription), which glosses the first 'to teach' and the second 'to admonish'. The second is said light here.

Lesson 2 of 6.
错过 (cuòguò) — to miss a chance; to miss a train.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Mandarin for to someone's face, then the Mandarin for a hard lesson learned.

You'll want to know: 错过.
错过 — cuòguò — "to miss a chance; to miss a train".
我错过了那个机会 wǒ cuòguò le nàge jīhuì — I missed that chance.
The new character, in pieces: 错 (cuò) — thirteen strokes in two pieces: on the left the metal piece of 钱, five strokes; on the right a short bar with two verticals through it and a long bar under them, then 日 rì, eight strokes. The next lesson puts it in your hand.

The word, taken apart.
错过 (cuòguò) is built from 错 (cuò) 'wrong' and 过 'to pass', both inherited from Middle Chinese, tshak and kwaH (Guangyun, in Baxter's transcription), which glosses the first 'a file' and 'mixed', and the second 'a mistake' and 'to pass over'. How the first came to mean wrong is not settled.

What you've built.
One more word for giving reasons.

Script — the metal piece, then a cross-bar over the sun.
错 (cuò).
Two pieces, left before right. Count the pieces, not the strokes.
The left piece, the metal piece, five strokes, as in 钱: a long left-falling stroke, a short horizontal, a second horizontal, a third that rises to the right, then a vertical that ends rising to the right.
The right piece, eight strokes: a short horizontal, two short verticals down through it, a long horizontal under them, then 日 rì, four strokes, at the foot.
5 + 8 = thirteen.

[once you have stopped driving — this part needs your eyes or your hands; if you are driving, skip ahead to the next part]
Guided Practice.
[once you have stopped driving — write: 错 (cuò), all thirteen, with the model in front of you]
[once you have stopped driving — cover: write it again from memory, and count the pieces, not the strokes]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
How many pieces in 错 (cuò)? (Two.) Which character you know has the same left piece? (钱.) What sits at the foot of the right piece? (日.)

Lesson 4 of 6.
出错 (chūcuò) — to slip up; to go wrong.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Mandarin for a hard lesson learned, then the Mandarin for to miss a chance.

You'll want to know: 出错.
出错 — chūcuò — "to slip up; to go wrong".
他工作从来不出错 tā gōngzuò cónglái bù chūcuò — He never slips up at work.

The word, taken apart.
出错 (chūcuò) is built from 出 'to come out' and 错 (cuò) 'wrong', both inherited from Middle Chinese, tsyhwit and tshak (Guangyun, in Baxter's transcription), which glosses the first 'to go forward' and 'to appear', and the second 'mixed'.
