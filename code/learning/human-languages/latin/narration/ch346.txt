Latin, chapter 346: Deī et deae.
6 lessons. All 6 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 6.
deus, deī — a god.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for the common people, then the Latin for a dictator.

You'll want to know: deus, deī.
deus, deī — "a god".
Poētae dē deīs multa canunt. Poets sing many things about the gods.
Masculine, 2nd declension; plural deī or dī. Its feminine dea is taught.

The word, taken apart.
deus is inherited from Old Latin deivos, Proto-Indo-European deywós, "god" (EtymDB). English deity comes from its family.

Lesson 2 of 6.
dīvus, dīvī — a deified emperor; a god.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a dictator, then the Latin for a god.

You'll want to know: dīvus, dīvī.
dīvus, dīvī — "a deified emperor; a god".
Post mortem imperātor dīvus factus est. After his death the emperor was declared a god.
Masculine, also an adjective, dīvus, -a, -um, 'divine'. Coins call dead emperors dīvus. English divine is of its family.

The word, taken apart.
dīvus is usually traced to the same Old Latin deivos, "god", as deus; Lewis & Short compare Greek dios, "divine". English divine comes from its family.

Lesson 3 of 6.
nūmen, nūminis — divine power, divine will.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a god, then the Latin for a deified emperor.

You'll want to know: nūmen, nūminis.
nūmen, nūminis — "divine power, divine will".
Nūmen deae hunc lūcum servābat. The power of the goddess protected this grove.
Neuter, 3rd declension: the nod of a god, and so divine power. English numinous.

The word, taken apart.
nūmen is built, says Lewis & Short, on nuō, "to nod", seen in innuō: a nod, the nod of a god. English numinous comes from it.

Lesson 4 of 6.
mortālis, mortāle — mortal.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a deified emperor, then the Latin for divine power.

You'll want to know: mortālis, mortāle.
mortālis, mortāle — "mortal".
Omnēs hominēs mortālēs sunt. All human beings are mortal.
Two endings, from mors, 'death'. As a noun, mortālēs: human beings.

The word, taken apart.
mortālis is built on mors, "death" (EtymDB): subject to death. English mortal comes from it.

Lesson 5 of 6.
immortālis, immortāle — immortal, undying.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for divine power, then the Latin for mortal.

You'll want to know: immortālis, immortāle.
immortālis, immortāle — "immortal, undying".
Deī immortālēs numquam moriuntur. The immortal gods never die.
Two endings: in- 'not' + mortālis. Dī immortālēs!: Good gods!

The word, taken apart.
immortālis joins in-, "not", and mortālis, "mortal" (EtymDB). English immortal comes from it.
