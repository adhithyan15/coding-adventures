Latin, chapter 474: To plough, Furrow, Ploughshare, Ploughman, To harrow.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
arō, arāre — to plough.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a cottage, then the Latin for a hut.

You'll want to know: arō, arāre.
arō, arāre — "to plough".
Vēre agricolae agrōs arant. In spring the farmers plough the fields.
arō, arāre, arāvī, arātum. The plough is arātrum. English arable comes from it.

The word, taken apart.
arō is inherited from Proto-Italic araō (EtymDB), with Greek aroō 'to plough' as a cousin (EtymDB; Lewis & Short); Lewis & Short add Gothic arjan. English arable comes from it.

Lesson 2 of 5.
sulcus, sulcī — a furrow; a trench.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a hut, then the Latin for to plough.

You'll want to know: sulcus, sulcī.
sulcus, sulcī — "a furrow; a trench".
Agricola sulcōs rēctōs in agrō facit. The farmer makes straight furrows in the field.
Second declension, masculine: sulcus, sulcī m. Also the wake of a ship. English keeps sulcus in anatomy for a groove.

The word, taken apart.
sulcus is inherited from PIE solk-o- 'furrow' (EtymDB); Lewis & Short compare Greek holkos 'furrow'.

Lesson 3 of 5.
vōmer, vōmeris — a ploughshare.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to plough, then the Latin for a furrow.

You'll want to know: vōmer, vōmeris.
vōmer, vōmeris — "a ploughshare".
Vōmer ex ferrō est et terram dūram frangit. The ploughshare is made of iron and breaks the hard ground.
Third declension, masculine: vōmer, vōmeris m. (also vōmis). The iron blade of the plough.

The word, taken apart.
The origin of vōmer is not settled: Lewis & Short give the sense but no source, and EtymDB lists it without a parent. English keeps vomer for a bone of the nose.

Third declension, masculine: arātor, arātōris m., from arō with -tor.

Lesson 5 of 5.
occō, occāre — to harrow, to break up the soil.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a ploughshare, then the Latin for a ploughman.

You'll want to know: occō, occāre.
occō, occāre — "to harrow, to break up the soil".
Agricola agrum prīmum arat, deinde occat. The farmer first ploughs the field, then harrows it.
occō, occāre, occāvī, occātum, from occa 'a harrow'.

The word, taken apart.
occō is built on occa 'a harrow' (EtymDB), from the PIE root h₂eḱ- 'sharp'. Lewis & Short give German eggen 'to harrow' as a cousin.
