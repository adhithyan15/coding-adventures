\chapter{Greetings}
\label{ch:greetings}

The Bengali greetings --- and, taught through them, how to read the Bengali
script and the one sound-shift that turns every Sanskrit word into something
recognisably \emph{Bengali}. Each lesson introduces the letters its word needs.
\emph{Practice lessons: \texttt{lessons/BN-C01-*.md}.}

\section{\bn{নমস্কার} \emph{(n\^{o}moshkar)} --- ``hello''}
\label{lesson:nomoshkar}

\begin{sounds}
\emph{(If you already read Bengali, skim this.)} Bengali runs \textbf{left to
right} under a top line, and every consonant carries a built-in vowel --- but in
Bengali that vowel is \textbf{\^{o}} (an open ``o''), \emph{not} ``a.'' \bn{ন}
n\^{o}, \bn{ম} m\^{o}, \bn{ক} k\^{o}, \bn{র} r\^{o}. A \textbf{vowel sign} changes
it (\bn{া} \=a); a \emph{hasanta} under \bn{স্} strips the vowel so it joins the
next as a conjunct: \bn{স্} $+$ \bn{ক} $\to$ \bn{স্ক}.
\end{sounds}

\begin{cousinweb}
\bn{নমস্কার} is the \emph{same word} as the Sanskrit \emph{namask\=ara} you met in
Hindi and Marathi (\emph{namas} ``a bow'' $+$ \emph{k\=ara} ``the making of'') ---
but listen to what Bengali does to it. The inherent ``a'' becomes \textbf{\^{o}},
and \emph{s} shifts to \textbf{sh}, so the written \emph{namaskāra} is
\emph{spoken} \textbf{n\^{o}\-moshkar}. That one habit --- \emph{a}$\to$\emph{\^{o}},
\emph{s}$\to$\emph{sh} --- is the fingerprint of Bengali, and once you hear it you
can un-shift almost any word back to its Sanskrit cousin.
\end{cousinweb}

\begin{culture}
\emph{N\^{o}moshkar}, palms together, is the everyday respectful greeting among
Bengali Hindus and in formal speech generally --- hello and, often, goodbye.
Bengali Muslims more often exchange \emph{as-sal\=amu alaikum} / \emph{n\=am\=ask\=ar}
by community; \emph{n\^{o}moshkar} is the broad, secular-to-Hindu default. Bengali
is the language of Tagore --- the only tongue to have written the national anthem
of \emph{two} countries, India and Bangladesh.
\end{culture}

\section{\bn{ধন্যবাদ} \emph{(dh\^{o}nyob\=ad)} --- ``thank you''}
\label{lesson:dhonnobad}

\begin{sounds}
New: \bn{ধ} \emph{dh\^{o}} (breathy \emph{d}), \bn{ব} \emph{b\^{o}}, \bn{দ}
\emph{d\^{o}}, and the conjunct \bn{ন্য} (\bn{ন} $+$ \bn{য} \emph{y}). With the
\bn{া} sign, read \bn{ধন্যবাদ} as \emph{dh\^{o}\,·\,nyo\,·\,b\=a\,·\,d}.
\end{sounds}

\begin{cousinweb}
\bn{ধন্যবাদ} is Sanskrit \emph{dhanya} (``worthy'') $+$ \emph{v\=ada} (``a
saying'') --- the very ``thank you'' you learned in Hindi (\emph{dhanyav\=ad}),
Marathi, and Punjabi. Two Bengali quirks show at once: the inherent
\emph{a}$\to$\emph{\^{o}} again (\emph{dh\^{o}nyob\=ad}), and \textbf{no separate
"v"} --- Bengali has only \bn{ব}, so Sanskrit \emph{v\=ada} is spoken
\emph{b\=ad}. \emph{V} becoming \emph{b} is a Bengali signature (think of
\emph{Bishnu} for \emph{Vishnu}).
\end{cousinweb}

\section{\bn{হ্যাঁ} / \bn{না} \emph{(hyã / n\=a)} --- ``yes'' and ``no''}
\label{lesson:hyan-na}

\begin{sounds}
New: \bn{হ} \emph{h\^{o}}, the \emph{y-phola} \bn{্য} that glides the sound, and
the nasal mark \emph{chandrabindu} \bn{ঁ} (a moon-with-dot) that sends the vowel
through the nose. \bn{না} is simply \bn{ন} $+$ \bn{া} \emph{n\=a}.
\end{sounds}

\begin{cousinweb}
\bn{হ্যাঁ} (\emph{hyã}, ``yes'') and \bn{না} (\emph{n\=a}, ``no''). That
``no'' rides the ancient negative \emph{na-} --- the same Proto-Indo-European
\emph{*ne} behind English \textbf{no}, \textbf{not}, \textbf{none}, Latin
\emph{n\=on}, and Hindi \emph{nah\=\i\d{n}}. Bengali kept it at its shortest,
bare \emph{n\=a}: the family resemblance to English \emph{no} is right on the
surface.
\end{cousinweb}

\section{\bn{আচ্ছা} \emph{(\=achchh\=a)} --- ``okay / I see''}
\label{lesson:achchha}

\begin{sounds}
New: \bn{আ} --- the \textbf{independent} vowel \=a, the shape a word uses when it
\emph{starts} with a vowel (a built-in vowel rides a consonant; a standalone one
gets its own letter). Then the doubled \bn{চ্ছ} (\bn{চ} \emph{ch} $+$ \bn{ছ}
\emph{chh}). Read \bn{আচ্ছা} as \emph{\=a\,·\,chchh\=a}.
\end{sounds}

\begin{cousinweb}
\bn{আচ্ছা} (\emph{\=achchh\=a}) is the all-purpose ``okay, alright, I see, go on''
--- a true workhorse of spoken Bengali, from Sanskrit \emph{acch\=a} (``good,
clear''). It is the little word that keeps a conversation moving; a Bengali
speaker will pepper speech with \emph{\=achchh\=a\ldots\ \=achchh\=a} the way an
English speaker says ``right\ldots\ right.''
\end{cousinweb}

\section{\bn{আসি} \emph{(\=ashi)} --- ``I'll be going'' (goodbye)}
\label{lesson:ashi}

\begin{sounds}
New: \bn{স} again --- here truly ``sh'' --- and the \bn{ি} sign (\emph{i}), which
in Bengali is \emph{written before} the consonant it follows but \emph{read
after} it. \bn{আসি} $=$ \bn{আ} $+$ \bn{সি}, \emph{\=a-shi}.
\end{sounds}

\begin{cousinweb}
\bn{আসি} (\emph{\=ashi}) literally means ``\textbf{I come}'' --- from the verb
\emph{\=as\=a} (``to come,'' Sanskrit root shared with Hindi \emph{\=an\=a}). Yet
it is how Bengalis say \emph{goodbye}. Never ``I'm leaving'' (unlucky, too final)
but ``I'll come [again]'' --- \bn{আসি} or the fuller \emph{\=aschhi} (``I am
coming''). It is the very same ``promise of return'' farewell you met in Tamil
and Marathi: a whole culture's optimism folded into one verb.
\end{cousinweb}

\begin{grammarlens}
Notice Bengali marks no gender on the verb --- \emph{\=ashi} is ``I come''
whether a man or a woman says it. That sets it apart from Marathi (\emph{yeto}
m./\emph{yete} f.) and Hindi, and makes Bengali verbs, in this one respect,
simpler than its neighbours'.
\end{grammarlens}

\section{Recap}
\label{BN-C01-practice}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
Bengali & & Meaning \\
\midrule
\bn{নমস্কার} & \emph{n\^{o}moshkar} & hello / goodbye \\
\bn{ধন্যবাদ} & \emph{dh\^{o}nyob\=ad} & thank you \\
\bn{হ্যাঁ} / \bn{না} & \emph{hyã / n\=a} & yes / no \\
\bn{আচ্ছা} & \emph{\=achchh\=a} & okay / I see \\
\bn{আসি} & \emph{\=ashi} & goodbye (``I'll come again'') \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

One habit unlocks this whole chapter: Bengali's inherent vowel is \textbf{\^{o}},
not ``a,'' and its \emph{s} leans toward \textbf{sh} while its \emph{v} collapses
to \textbf{b}. Hear those shifts and the ``foreign'' \emph{n\^{o}moshkar} and
\emph{dh\^{o}nyob\=ad} snap back into the Sanskrit \emph{namask\=ara} and
\emph{dhanyav\=ada} you already knew. Next chapter: introducing yourself ---
\emph{\=am\=ar n\=am\ldots} (``my name\ldots'') --- and Bengali's \emph{tumi} /
\emph{\=apni} (familiar / respectful ``you'').
