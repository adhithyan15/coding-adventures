German, chapter 9: Months and Seasons.
2 lessons. All 2 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 2.
die Monate — Rome's calendar in German.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Here's the German pattern again. Its numbers are homegrown (eins, zwei), its weekday-gods are Germanic (Donnerstag). But its months — like its clock-word Uhr — are borrowed straight from Latin. Time-reckoning, again, came to the German world from Rome.

The months, taken apart.
[a table of 12 rows, read aloud]
German: Januar. from Latin: Januarius. who / what: Janus, god of doors & beginnings.
German: Februar. from Latin: Februarius. who / what: the Februa purification festival.
German: März. from Latin: Martius. who / what: Mars, the war-god — the same Mars as English Tuesday / French mardi.
German: April. from Latin: Aprilis. who / what: perhaps aperire "to open".
German: Mai. from Latin: Maius. who / what: Maia, goddess of growth.
German: Juni. from Latin: Junius. who / what: Juno, queen of the gods.
German: Juli. from Latin: Julius. who / what: Julius Caesar.
German: August. from Latin: Augustus. who / what: the emperor Augustus.
German: September. from Latin: september. who / what: "7th" (septem).
German: Oktober. from Latin: october. who / what: "8th" (octo).
German: November. from Latin: november. who / what: "9th" (novem).
German: Dezember. from Latin: december. who / what: "10th" (decem).
Two familiar payoffs:
März = Mars. German borrowed the Roman war-god's month whole — the same god behind English March and, as Tuesday (Tiw = Mars), behind German's own Dienstag. The god came in twice, once by name, once by translation.
September–Dezember = 7–10. Even in German, these keep the Latin numbers septem…decem (the Roman year began in March). So German's own numbers are sieben, acht, neun, zehn — but its month names count in Latin.
Three layers of the German calendar, three origins: numbers native, weekdays Germanic gods, months (and the clock, Uhr) Latin.

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Are German month names native or borrowed, and like which other German word? (Borrowed from Latin — like the clock-word Uhr.) What links März to Dienstag? (The war-god Mars = Germanic Tiw.) Why is Dezember Latin for "ten"? (The Roman year began in March.) Next: the seasons — where German goes native again.


Lesson 2 of 2.
die Jahreszeiten — the seasons go native.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
The months were Latin. The seasons swing back to native German — and one of them, Herbst, is the same word as English harvest. The word for "season" itself is transparent: Jahres-zeit = "year-time."

The four seasons, taken apart.
[a table of 4 rows, read aloud]
German: der Frühling (spring). =: früh "early" + -ling. English twin: early-season (from früh, cousin of English early).
German: der Sommer (summer). =: native Germanic. English twin: summer.
German: der Herbst (autumn). =: native Germanic. English twin: harvest.
German: der Winter (winter). =: native Germanic. English twin: winter.
All four are homegrown Germanic, and three are the plain twins of English summer, winter, and — the surprise — harvest:
Frühling is built from früh ("early") — literally the "early-season," the year's first. (German also uses Lenz, an old poetic word for spring.)
Herbst corresponds to harvest. They're the same Germanic word: the reaping season. English kept harvest but narrowed it to the act of gathering and reached for Latin-derived autumn for the season; German kept the old word for the season itself. Two languages, one word, split in meaning.
So the German year splits cleanly: Latin for the months (like Uhr, the clock), native Germanic for the seasons (like the numbers and Mittag/Mitternacht).

[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Are German seasons native or Latin, unlike the months? (Native Germanic.) What English word is Herbst the twin of, and what happened to it? (Harvest — English narrowed it to the reaping act and took autumn from Latin for the season.) What is Frühling built from? (früh "early" — the early season.)
