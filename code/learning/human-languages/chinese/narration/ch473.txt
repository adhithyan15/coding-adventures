Lesson 1 of 6.
zuǐ — mouth; beak.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Mandarin for the look on one's face, then the Mandarin for the forehead.

You'll want to know: zuǐ.
zuǐ — "mouth; beak".
zhège cài tài là le wǒ de zuǐ hěn nánshòu — This dish is so hot my mouth is burning.
Its characters come later; for now it is a word for the ear.
zuǐ is the everyday word for the mouth; 口 kǒu mostly appears inside other words, as in rùkǒu 'entrance'.

The word, taken apart.
zuǐ No dictionary we use records an older form, so its history is not settled.

Lesson 2 of 6.
牙医 (yáyī) — dentist.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Mandarin for the forehead, then the Mandarin for the mouth.

You'll want to know: 牙医.
牙医 — yáyī — "dentist".
我的牙医很和气 wǒ de yáyī hěn héqi — My dentist is very nice.
The new character, in pieces: 牙 (yá) — four strokes: a short bar on top; a short stroke down on the left that turns and runs right; a vertical that hooks to the left at the foot; and a long stroke falling to the left. The next lesson puts it in your hand.

The word, taken apart.
牙医 (yáyī) is built from 牙 (yá) 'tooth' and 医 'doctor', both inherited from Middle Chinese, ngae and 'i (Guangyun, in Baxter's transcription), which glosses the first 'teeth' and the second 'to heal'.

Script — four strokes, each one different.
牙 (yá).
One piece of four strokes. Count them as you go.
The first two. A short bar along the top, from left to right. Then under it on the left a short stroke down that turns and runs right, longer than the bar.
The last two. A vertical from the top bar down through the second stroke, hooking to the left at the foot. Then a long stroke falling to the left, from the middle down to the bottom left.
2 + 2 = four.

Guided Practice.

[once you have stopped driving — cover: write it again from memory, and count the pieces, not the strokes]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
How many strokes does 牙 (yá) have? (Four.) Which comes last? (The long stroke falling to the left.)

Lesson 5 of 6.
báyá — to have a tooth pulled.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Mandarin for a dentist, then the Mandarin for the teeth.

You'll want to know: báyá.
báyá — "to have a tooth pulled".
wǒ jīntiān qù báyá le xiànzài hái hěn téng — I had a tooth out today, and it still really hurts.
Its characters come later; for now it is a word for the ear.

The word, taken apart.
báyá is built from bá 'to pull up' and 牙 (yá) 'tooth', both inherited from Middle Chinese, beat and ngae (Guangyun, in Baxter's transcription). The dictionary lists the first under three readings and glosses this one 'to pull up' and 'to promote'.
