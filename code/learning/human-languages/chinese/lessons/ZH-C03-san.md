# 三 — three, and the end of the easy road

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ZH-SCRIPT-NUM-SAN] -->

[PAUSE 2s] [YOU WRITE: the three bars — middle short, bottom long]

## Sounds you'll need
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ZH-LEX-NUM-SAN]; assesses=[ZH-METHOD-STROKE-COUNT] -->

**三** *sān* means **three**. Three strokes, three — for the third and last time.

**sān** is **first tone**, the flat high one you met on *yī*. Hold it level. The
*a* is open, the *n* light at the end. [REPEAT x2]

So of your first three numbers, two sit flat and one falls:

> **yī** flat, **èr** falling, **sān** flat

Say the run. It goes *level, down, level*, and that shape is the first thing a
listener recognises — before any of the individual words.

## Why it's said this way — the pattern stops here
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ZH-METHOD-STROKE-COUNT] -->

In the three number characters you have met, the stroke count matches the
number: 一, 二, 三. **That local pattern stops here.** The next character uses a
different shape, so do not try to guess it by stacking another bar. You will
learn its shape directly in the next lesson.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[] -->

**三** *sān* is inherited from Middle Chinese *sam*, "three" (Guangyun, in
Baxter's transcription). The final *m* has since become *n*.
