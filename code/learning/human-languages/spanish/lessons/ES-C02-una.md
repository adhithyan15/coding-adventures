# una — the feminine one

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-GRAMMAR-INDEFINITE-ARTICLE-MASCULINE, ES-LEX-TARDE] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say "a day." (*Un día*.) And what is the word for "the afternoon"?
(*La tarde*.)

## Grammar Lens: the partner
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-GRAMMAR-INDEFINITE-ARTICLE-FEMININE]; assesses=[ES-GRAMMAR-NOUN-GENDER, ES-LEX-TARDE, ES-LEX-NOCHE] -->

*Tarde* and *noche* are feminine, so they take the feminine article:

> **una tarde** — *an afternoon.*
> **una noche** — *a night.*

You now have both, and they behave exactly as *el* and *la* do — the article
takes the gender of the noun standing next to it. Nothing new is being asked of
you; the rule you learned for *el* and *la* simply applies again.

| | masculine | feminine |
|---|---|---|
| the | *el* día | *la* tarde |
| a | **un** día | **una** tarde |

This table is allowed here because every box in it is something you can already
say.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-ETYMON-UNUS, ES-GRAMMAR-INDEFINITE-ARTICLE-FEMININE] -->

One detail, because it runs against what you might expect.

*Una* is not *un* with something added. It is the other way round: Latin had
*ūnus* and *ūna*, and **una kept the whole word while un lost a syllable.**

That is the ordinary direction of travel. The forms said most often wear down
fastest, and *un* — standing in front of masculine nouns, which are the default
for anything unspecified — got said more.
