# au printemps, en été — saying you are *in* a season

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[] -->

[PAUSE 2s] You have the four seasons. Now put something *in* one — and meet a
rule with exactly one exception, which turns out not to be an exception at all.

## Grammar Lens: three take en, one takes au
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[FR-GRAM-EN-SAISON-07]; assesses=[] -->

Three of the four use **en**:

> **en été** · **en automne** · **en hiver**

And spring does not:

> **au printemps**

Learn it as a list and it is an oddity to memorise. Look at the words and it
stops being odd. **Été**, **automne** and **hiver** all begin with a **vowel
sound** — that is why each of them elides its article to *l'*. **Printemps**
begins with a consonant, and it is the only one that does.

So the split is not about seasons at all. It is the **same vowel-or-consonant
question** that decided *le* against *l'*, asked one more time about a different
little word. French keeps asking it, and once you hear it you stop needing the
list.

## Wrap-up Recall
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[FR-GRAM-EN-SAISON-07, FR-LEX-PRINTEMPS-01, FR-LEX-HIVER-05] -->

[PAUSE 3s] How do you say "in summer"? (**en été**.) In winter? (**en hiver**.)
In spring? (**au printemps** — not *en*.) And **why** is spring different? (It
is the only season starting with a **consonant**; the other three begin with a
vowel sound, which is also why they elide to *l'*.)
