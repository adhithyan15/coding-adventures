German, chapter 26: Being Your Years.
7 lessons. All 7 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 7.
alt — "old".

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
You can say ich bin müde. Here is a second adjective to put in the same slot, and it opens a sentence you will be asked for constantly.

You'll want to know: alt.
alt — "old."
One syllable. It behaves exactly as müde did: after sein an adjective takes no ending at all, so ich bin alt needs nothing added.
It is the same word as English old, and you can nearly hear it — German kept the a where English rounded it.

Sounds you'll need.
vowel-a — short and clipped, the a of English hat.
d-t-final — the -lt closes crisply, both consonants sounded.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: "alt"]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: the pair — "alt, old"]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: with the verb — "ich bin alt", "ich bin müde"]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[repeat that twice]
"alt, alt — old."

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Say "old." (Alt.) Does it take an ending after bin? (No.) Which English word is it? (Old.) Next: the noun that goes with it.


Lesson 2 of 7.
die Jahre — the form the age sentence uses.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
A noun you were given ten chapters ago. The age sentence wants it in a shape you have not used yet.

You'll want to know: die Jahre.
die Jahre — the plural of das Jahr.
The plural adds -e and takes die, as plurals do, and it stays capitalised because German capitalises every noun in every number.
Here is why it matters. The age sentence counts, and a count takes a plural: you are so many Jahre old, never so many Jahr. From here on it is Jahre that does the work, and das Jahr that sits behind it.

Sounds you'll need.
j-as-y — German j is the y of English yes. So Jahr opens like year, not like jar.
h-silent-lengthening — that h is not pronounced. It is the silent lengthening h you met inside gehen, and Jahr was named there as one of the three words that hide it, beside sehr and Ihnen. Here it is.
long-a — so the vowel runs long: YAAR. Say it beside English year and the two are almost the same noise.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: singular and plural — "das Jahr, die Jahre"]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: the plural alone, which is the one you need — "die Jahre"]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: the two words the sentence will need — "Jahre, alt"]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[repeat that twice]
"das Jahr, die Jahre."

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Say "the year." (Das Jahr.) And the plural. (Die Jahre.) Which of the two does an age sentence use? (The plural — you are counted.) What does the plural add? (An -e, and die.) Next: where Jahr and alt both come from.


Lesson 3 of 7.
Jahr and alt, taken apart — both words are English wearing German spelling, and alt has a Latin cousin hiding in adult.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Two words, both inherited rather than borrowed, and one of them has a Latin cousin you would never guess.

The word, taken apart.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
German: Jahr. from: Germanic jēra. English: year.
German: alt. from: Germanic aldaz. English: old.
Neither line passes through Latin, which is worth noticing in a chapter about age: German borrowed its month names wholesale from Latin, and kept its own words for the year and for being old.
Now the cousin. The root behind aldaz also went into Latin, as alere — "to nourish, to grow." From alere English took adult, which is literally "grown up."
So alt and adult are the same idea taken down two roads: something that has been fed and has grown. Ageing as growth rather than as decay, which is a kinder picture than English usually admits to.

[your turn — say: the two twins — "Jahr, year; alt, old"]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: the Latin cousin — "alt, alere, adult"]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: what the root meant — "to nourish, to grow"]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[repeat that twice]
"Jahr, year. Alt, old."

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Which English word is Jahr? (Year.) And alt? (Old.) Which English word comes from alt's Latin cousin? (Adult.) What did that cousin mean? ("To nourish, to grow.") Next: the question you will be asked with these two words.


Lesson 4 of 7.
Wie alt bist du? — how old are you — word for word the English question, built from four words you own.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
You have wie, you have alt, and you have du bist. That is the whole question.

You'll want to know: Wie alt bist du?
Wie alt bist du? — "How old are you?"
Four words, and every one of them is already yours. Read it against the English and nothing has moved: how — old — are — you.
That is not a coincidence to be grateful for; it is inheritance. Neither language borrowed this question from the other. They have been asking it the same way since before either was called German or English.
The verb sits second, as it does in every German statement and question with a question word in front — Wie first, bist second, and the rest behind.
To someone you would address formally, it is Wie alt sind Sie? — the same question with the polite form of the verb.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: "Wie alt bist du?"]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: it against the English — "how old are you; wie alt bist du"]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: the formal one — "Wie alt sind Sie?"]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[repeat that twice]
"Wie alt bist du?"

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Ask how old someone is, informally. (Wie alt bist du?) And formally. (Wie alt sind Sie?) Which word sits second? (Bist.) How many of the four words were new? (None.) Next: how to answer it.


Lesson 5 of 7.
ich bin zwanzig Jahre alt — I am twenty years old — the answer, word for word the English sentence.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
The answer, and it is as English as the question was.

You'll want to know: ich bin zwanzig Jahre alt.
Ich bin zwanzig Jahre alt. — "I am twenty years old."
Word for word, that is the English sentence: I — am — twenty — years — old. Put any number you own in the middle and the sentence is yours.
And German shortens it the same way English does. Ich bin zwanzig is complete and ordinary, exactly as "I'm twenty" is — the Jahre alt can go, and in speech usually does.
Notice which verb is doing the work: bin. Not habe. Hold on to that, because it is the whole point of the next lesson.

Guided Practice.

[your turn — say: "Ich bin zwanzig Jahre alt."]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: the short form — "Ich bin zwanzig."]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: your own age, in both lengths]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: the question and the answer together]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[repeat that twice]
"Ich bin zwanzig Jahre alt."

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Say "I am twenty years old." (Ich bin zwanzig Jahre alt.) Shorten it. (Ich bin zwanzig.) Which verb does it use? (Bin.) Which does it not use? (Habe.) Next: why that choice puts German and English on one side and every Romance language on the other.


Lesson 6 of 7.
sein, not haben — Germanic IS its years where Romance HAS them — the one place German refuses haben.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
You spent a whole chapter on haben. Here is the everyday sentence where German refuses to let you use it.

Grammar Lens: you ARE your years.
German states age with sein, never with haben.
Ich habe zwanzig Jahre is not German. It is not a clumsy way of putting it; it is not a sentence.
Look what happens when you line the languages up:
[a table of 6 rows, read aloud]
language: German. how age works: ich bin … Jahre alt. literally: I am …
language: English. how age works: I am twenty. literally: I am …
language: French. how age works: j'ai vingt ans. literally: I have …
language: Spanish. how age works: tengo veinte años. literally: I have …
language: Italian. how age works: ho venti anni. literally: I have …
language: Portuguese. how age works: tenho vinte anos. literally: I have …
Two on one side, four on the other, and the line falls exactly where the families do.

Why it's said this way.
Romance has its years; Germanic is its years. Age as something you carry against age as something you are — two pictures of the same fact, and each family committed to one of them long ago.
What makes German's choice worth pointing at is that German did not hold the Latin line out generally. Its month names are Latin borrowings wholesale; Januar, Februar and the rest came in and stayed. But on this sentence, said several times a week for a lifetime, German kept its own logic.
The words a language uses most are the ones it is least willing to trade — which is the same force that keeps its commonest verbs irregular.

[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: the two sides — "ich bin zwanzig; j'ai vingt ans"]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: the picture — "Romance has its years, Germanic is its years"]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[repeat that twice]
"Ich bin zwanzig Jahre alt — bin, not habe."

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Which verb does German use for age? (Sein.) Which does French use? (Avoir — "to have".) Which two languages sit together here? (German and English.) Did German borrow its month names from Latin? (Yes — which is what makes keeping this sentence its own worth noticing.) Next: the chapter said through.


Lesson 7 of 7.
Practice — asking and answering an age.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
No new words. One question, one answer, and one thing to keep hearing: which verb carries it.

The exchange.
[a table of 5 rows, read aloud]
Wie alt bist du? means How old are you?
Zwanzig Jahre alt. Und du? means Twenty years old. And you?
Ich bin achtzehn. means I am eighteen.
Und du bist müde. means And you are tired.
Aber nicht alt! means But not old!
Every line runs on the same verb, and two of them drop it the way speech does — zwanzig Jahre alt answers the question on its own. Nothing here needs haben, and the last two lines put müde and alt side by side in the slot they share.
