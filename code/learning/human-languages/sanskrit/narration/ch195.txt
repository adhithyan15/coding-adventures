पत्तनम् (pattanam) — a port town.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]

You'll want to know: पत्तनम्.
पत्तनम् — pattanam — "a port town".

The word, taken apart.
The origin of pattana is disputed. CDIAL 7705 reports that it is probably borrowed from Dravidian, but notes that its sense "ferry" in several modern languages supports a native partana-, related to Latin portus, "harbour"; the two may have merged. It lives on as Hindi pāṭan, "town".

Lesson 2 of 5.
राजधानी (rājadhānī) — a capital.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Sanskrit for a fair, then the Sanskrit for a port town.

You'll want to know: राजधानी.
राजधानी — rājadhānī — "a capital".

The word, taken apart.
rājadhānī is a compound of rāja-, "king", and dhānī, "receptacle, seat", built on the root √dhā, "to put, place" (MW): the place where the king is set. MW lists it as a royal residence, and modern languages use it for a capital city.

Lesson 3 of 5.
जनपदः (janapadaḥ) — a district, a region.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Sanskrit for a port town, then the Sanskrit for a capital.

You'll want to know: जनपदः.
जनपदः — janapadaḥ — "a district, a region".

The word, taken apart.
janapada is a compound of jana, "people", and pada, "footing, place" (MW). In the Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa it means "a people", and in the Mahābhārata "a country" (CDIAL 5101).

Lesson 4 of 5.
प्रान्तः (prāntaḥ) — a province, a border.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Sanskrit for a capital, then the Sanskrit for a district.

You'll want to know: प्रान्तः.
प्रान्तः — prāntaḥ — "a province, a border".

The word, taken apart.
prānta is pra with anta, "end, limit" (MW), and so "edge, border" (CDIAL 8940); a province is the land out at the edge. MW compares anta with English end, an inherited Indo-European word.

Lesson 5 of 5.
सीमा (sīmā) — a boundary.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Sanskrit for a district, then the Sanskrit for a province.

You'll want to know: सीमा.
सीमा — sīmā — "a boundary".

The word, taken apart.
sīmā comes from sīman, which in the Atharvaveda means "the parting of the hair" (CDIAL 13435): a line that divides. Later it means "boundary" (MW). It lives on as Gujarati sīm, "boundary of a village".
