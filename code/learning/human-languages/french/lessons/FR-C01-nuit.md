# nuit — "night," and a sound-change that mirrors Spanish

## Warm-up

[PAUSE 2s] "Night." In Spanish it's *noche*; in French, *nuit*. They came from
the *same* Latin word and drifted apart by two different rules — and once you
see the rules, you can predict a dozen more words.

## You'll want to know first

- [le / la / les](./FR-C01-le-la.md) — *nuit* is feminine, *la nuit*.

## Sounds you'll need

- **nuit** = *nwee* — the **u** is the front-rounded French *u* gliding into
  *ee*; the final **t** is silent (`silent-final`). → [reference](../pronunciation-reference.md)

## The word, taken apart

**la nuit** — "the night" (feminine). Root: Latin **noctem** (*nox*, stem
*noct-*). English cousins through the *noct-* root: **noct**urnal,
**noct**urne, equi**nox** ("equal night").

**The sound-change, side by side with Spanish.** Latin *noctem* went two
ways:

- Spanish softened *-ct-* → **-ch-**: *noctem → noche*.
- French turned *-ct-* → **-it-**: *noctem → nuit*.

And it's a *rule*, not a one-off — watch it repeat:

| Latin | Spanish | French | English |
|---|---|---|---|
| *noctem* | noche | nuit | night |
| *lactem* | leche | lait | (lact-, "milk") |
| *factum* | hecho | fait | fact |

So a Spanish *-ch-* word and a French *-it-* word are often the *same* Latin
word — and the English cousin usually kept the *-ct-*.

## Grammar Lens

*nuit* is **feminine** — *la nuit* — inherited from Latin, where *nox* was
feminine (exactly as Spanish *la noche* is). Its gender matters next lesson:
it's why "good night" is *bon**ne** nuit*, not *bon nuit*.
