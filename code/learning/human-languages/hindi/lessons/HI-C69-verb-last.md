# The verb goes last

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[HI-CONCEPT-C02-MERANAAMHAI-01, HI-CONCEPT-C05-MAINHINDIBOLTAHUN-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say two sentences you have had since chapter two and chapter five:
**मेरा नाम मीरा है**, and **मैं हिंदी बोलता हूँ**. Now listen to where each one
ENDS.

## Grammar Lens: a rule you have been obeying for sixty-eight chapters
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[HI-GRAMMAR-VERB-FINAL-01]; assesses=[HI-CONCEPT-C37-KITAAB-01, HI-JOIN-LEKIN-01] -->

Every Hindi sentence in this book puts its **verb at the end**:

- **मेरा नाम मीरा है** — my name Mira **is**
- **मैं हिंदी बोलता हूँ** — I Hindi **speak**
- **यह किताब है** — this book **is**

You have seen this a hundred times and no lesson has ever said it out loud. It
is being said now because you are about to start joining clauses, and the moment
a sentence has two halves you need to know where each half stops. **The verb is
the boundary marker.** In **चाय है लेकिन दूध नहीं है**, the first **है** is what
tells the ear the first clause has ended.

English is the odd one here, not Hindi. Hindi's order is subject, object, verb —
so is Japanese's, so is Turkish's, and so is roughly half the world's. English
puts the verb in the middle and makes it look normal.

The practical form of the rule: **when you build a Hindi sentence, decide the
verb first and then say it last.** A learner who starts translating word by word
from English will hit the verb too early, every time.
