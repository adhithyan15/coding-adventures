# werden — "to become"

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GE-LEX-FAHREN-01, GE-GRAMMAR-PERFEKT-SEIN-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] The rule has two halves. *Gehen*, *kommen* and *fahren* are the
motion half. This one word is the whole of the other half.

## You'll want to know: werden
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[GE-LEX-WERDEN-01]; assesses=[GE-GRAMMAR-PERFEKT-SEIN-01, GE-LEX-MUEDE-01] -->

> **werden** — "to become."

> **ich bin geworden** — "I became."

*Sein* says what you are. *Werden* says what you turn into. Where *sein* sits
still, *werden* moves — and that is exactly why it takes *sein* as its helper:
becoming is change of state in its purest form.

> **Ich bin müde geworden.** — "I got tired."

Notice what that sentence does. The adjective you learned with *sein* now
describes an arrival rather than a condition. German makes the difference with
the verb; English does it with *be* against *get*.

## Sounds you'll need
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GE-SOUND-FINAL-DEVOICING-D-01, GE-LEX-WERDEN-01] -->

- `w-as-v` — the **w** is English **v**, as in *war* and *wir*.
- `d-t-final` — and in **wird** ("he becomes") the final **d** devoices to a
  **t**, the same rule that turned *sind* into *zint*.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GE-LEX-WERDEN-01] -->

**werden** is a lost English verb — *weorthan*, "to turn, to come to be" — and
its bones are still in the language:

| English | the buried meaning |
|---|---|
| to**ward** | turning to |
| back**ward** | turning back |
| **worth** | what a thing turns out to be |

So *werden* means "to turn into," and every time you say *toward* you are using
the same root without knowing it.
