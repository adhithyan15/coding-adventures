Portuguese, chapter 6: Numbers One to Ten.
2 lessons. Both can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 2.
um, dois, três, quatro, cinco — counting to five.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Portuguese counts almost like its sister Spanish — but with two signature Portuguese twists: heavy nasal vowels, and a number that still changes for gender where Spanish long stopped. Spanish twins are beside each.

Sounds you'll need.
nasal-um — um is nasal: oong said softly through the nose, no real m. (The -m is just the spelling for nasality, as in sim, bom.)
open-e — três = trehs with an open e and the circumflex marking it.

The numbers, taken apart.
[a table of 5 rows, read aloud]
Portuguese from Latin: um (1) from ūnus. Spanish twin: uno. English cousins: unit, union, unique (and a/an, one).
Portuguese from Latin: dois (2) from duo. Spanish twin: dos. English cousins: duo, dual, double.
Portuguese from Latin: três (3) from trēs. Spanish twin: tres. English cousins: trio, triple, triangle.
Portuguese from Latin: quatro (4) from quattuor. Spanish twin: cuatro. English cousins: quart, quarter, quadrant.
Portuguese from Latin: cinco (5) from quīnque. Spanish twin: cinco. English cousins: quintet, quintessence.
Cinco is spelled identically to Spanish — the two closest sisters. But watch two.

Grammar Lens: um/uma and the gendered "two" (dois/duas).
um is also "a/an," gendered um (masc.) / uma (fem.): um dia, uma noite. That much matches Spanish. The surprise is two: Portuguese still inflects it — dois (masc.) / duas (fem.) — a direct survival of Latin duo (masc.) / duae (fem.). So it's dois dias but duas noites. Spanish levelled this to a single dos centuries ago; Portuguese kept the old split. The rest (três, quatro, cinco) don't change.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: "um, dois, três, quatro, cinco" — count up]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: the gendered two — "dois dias" (masc.) / "duas noites" (fem.)]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: each with its English cousin — "dois/duo, três/trio, cinco/quintet"]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Give 1–5 in Portuguese. (Um, dois, três, quatro, cinco.) Which number still changes for gender, and how? (Two — dois / duas, from Latin duo/duae.) What does um/uma mean besides "one"? ("A/an.") Next: 6–10, where a Portuguese sound-shift is on full display.


Lesson 2 of 2.
seis, sete, oito, nove, dez — and the calendar's secret.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
The rest of the way to ten. Like Spanish, Portuguese hides the calendar fact — setembro–dezembro are Latin for 7, 8, 9, 10 — and one number, oito, puts a signature Portuguese sound-shift on full display.

Sounds you'll need.
diphthong-oi — oito = OY-too, dois rhymes with it: the oi glide.
dez = dehs (open e); the final -z is a soft "sh"/"s" in Portugal, "s" in Brazil.

The numbers, taken apart.
[a table of 5 rows, read aloud]
Portuguese from Latin: seis (6) from sex. Spanish twin: seis. English cousins: sextet, sextant.
Portuguese from Latin: sete (7) from septem. Spanish twin: siete. English cousins: setembro (the 7th month!), septet.
Portuguese from Latin: oito (8) from octō. Spanish twin: ocho. English cousins: outubro (8th), octave, octopus.
Portuguese from Latin: nove (9) from novem. Spanish twin: nueve. English cousins: novembro (9th), novena.
Portuguese from Latin: dez (10) from decem. Spanish twin: diez. English cousins: dezembro (10th), decimal, dízimo.
The star is oito. Latin octō had a -ct- cluster, and Portuguese turned every Latin -ct- into -it-: octō becomes oito, noctem becomes noite ("night"), factum becomes feito ("done"). So oito, noite, and feito all carry the same fingerprint — once you spot the -it-, you can often guess the Latin word had a -ct-. (Spanish took a different path: octō becomes ocho, with -ct- becomes -ch-. Same cluster, two sisters, two solutions — oito vs ocho.)

Grammar Lens: why the months are "off by two".
Setembro means "7th," yet sits 9th. The old Roman year began in March, so September really was the seventh; julho and agosto (July, August) were inserted later and shoved the counting months down. The Latin numbers stuck to the month names. Every dezembro, you're saying Roman "ten" — and dez also gave Portuguese dízimo, a "tithe" (a tenth), the twin of English dime.

Guided Practice.

[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: "sete/setembro, oito/outubro, nove/novembro, dez/dezembro"]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: the -ct- becomes -it- set — "oito, noite, feito" — then Spanish "ocho"]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Give 6–10 in Portuguese. (Seis, sete, oito, nove, dez.) What Latin cluster became -it- in oito, and one more example? (-ct-; noite from noctem / feito from factum.) Why is dezembro Latin for "ten"? (The Roman year began in March.) Next chapter builds on this toward time and days.
