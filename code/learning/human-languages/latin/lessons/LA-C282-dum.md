# dum — while

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[LA-GRAMMAR-C282-ORDO-01, LA-GRAMMAR-C281-SCAENA-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say "first I walked to the forum". (*Prīmum ad forum ambulāvī.*)
Then a scene: "it was raining". (*Pluēbat.*)

## You'll want to know: dum
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[LA-LEX-C282-DUM-01]; assesses=[LA-LEX-C107-URBS-03, LA-GRAMMAR-C281-PERFECTUM-01] -->

**dum** — "while". It opens a stretch of time, and something happens inside
it:

> **Dum in forō ambulant, amīcam vīdērunt.** — While they were walking in
> the forum, they saw a friend.

[PAUSE 2s] The seeing is an event, so it is a perfect, *vīdērunt*. The
walking is the stretch it happened in, and *dum* holds it open.

## Grammar Lens: dum keeps the present
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[LA-GRAMMAR-C282-DUM-PRAESENS-02]; assesses=[LA-GRAMMAR-C281-IMPERFECTUM-01] -->

Listen to *ambulant*. The story is in the past, and the verb after *dum* is
the **present**: "they walk". When *dum* means "while", a past story usually
keeps the present after it, and English turns it into "were walking".

> **Dum mercātōrēs pānem vendunt, amīca vēnit.** — While the merchants were
> selling bread, my friend came.

[PAUSE 2s] Caesar writes it that way: *dum haec geruntur*, "while these
things are being done", and then the event in the perfect (Lewis & Short
quote it from his war in Gaul). In the main sentence, the scene still takes
the imperfect: *mercātōrēs pānem vendēbant*.

## The word, taken apart: dum
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[LA-LEX-C263-TEMP263-04] -->

The origin of **dum** is not settled. Lewis & Short connect it with
*diū*, "for a long time", the word whose own lesson traced it to the root
of *diēs*, "day"; EtymDB 2.1 traces *dum* to a Proto-Indo-European particle
*\*de*. Either way it is a small, old word, and its meaning has stayed
"for the stretch of time that".
