Arabic, chapter 8: Days of the Week.
2 lessons. Both can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 2.
الأحد to الخميس (al-aḥad to al-khamīs) — Sunday to Thursday, by the numbers.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Most European and North Indian languages name their weekdays for planet-gods. Arabic does something completely different: it just counts them.

Grammar Lens — The pattern: definite article + ordinal number.
Five of Arabic's seven days are simply "the [number]th" — the definite article ال (al-) plus an ordinal built on the number word:
[a table of 5 rows, read aloud]
Arabic: الأحد (al-aḥad). literally, and the number it is built on: "the first", from wāḥid, "one". day: Sunday.
Arabic: الإثنين (al-ithnayn). literally, and the number it is built on: "the second" (lit. "the two"), from ithnān, "two". day: Monday.
Arabic: الثلاثاء (ath-thulāthāʾ). literally, and the number it is built on: "the third", from thalātha, "three". day: Tuesday.
Arabic: الأربعاء (al-arbiʿāʾ). literally, and the number it is built on: "the fourth", from arbaʿa, "four". day: Wednesday.
Arabic: الخميس (al-khamīs). literally, and the number it is built on: "the fifth", from khamsa, "five". day: Thursday.
No moon, no war-god, no planets at all — just day one, day two, day three… This also tells you something about the week itself: counting starts from Sunday, so Sunday is "day one" — the Arabic week, like the Jewish and much of the Christian calendar tradition, begins where the European Monday-first convention doesn't.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: "al-aḥad" — the first, Sunday]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: count up — "al-ithnayn, ath-thulāthāʾ, al-arbiʿāʾ, al-khamīs" — second through fifth]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: contrast — no planet-gods here, just numbers]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
How does Arabic name Sunday through Thursday? (As ordinal numbers — "the first" through "the fifth," not planet-gods.) Which number is Sunday, and what does that tell you about the week? ("The first" — the Arabic week counts starting from Sunday.) What Arabic prefix turns a number into "the [x]th day"? (ال al-, the definite article.)


Lesson 2 of 2.
الجمعة and السبت (al-jumʿa, as-sabt) — Friday and Saturday.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Five days counted off — and then two days important enough to get real names instead of numbers.

You'll want to know — The two named days.
الجمعة (al-jumʿa) = "the gathering," from جمع (jamaʿa), "to gather, collect." This is the day of the congregational Friday prayer (ṣalāt al-jumʿa) — the most religiously significant day of the Islamic week, and important enough to earn its own name rather than a number.
السبت (as-sabt) = "the Sabbath."

You'll want to know — Be honest about how السبت differs from Latin's Sabbatum.
You may remember Latin borrowed the word Sabbatum from Hebrew to replace its own pagan diēs Saturnī — a loanword crossing from a Semitic language into an Italic one. Arabic's سبت (sabt) is a different kind of relationship entirely: Arabic is itself a Semitic language, in the same family as Hebrew. So as-sabt and Hebrew shabbāt aren't borrower-and-lender — they're cousins, both descending from the same ancient Semitic root س-ب-ت meaning "to rest, to cease." Where Latin borrowed a foreign word, Arabic simply inherited a family word.

[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: the contrast — Latin borrowed "Sabbatum"; Arabic's "sabt" is a blood relative of Hebrew "shabbāt"]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Why does الجمعة break the counting pattern? (It's named for the congregational Friday prayer — "the gathering" — not counted as a number.) How is Arabic's السبت different from Latin's borrowed Sabbatum? (Arabic and Hebrew are both Semitic languages — as-sabt and shabbāt are cousins from a shared root, not a loanword crossing between unrelated language families.)
