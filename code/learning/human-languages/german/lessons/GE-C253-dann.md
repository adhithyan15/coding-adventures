# dann — and the verb comes next

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GE-LEX-C171-TIME171-02, GE-LEX-C171-TIME171-03] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say "first" and "afterwards". (*Zuerst. Danach.*)

[PAUSE 1s] A story needs one more step word between those two: **dann**,
"then".

## Grammar Lens: the step word, then the verb
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[GE-LEX-DANN-01, GE-GRAMMAR-DANN-VERB-ZWEI-02]; assesses=[GE-GRAMMAR-VERB-SECOND-02, GE-GRAMMAR-PRAETERITUM-STARK-01, GE-LEX-C64-VERBEN1-01] -->

In a German statement the verb is the second piece. When a step word takes
the first place, the verb comes straight after it, and the subject moves
behind the verb:

> **Zuerst** *kaufte ich einen Kaffee.* — First I bought a coffee.
> **Dann** *las ich die Nachricht.* — Then I read the message.
> **Danach** *ging ich.* — Afterwards I went.

English keeps "I" in front: "then I read". German says *dann las ich*, not
*dann ich las*. The verb holds its place, and the subject gives way.

*Und* does not take a place of its own. After *und dann*, the verb still
follows *dann*: *… und dann kam der Zug.*

*Dann* and *danach* overlap. *Dann* is the next step, and the shortest way
on. *Danach* leans on the time: after that.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GE-LEX-DENN-01] -->

**dann** ← Old High German *danne* ← Proto-Germanic *\*þan*, "at that
time" (EtymDB 2.1), which EtymDB sets beside English *then*. In chapter
forty-eight you met its twin, **denn**, "because": the two were once one
word, and German gave *dann* the when and *denn* the why.
