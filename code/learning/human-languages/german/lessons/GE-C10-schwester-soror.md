# Schwester and soror

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GE-LEX-SCHWESTER-01, GE-ETYMON-BRUDER-FRATER-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] The brother's first sound split the two branches. The sister's did
not split anything.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[GE-ETYMON-SCHWESTER-SOROR-01]; assesses=[GE-LEX-SCHWESTER-01, GE-SOUND-SCHW-01] -->

The ancient word is reconstructed as *\*swésōr*, and all three descendants keep
its **s**:

| language | the word | the first sound |
|---|---|---|
| German | *Schwester* | *sch* is a later German dressing on an **s** |
| English | *sister* | plain **s** |
| Latin | *soror* | plain **s** |

Grimm's law had nothing to do to an *s*. It moved the stops — the *p*s, *t*s and
*b*s — and left this one where it stood, which is why the sister is the one
family word whose front sound matches across the whole family.

## Why it's said this way
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GE-ETYMON-SCHWESTER-SOROR-01, GE-ETYMON-BRUDER-FRATER-01] -->

Latin *soror* is behind French *sœur*, and behind English **sorority** — which
English borrowed back from Latin centuries after it already had *sister* from
the same root. The two came in by different doors and nobody noticed they were
relatives.

That is the ordinary condition of a big vocabulary: the same ancient word
arriving twice, once inherited and once bought, sitting in the dictionary
without recognising each other.
