Portuguese, chapter 5: The First Verbs.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
falar — "to speak," and the f that Spanish lost.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Until now you've assembled fixed phrases. Falar (“to speak”) is your first real verb and the model for the large -ar family. Its first letter also hides a beautiful contrast with Spanish.

Sounds you'll need.
final-r — falar = fah-LAR, tapped final r. The stem is fal-.

The word, taken apart.
falar comes from Latin fabulārī, "to chat, to tell tales" — from fābula, "a story" ( becomes English fable, fabulous, confabulate). The same Latin verb as Spanish hablar — but here's the twist:
Portuguese kept the Latin f-; Spanish turned f- into h-. So the two "speak" verbs diverge at letter one, and the pattern runs through the whole vocabulary:
[a table of 5 rows, read aloud]
Latin (f-): fabulārī. Portuguese (f-): falar. Spanish (h-, silent): hablar.
Latin (f-): farīna. Portuguese (f-): farinha (flour). Spanish (h-, silent): harina.
Latin (f-): fīlius. Portuguese (f-): filho (son). Spanish (h-, silent): hijo.
Latin (f-): facere. Portuguese (f-): fazer (to do). Spanish (h-, silent): hacer.
Latin (f-): ferrum. Portuguese (f-): ferro (iron). Spanish (h-, silent): hierro.
So when a Spanish word starts with a silent h, its Portuguese twin usually still has the honest Latin f — hijo/filho, hacer/fazer. Portuguese is the more conservative sister here.

Grammar Lens: the -ar present tense (pro-drop).
Drop -ar to get fal-, then add the endings:
[a table of 5 rows, read aloud]
person: (eu). ending: -o. falar, which gives:: falo (I speak).
person: (tu). ending: -as. falar, which gives:: falas (you speak).
person: (você/ele/ela). ending: -a. falar, which gives:: fala.
person: (nós). ending: -amos. falar, which gives:: falamos.
person: (vocês/eles). ending: -am. falar, which gives:: falam.
Like Spanish and Italian, Portuguese drops the subject pronoun — falo already says "I," so no eu needed. (Portuguese joins the drop camp: ES, PT, IT drop; FR, DE keep.)

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
What Latin verb is falar from, and which Spanish verb is its twin? (fabulārī — Spanish hablar.) What sound-law separates them? (Portuguese kept f-; Spanish shifted f- becomes h-: filho/hijo.) Does Portuguese keep or drop the pronoun? (Drops it.) Next: morar, "to live."


Lesson 2 of 5.
morar — "to live," i.e. to linger somewhere.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
A second -ar verb — and a distinctive Portuguese choice. Where its sisters say "live" with habitāre ("keep having a place"), everyday Portuguese uses morar — from a verb that means to linger.

Sounds you'll need.
morar = moh-RAR, tapped final r, clean vowels.

The word, taken apart.
morar comes from Latin morārī, "to delay, to linger, to tarry, to stay a while." To dwell somewhere is, in Portuguese, to linger there — a softer, more temporary image than "keeping" a place. That morārī root survives in English in a couple of unexpected words:
moratorium — an official delay (a pause on payments or actions).
demur — to linger / hesitate, to raise an objection (without demur).
(Portuguese does also have habitar — the habitāre verb its sisters use — but it's formal; morar is what you actually say: Onde você mora?, "Where do you live?")

Grammar Lens: same -ar template.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
(eu) moro, (tu) moras, (você) mora, (-o / -as / -a).
moramos, moram, blank.
Moro em Lisboa. — "I live in Lisbon."

Lesson 3 of 5.
trabalhar — "to work," and the torture returns.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
A third -ar verb — and Portuguese rejoins its Iberian sister on one of the darkest etymologies anywhere. trabalhar ("to work"), like Spanish trabajar, began as a word for torture.

Sounds you'll need.
nasal-anha — the -lh- is a ly glide (as in Spanish ll): trabalhar = tra-ba-LYAR.

The word, taken apart.
trabalhar comes from Vulgar Latin tripaliāre, "to torture" — from tripalium, a Roman instrument of torture made of three stakes (tri- "three" + pālus "stake" becomes English pale, impale). Torture becomes toil becomes work, and English took the same road: travail (painful effort) becomes travel (a journey was an ordeal).
So Portuguese and Spanish are twins here — trabalhar and trabajar, both from tripaliāre. Only the middle consonant differs: Latin -li- became Portuguese -lh- (trabalhar) and Spanish -j- (trabajar). And this puts the "work" verbs of the five languages into two neat camps:
Torture (tripalium): Portuguese trabalhar, Spanish trabajar, French travailler.
Labour (labor): Italian lavorare.
Three languages remember work as torture; only Italy remembers it as labour.

Grammar Lens: same -ar template.
[a table of 1 row, read aloud]
(eu) trabalho, (tu) trabalhas, (você) trabalha, (-o / -as / -a).
Trabalho no Porto. — "I work in Porto."

[pause 3 seconds]

Lesson 4 of 5.
Falo português — your first real sentence.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
The payoff: you take a verb you conjugated and a noun, and build a sentence no one handed you — Falo português, "I speak Portuguese." (Falo covers both "I speak" and "I'm speaking.")

You'll want to know: The new word: português.
português = "Portuguese," from Portus Cale — a Roman-era port at the mouth of the Douro river (today's Porto / Oporto). Portus is Latin "port, harbour" ( becomes English port, portal, import); Cale is older and debated — perhaps Celtic for "harbour/shelter," or Latin cālidus "warm." So Portugal began as a single harbour town, whose name spread to the whole country and its language. Say it por-too-GESH (final -s = sh in Portugal).

You'll want to know: The sentence, assembled.
Falo (I speak, from falar) + português = Falo português.
A complete, grammatical Portuguese sentence. As before:
No eu. The -o of falo says "I" — Portuguese drops the pronoun (like Spanish and Italian).
No article on the language: falo português.
With this, all five languages you've been building now cross the same line — from reciting phrases to making sentences:
[a table of 5 rows, read aloud]
Spanish. "I speak/learn [language]": Hablo español.
Portuguese. "I speak/learn [language]": Falo português.
Italian. "I speak/learn [language]": Parlo italiano.
French. "I speak/learn [language]": Je parle français.
German. "I speak/learn [language]": Ich lerne Deutsch.
Swap the pieces: Moro em Lisboa., Trabalho no Porto., Você fala português?

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: "Falo português" — FAH-loo por-too-GESH]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: drop the eu — Portuguese doesn't need it]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: "português" from Portus Cale, a single harbour town that named a nation]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Where does português come from? (Portus Cale, a port at the Douro's mouth becomes Porto.) In Falo português, why no eu and no article? (The -o is "I"; languages take no article.) Which three of the five languages drop the pronoun? (Spanish, Portuguese, Italian.) Next: practice.


Lesson 5 of 5.
Practice — making sentences with -ar verbs.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
For the first time you're building Portuguese sentences from a pattern. Drill the three verbs until the endings are automatic — and remember, Portuguese drops the eu.

Guided Practice: conjugate on command.
[pause 1 second]
Run each through eu / tu / você (pronoun dropped in speech):
falar: falo, falas, fala.
morar: moro, moras, mora.
trabalhar: trabalho, trabalhas, trabalha.
Same endings every time (-o / -as / -a), no pronoun needed.

Guided Practice: say something real.
— Você fala português? — Falo um pouco. E você? — Também. Onde você mora? — Moro em Lisboa, e trabalho no Porto. — Obrigado! — De nada.

What you've built this chapter.
the regular -ar present tense — drop -ar, add -o/-as/-a/-amos/-am; pronoun dropped (like Spanish, Italian).
falar ( from fabulārī becomes fable; twin of Spanish hablar but with the f kept), morar ( from morārī "to linger" becomes moratorium — a root no sibling uses), trabalhar ( from tripalium "torture"; twin of trabajar).
Falo português ( from Portus Cale, a harbour town) — first self-assembled sentence, and the fifth language to cross from phrases into sentences.

Wrap-up Recall.

What are the three singular -ar endings? (-o / -as / -a.) Give the "I" forms of the three verbs. (Falo, moro, trabalho.) falar vs Spanish hablar — what's the sound-law difference? (Portuguese kept f-; Spanish shifted f becomes h.) Portuguese now moves in real sentences — this book has handed over rules, not phrases.
