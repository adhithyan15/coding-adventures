Lesson 1 of 2.
il pane — bread, and its companions.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
il pane ("bread") — and, true to form, Italian keeps it closest to Latin: pānis becomes pane, barely touched. Its root carries the same warm story as the French pain: a companion is one you share bread with.

Taken apart.
il pane ("bread") from Latin pānis — masculine, il pane; the plural is i pani.

The word, taken apart: bread family.
companion / company from com- ("with") + pānis — "one you break bread with."
pantry, pannier (a bread-basket).
And a purely Italian gem: companatico — the word for "whatever you eat with bread" (cheese, salami, anything on it). The bread is the given; the companatico is what joins it — the same com- + pānis idea, alive in one Italian word.

Lesson 2 of 2.
l'acqua, il vino — water and wine, close to the source.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
The Italian table's two drinks — and a neat contrast with French. Where French dissolved Latin aqua into a bare eau ("oh"), Italian kept it almost whole: acqua, with the aqua still ringing in it.

The word, taken apart: l'acqua.
l'acqua ("water") from Latin aqua. Italian barely moved it — it even doubles the sound with -cq- (AH-kwa), holding onto the hard qu that French threw away. Set them side by side: Latin aqua becomes Italian acqua (kept) vs French eau (worn to a vowel). English borrowed the same loud original for aquatic, aquarium, aqueduct. Takes l' before the vowel.

The word, taken apart: vino.
il vino ("wine") from Latin vīnum — clean and close, like everything Italian. The same word travelled into English as wine, and gives vine, vinegar, vintage, vineyard, vinyl. Masculine: il vino.
