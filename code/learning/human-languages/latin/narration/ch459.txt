Lesson 1 of 5.
porticus, porticūs — a colonnade, a covered walk, a portico.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a sewer, then the Latin for a water tower.

You'll want to know: porticus, porticūs.
porticus, porticūs — "a colonnade, a covered walk, a portico".
Aestāte cīvēs in porticū ambulant, quod ibi umbra est. In summer the citizens walk in the colonnade, because there is shade there.
Fourth declension, feminine: porticus, porticūs f. English portico and porch come from it.

The word, taken apart.
porticus is built on porta 'gate, passage' (EtymDB): a covered passage. English portico comes from it through Italian, and porch through French.

Lesson 2 of 5.
aedēs, aedis — a temple; (plural) a house.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a water tower, then the Latin for a colonnade.

You'll want to know: aedēs, aedis.
aedēs, aedis — "a temple; (plural) a house".
In forō aedēs magna et antīqua est. In the forum there is a great old temple.
Third declension, feminine: aedēs or aedis, aedis f. In the singular a temple, a house of a god; in the plural a house of people, with all its rooms.

The word, taken apart.
aedēs is usually traced to PIE h₂eydʰ- 'to burn', since Lewis & Short give 'hearth' as an early sense; aestās 'summer' is from the same root. English edifice comes from aedificium, built on it.

Lesson 3 of 5.
aedicula, aediculae — a shrine, a small temple; a niche.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a colonnade, then the Latin for a temple.

You'll want to know: aedicula, aediculae.
aedicula, aediculae — "a shrine, a small temple; a niche".
In compitō aedicula parva est, et viātōrēs ibi flōrēs pōnunt. At the crossroads there is a small shrine, and travellers put flowers there.
First declension, feminine: aedicula, aediculae f., a diminutive of aedēs. A little shrine, or a niche for a statue.

The word, taken apart.
aedicula is a diminutive of aedēs 'temple, house' (Lewis & Short), made with -cula. English keeps aedicule in architecture.

Lesson 4 of 5.
pōmērium, pōmēriī — the sacred boundary (of a city).

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a temple, then the Latin for a shrine.

You'll want to know: pōmērium, pōmēriī.
pōmērium, pōmēriī — "the sacred boundary (of a city)".
Intrā pōmērium mīlitēs arma nōn gerunt. Inside the sacred boundary soldiers do not carry weapons.
Second declension, neuter: pōmērium, pōmēriī n., also pōmoerium. The holy line round a Roman town, marked with stones.

The word, taken apart.
pōmērium is usually explained, as by ancient writers, as post 'behind' and moerus, an old form of mūrus 'wall' (Lewis & Short): the strip of land along the wall. EtymDB lists it without a parent.

Lesson 5 of 5.
exstruō, exstruere — to build up, to raise, to pile up.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a shrine, then the Latin for the sacred boundary.

You'll want to know: exstruō, exstruere.
exstruō, exstruere — "to build up, to raise, to pile up".
Cīvēs novam porticum in forō exstruunt. The citizens put up a new colonnade in the forum.
exstruō, exstruere, exstrūxī, exstrūctum. Also to pile up: mēnsās epulīs exstruere, to load tables with food.

The word, taken apart.
exstruō is built from ex- 'up, out' and struō 'to pile up, to build' (EtymDB; Lewis & Short). English construct and structure come from struō.
