# دست — hand, and a sound law that hides a cousin in plain sight

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[FA-LEX-CHESHM] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say **چشم** (*cheshm*). This lesson's word looks nothing like its closest
Indo-Iranian relative — and that mismatch is not an accident.

## You'll want to know first — one word
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[FA-LEX-DAST]; assesses=[] -->

> **دست** — *dast* — **hand**

Three letters, all already yours: **د س ت** — *d*, *s*, *t*.

## The word, taken apart — one root, three disguises
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[FA-ETYMON-DAST]; assesses=[] -->

**دست** continues Old Persian **dastā-**, from Indo-European \**ǵʰes-to-*,
“hand.” Sanskrit's word for hand, **hasta**, is the very same root — and so,
further out, is Greek **kheír**, which English kept indirectly in
**surgery** (literally “hand-work”) and **chiropractic** (“done by hand”).

Old Persian's **d**, Avestan's **z**, and Sanskrit's **h** look like three
unrelated sounds, but they are the completely regular Indo-Iranian outcome of
the same ancestral sound in each of the three branches — the kind of
correspondence a false cousin never has, and a real one always does.
