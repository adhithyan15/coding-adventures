# Synthesis — real conditions

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-GRAMMAR-SI-REAL-CONDITION, ES-GRAMMAR-SI-FUTURE-RESULT] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Which tense after *si*? (**Present**, always.) Which after the comma?
(**Present or future** — your choice of meaning.)

## What you've built
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-GRAMMAR-SI-REAL-CONDITION, ES-GRAMMAR-SI-FUTURE-RESULT, ES-GRAMMAR-TENER-PRESENT-SINGULAR, ES-GRAMMAR-AR-FUTURE-SINGULAR] -->

Two chapters, one word, and a sentence shape you did not have before:

| | |
|---|---|
| *Si tengo tiempo, hablo español.* | a habit — whenever it happens |
| *Si tengo tiempo, hablaré español.* | a plan — this once |
| *Si llueve, comeré en casa.* | a decision waiting on the weather |

**This is the first thing in the book that needs two clauses to say.** Every
sentence before it was one thought. A condition is two, hooked together — and
the hook cost you no new grammar at all, only a word and a rule about where the
future may stand.

## Grammar Lens: what a condition is for
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-GRAMMAR-SI-FUTURE-RESULT] -->

A condition lets you talk about a world you are not claiming is real yet. That
is a genuinely new power, and it is the doorway to the rest of this book: once
you can suppose, you can also suppose things that are **not** true — *if I had
time* — and that needs a verb form you do not have yet.

[PAUSE 2s] You will meet it soon. For now the supposing is real, and real
supposing is most of what anyone says all day.
