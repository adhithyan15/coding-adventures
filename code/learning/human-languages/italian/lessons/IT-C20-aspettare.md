# aspettare — "to wait," which began as "to watch"

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[IT-LEX-COMPRARE-02, IT-ETYMON-COMPRARE-04, IT-LEX-PORTARE-02] -->

[PAUSE 2s] A third *-are* verb. Its endings are free, its root is one of the most
productive in English, and the doubled *tt* in the middle is the work of a
sound-law you learned while counting to ten.

## You'll want to know: the word
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[IT-LEX-ASPETTARE-02]; assesses=[IT-GRAMMAR-PORTARE-04] -->

> **aspettare** — to wait, to wait **for**.

> **Aspetto.** — I'm waiting.
> **Aspetto Anna.** — I'm waiting for Anna.
> **Aspetto Marco.** — I'm waiting for Marco.

Notice what the Italian is missing: any word meaning **for**. English cannot
"wait somebody." Italian takes the person straight, as *porto il pane* takes the
bread.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[IT-ETYMON-ASPETTARE-03]; assesses=[IT-ETYMON-COMPRARE-03] -->

**aspettare** comes from Latin **spectāre**, *"to watch, to gaze at"*, with
**ad-**, "toward," welded on the front: to watch **toward** something. Waiting is
watching for a thing to arrive, and the root runs through English:

- **spectator**, **spectacle**, **spectacles**, **spectrum**.
- **inspect** ("look into"), **respect** ("look back at"), **prospect** ("look
  forward"), **suspect** ("look from underneath"), **aspect**.
- **species** and **specimen**, from the plain verb *specere*, "to look."

Classical Latin's own word for waiting was **exspectāre** — *ex-* plus that same
verb, "to watch out for" — and English took **that** one and made it **expect**.
So *wait* and *expect* are one Latin verb wearing two prefixes, and Italian's
single *aspettare* stands on the ground of both.

## Grammar Lens: the doubled t, and the word that narrows the meaning
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[IT-GRAMMAR-ASPETTARE-04]; assesses=[IT-GRAMMAR-SCRIVERE-03, IT-ETYMON-NUMERI-6-10-03, IT-GRAMMAR-PASSATO-PROSSIMO-02, IT-GRAMMAR-MI-CHIAMO-04, IT-LEX-MI-PIACE-02, IT-GRAMMAR-MI-PIACE-03] -->

Present: **aspetto, aspetti, aspetta**. Past: **ho aspettato**, regular.

First that *tt*. Latin *spectāre* carries *-ct-*, and you know what Italian does
with *-ct-*: *octō* became **otto**, *lectum* **letto**, *scriptum* **scritto**.
The same law gives **aspettare**.

Second, the narrowing. Put **mi** in front and the verb stops meaning *wait* and
starts meaning *expect*:

| Italian | English |
|---|---|
| **Aspetto.** | I'm waiting. |
| **Mi aspetto.** | I expect. |

That **mi** is a word you have met working elsewhere. In **mi chiamo** it turned
the verb back on you — "I call *myself*." In **mi piace** it named the person a
thing is pleasing *to*. Here it turns outward watching into inward reckoning.
