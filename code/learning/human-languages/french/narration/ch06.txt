un, deux, trois, quatre, cinq — counting to five.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Numbers are the most-reused words in any language, and French — like its sibling Spanish — inherited them almost unchanged from Latin. So you already half-know them from English, and you'll see the Spanish twin beside each.

Sounds you'll need.
nasal-un — un is a nasal vowel, œ̃, close to "uh" said through the nose; no n is actually pronounced.
vowel-eu — deux = deuh (rounded eu), the x is silent.

The numbers, taken apart.
There is a table here I cannot read to you — 4 columns and 5 rows, and it has more columns than can be held in the ear at once. Its columns are: French, from Latin, Spanish twin, English cousins. Come back and look at it when you have stopped.
Latin ūnus, duo, trēs barely changed on the way into French and Spanish alike — trois and tres are the same Roman trēs, just worn two different ways.

Grammar Lens: un becomes un / une (one = a/an).
un doubles as the article "a/an," and it takes gender: un (masc.) / une (fem.) — un café "a/one coffee," une bière "a/one beer." French makes no difference between "one" and "a," exactly like Spanish un/una. The others (deux, trois…) don't change.

Lesson 2 of 2.
six, sept, huit, neuf, dix — and the calendar's secret.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, because the lesson points at something written down and your eyes for one table that cannot be read aloud. You can listen to everything else now — leave the section called The numbers, taken apart until you have stopped, and I will say so again when we reach it.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
The rest of the way to ten. These four hide the same fact the English and Spanish numbers do: septembre–décembre are just Latin for 7, 8, 9, 10. And huit shows how violently French can wear a Latin word down.

Sounds you'll need.
six and dix end in a -s sound when counting (sees, dees) but go silent before a consonant (six livres = see livres) — a liaison quirk.
neuf = nuhf; its f turns to a v sound before a vowel (neuf ans = neu-v-ans).

The numbers, taken apart.
There is a table here I cannot read to you — 4 columns and 5 rows, and it has more columns than can be held in the ear at once. Its columns are: French, from Latin, Spanish twin, English cousins. Come back and look at it when you have stopped.
Look at huit: Latin octō lost its c, softened, and grew an h- prop — octō becomes oit becomes huit. The number is unrecognizable, but octobre keeps the old oct- intact, so the 8 is still visible in the month. And dix gave English dime (a tenth of a dollar), through Old French disme from decima "a tenth."

Grammar Lens: why the months are "off by two".
Septembre means "7th," yet it's the 9th month. Why? The old Roman year began in March, so September really was the seventh. Julius and Augustus later inserted juillet and août (named for themselves), shoving the counting months out of place — but the Latin numbers stuck. Every décembre, you're saying Roman "ten." (Identical to the Spanish story — diciembre from decem too.)
