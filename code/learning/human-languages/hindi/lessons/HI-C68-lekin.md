# लेकिन (*lekin*) — "but"

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[HI-JOIN-AUR-01, HI-JOIN-YA-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Two joining words are yours: *aur* adds, **या** offers. Both are
agreeable. Neither can disagree with anything, and until one of them can, every
sentence you build only ever confirms itself.

## You'll want to know: लेकिन
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[HI-JOIN-LEKIN-01]; assesses=[HI-CONCEPT-C02-HAI-01, HI-CONCEPT-C07-NAHIN-01] -->

> **लेकिन** — *lekin* — **but**

> **चाय है लेकिन दूध नहीं है.** — *chāy hai lekin dūdh nahīṁ hai.* —
> **There is tea but there is no milk.**

**लेकिन** takes the same slot as the other two and does the one thing they
cannot: it lets the second half push back against the first. Notice that neither
half changed — **नहीं** is doing its own separate work inside the second clause,
and **लेकिन** only says how the two halves stand to each other.

Every letter in it is one you have: **ल**, **े**, **क**, **ि**, **न**.

**लेकिन** is Arabic — **لكن** (*lākin*) — and it walked into Hindi through
Persian, in the same company as **शुक्रिया**. Beside it Hindi keeps the
inherited **पर**, from Sanskrit **परम्**, "beyond." Two words for *but*, one
Persianate and one Sanskritic, is exactly what this language looks like: it
almost never replaced a word, it collected a second one.
