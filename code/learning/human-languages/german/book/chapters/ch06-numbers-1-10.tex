\chapter{Numbers One to Ten}
\label{ch:numbers-1-10}

Here is where German feels \emph{closer to English than any Romance language
does}. German's numbers are not borrowed from Latin --- they are German's own
Germanic words, and they are the near-twins of English \emph{one, two, three,
four, five}. One sound-law explains every difference.
\emph{Practice lessons: \texttt{lessons/GE-C06-*.md}.}

\section{\emph{eins, zwei, drei, vier, f\"{u}nf} --- counting to five}
\label{lesson:zahlen-1-5}

\begin{sounds}
\emph{eins}, \emph{zwei}, \emph{drei} all carry the \emph{ei} $=$ English
``eye'' sound: \emph{eyns, tsvy, dry}.\\[3pt]
\emph{f\"{u}nf}: the \"{u} is ``ee'' said with rounded lips; \emph{f\"{u}nf}
$\approx$ \emph{fewnf}.
\end{sounds}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}llll@{}}
\toprule
German & English twin & shared ancestor & Latin cousin \\
\midrule
\emph{eins} (1) & \textbf{one} (and \emph{a/an}) & \emph{*\'{o}ynos} & \emph{\={u}nus} \\
\emph{zwei} (2) & \textbf{two} (\emph{tw-} $\to$ \emph{zw-}) & \emph{*dw\'{o}h}\ensuremath{_1} & \emph{duo} \\
\emph{drei} (3) & \textbf{three} (\emph{th-} $\to$ \emph{d-}) & \emph{*tr\'{e}yes} & \emph{tr\={e}s} \\
\emph{vier} (4) & \textbf{four} & \emph{*k\ensuremath{^w}etw\'{o}res} & \emph{quattuor} \\
\emph{f\"{u}nf} (5) & \textbf{five} (\emph{f\ldots f}) & \emph{*p\'{e}nk\ensuremath{^w}e} & \emph{qu\={\i}nque} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{cousinweb}
German and English are \textbf{siblings}; Latin is a \textbf{cousin}. So
\emph{drei} is not from Latin \emph{tr\={e}s} --- both descend from one
prehistoric word, and \textbf{Grimm's Law} is the recipe for the differences:
an old \textbf{\emph{t}} became Germanic \textbf{\emph{th/d}} (\emph{tr\={e}s}
$\to$ \textbf{three} $\to$ \emph{drei}), and an old \textbf{\emph{p}} became
\textbf{\emph{f}} (\emph{*p\'{e}nk\ensuremath{^w}e} $\to$ \textbf{five} /
\emph{f\"{u}nf}, while Latin kept it as \emph{qu\={\i}nque}). Say
\emph{f\"{u}nf} and \textbf{five} back to back --- same word, one
\emph{p}-turned-\emph{f} apart.
\end{cousinweb}

\begin{grammarlens}
\textbf{\emph{eins} $\to$ \emph{ein} / \emph{eine} (one $=$ a/an).} Like English
\emph{one/an} and Spanish \emph{uno/un}, German \textbf{eins} becomes the
article \textbf{ein} (masculine/neuter) / \textbf{eine} (feminine) before a
noun: \emph{ein Kaffee} ``a/one coffee,'' \emph{eine Katze} ``a/one cat.'' The
counting form is \emph{eins}; the article drops the \emph{-s}. The other
numbers do not change for the article.
\end{grammarlens}

\section[\emph{sechs} to \emph{zehn}]{\emph{sechs, sieben, acht, neun, zehn} --- finishing the count}
\label{lesson:zahlen-6-10}

The rest of the way to ten --- and again, each is the twin of its English
number, with Latin as the cousin one sound-shift away. Two of them show Grimm's
Law beautifully.

\begin{sounds}
\emph{acht}: the \emph{ch} is the raspy back-of-throat sound (as in
\emph{Bach}): \emph{akht}. It matches the \emph{gh} in English \emph{eight} ---
both were once a real throat sound.\\[3pt]
\emph{neun} $=$ \emph{noyn} (the \emph{eu} $=$ ``oy'').
\end{sounds}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}llll@{}}
\toprule
German & English twin & shared ancestor & Latin cousin \\
\midrule
\emph{sechs} (6) & \textbf{six} & \emph{*sw\'{e}\'{k}s} & \emph{sex} \\
\emph{sieben} (7) & \textbf{seven} & \emph{*septm} & \emph{septem} \\
\emph{acht} (8) & \textbf{eight} & \emph{*o\'{k}t\={o}} & \emph{oct\={o}} \\
\emph{neun} (9) & \textbf{nine} & \emph{*n\'{e}wn} & \emph{novem} \\
\emph{zehn} (10) & \textbf{ten} & \emph{*d\'{e}\'{k}m} & \emph{decem} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{etymology}
Two showpieces of \textbf{Grimm's Law}:\\[3pt]
\emph{zehn} $\leftrightarrow$ \textbf{ten} $\leftrightarrow$ \emph{decem}: an
old \textbf{\emph{d}} became Germanic \textbf{\emph{t}} --- so Latin kept
\emph{decem}, English shifted to \textbf{ten}, and German went one hop further
(\emph{t} $\to$ \emph{z}) to \emph{zehn}. Same number, three snapshots of one
consonant drifting.\\[3pt]
\emph{acht} $\leftrightarrow$ \textbf{eight} $\leftrightarrow$
\emph{Nacht}/\textbf{night}: the German \emph{-cht-} answers English
\emph{-ght-} every time (\emph{acht}/\textbf{eight},
\emph{Nacht}/\textbf{night}, \emph{Licht}/\textbf{light}) --- a fossil of a
throat sound both languages once had.
\end{etymology}

\begin{grammarlens}
\textbf{German borrowed the \emph{months}, not the numbers.} English and German
both took their \textbf{month names straight from Latin} --- \emph{September,
Oktober, November, Dezember} in German too. So the same ``off by two'' trick
works: those months mean Latin \textbf{7, 8, 9, 10} (the Roman year began in
March), even though German's own numbers \emph{sieben/acht/neun/zehn} look
nothing like them. The numbers are homegrown; the month labels are Latin
imports.
\end{grammarlens}

Give 1--10 in German: \emph{eins, zwei, drei, vier, f\"{u}nf, sechs, sieben,
acht, neun, zehn}. None of them is borrowed from Latin --- they are Germanic
\textbf{twins} of the English numbers, cousins of Latin via \textbf{Grimm's
Law}. Next chapter builds on this toward the days of the week, and after that,
telling the time.
