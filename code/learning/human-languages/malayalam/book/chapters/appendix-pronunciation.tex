\section*{The three facts}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Consonants carry a built-in ``a.''} \ml{ക} = ka, \ml{മ} = ma,
    \ml{ര} = ra.
  \item \textbf{A vowel sign changes that built-in vowel.} \ml{കാ} = kā,
    \ml{കി} = ki, \ml{കീ} = kī, \ml{കു} = ku, \ml{കൂ} = kū, \ml{കെ} = ke,
    \ml{കേ} = kē, \ml{കൊ} = ko, \ml{കോ} = kō. (The \emph{e/o} signs are written
    \emph{before} their consonant but read after.)
  \item \textbf{The \emph{chandrakkala} strips the built-in vowel, and bare
    consonants join as conjuncts.} \ml{സ} + \ml{ക} $\to$ \ml{സ്ക} (ska),
    \ml{ന} + \ml{ദ} $\to$ \ml{ന്ദ} (nda), \ml{ല} + \ml{ല} $\to$ \ml{ല്ല}
    (lla). A circle \textbf{anusvāram} \ml{ം} adds a final nasal.
\end{enumerate}

\section*{Worth flagging}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The largest Dravidian alphabet.} Because Malayalam borrowed so
    heavily from Sanskrit, it keeps the full Sanskrit inventory --- aspirates
    (\ml{ഖ} kh, \ml{ധ} dh\ldots) and all three sibilants (\ml{ശ} ś, \ml{ഷ} ṣ,
    \ml{സ} s) --- alongside native Dravidian letters Tamil also has (the
    retroflex \ml{ള} ḷ, \ml{ഴ} ḻ, \ml{ണ} ṇ).
  \item \textbf{Rounded forms, many conjuncts.} Malayalam's curved letters and
    rich stock of stacked/ligated conjuncts give it a distinctive dense look.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Malayalam in the Dravidian family}

\textbf{Dravidian} is a family entirely separate from Indo-European (English,
Latin, Sanskrit). Malayalam is the \emph{closest sister of Tamil} --- the two
split from one language only about a thousand years ago --- so its everyday core
is strikingly Tamil-like (\emph{nandi}, \emph{illa}, \emph{śari}, and the
\emph{pōyi varām} farewell). At the same time it absorbed a vast Sanskrit layer,
which is why its formal greeting (\emph{namaskāram}) and its full letter-set are
Sanskritised. The lessons trace, word by word, what is inherited Dravidian and
what is borrowed.
