\chapter{To Be, and the Past That Takes It}
\label{ch:sein}

\emph{sein}, ``to be'' --- the partner of \emph{haben}, and the most irregular
verb in German, because it is not really one verb at all but three ancient ones
sharing a paradigm. Then the second half of the past tense: the family of verbs
that builds its perfect on \emph{sein} instead of \emph{haben}.
\emph{Practice lessons: \texttt{lessons/GE-C16-*.md}.}

\section{\emph{sein} --- ``to be,'' three verbs under one roof}
\label{lesson:sein}

\begin{sounds}
\emph{sein} $=$ \emph{zine}: German \emph{s} before a vowel is English ``z,'' and
\emph{ei} is the vowel of English \emph{eye}. In \emph{ist} and \emph{bist} the
final \emph{t} is crisp; \emph{ich} ends in the soft ich-sound.
\end{sounds}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}
\toprule
Singular & Plural \\
\midrule
ich \textbf{bin} & wir \textbf{sind} \\
du \textbf{bist} & ihr \textbf{seid} \\
er/sie/es \textbf{ist} & sie/Sie \textbf{sind} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Past: \emph{ich \textbf{war}}, \emph{du \textbf{warst}}, \emph{er \textbf{war}},
\emph{wir \textbf{waren}}.

Look at that with fresh eyes. \emph{Bin}, \emph{ist}, \emph{war} --- those do not
look like forms of the same word, and they aren't.

\begin{cousinweb}
\textbf{ist, sind, seid, sein} $\leftarrow$ Proto-Indo-European
\emph{*h\ensuremath{_1}es-}, ``to be'' --- which also gave Latin \emph{est},
\emph{sunt} and French \emph{est}, \emph{sont}.\\[3pt]
\textbf{bin, bist} $\leftarrow$ \emph{*b\ensuremath{^h}uH-}, ``to grow,
become'' --- which also gave English \textbf{be} and Latin \emph{fu\=\i}.\\[3pt]
\textbf{war, waren} $\leftarrow$ \emph{*wes-}, ``to dwell, remain'' --- which
also gave English \textbf{was}, \textbf{were}.
\end{cousinweb}

\begin{morphologybox}
Three separate ancient verbs, each contributing a slice of the paradigm. When one
verb's forms are filled in from another verb, that is \textbf{suppletion} --- the
same thing English does in \emph{go} / \emph{went}.
\end{morphologybox}

\begin{culture}
And notice that \emph{bin}/\emph{bist} has a cousin further afield. Latin
\emph{fu\=\i} is the source of Spanish \textbf{fui}, ``I was / I went.'' German
\emph{bin} and Spanish \emph{fui} descend from the same PIE root
\emph{*b\ensuremath{^h}uH-} --- one landed in the present, the other in the past.
\end{culture}

\begin{grammarlens}
\textbf{Why ``be'' verbs are the messiest words in every language.} This is not
bad luck; it is a rule: \emph{the most-used words are the most irregular}.
Regularity spreads by analogy --- you meet an unfamiliar verb and guess its forms
from the pattern. But you never have to guess at ``to be.'' You hear it a hundred
times a day, so the odd old shapes are reinforced faster than analogy can sand
them down. Rare words get regularized; common words are protected fossils.
\end{grammarlens}

Run the six forms until they are automatic --- \emph{ich bin, du bist, er ist,
wir sind, ihr seid, sie sind} --- and put them to work: \emph{Ich bin m\"{u}de}
(``I am tired''), \emph{Das ist gut} (``that is good'').

\section{\emph{ich bin gegangen} --- the perfect built on \emph{sein}}
\label{lesson:perfekt-sein}

The last chapter built the perfect with \emph{haben}: \emph{ich habe gelernt}. A
particular family of verbs takes \textbf{sein} instead --- and the line it draws
will look familiar to anyone who has met French.

\begin{grammarlens}
\textbf{The rule: verbs of motion and change of state take \emph{sein}.}
\end{grammarlens}

\emph{gehen} (``to go'') is the model:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}
\toprule
German & English \\
\midrule
\emph{ich \textbf{bin} gegangen} & I went / I have gone \\
\emph{du \textbf{bist} gegangen} & you went \\
\emph{er \textbf{ist} gegangen} & he went \\
\emph{wir \textbf{sind} gegangen} & we went \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The rest of the family:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
Verb & Perfect & English \\
\midrule
\emph{kommen} & \emph{ich \textbf{bin} gekommen} & I came \\
\emph{fahren} & \emph{ich \textbf{bin} gefahren} & I travelled, drove \\
\emph{bleiben} & \emph{ich \textbf{bin} geblieben} & I stayed \\
\emph{werden} & \emph{ich \textbf{bin} geworden} & I became \\
\emph{sein} itself & \emph{ich \textbf{bin} gewesen} & I have been \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Motion (\emph{gehen}, \emph{kommen}, \emph{fahren}), change of state
(\emph{werden}) --- and then a small set that breaks the pattern and simply has
to be learned: \textbf{sein} and \textbf{bleiben}, which are the \emph{opposite}
of change, plus \emph{gelingen} (``succeed''), \emph{geschehen} /
\emph{passieren} (``happen'') and \emph{begegnen} (``meet'').

\subsection*{No agreement --- the German simplification}

Here is a place where German is \emph{easier} than French:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
& French & German \\
\midrule
he went & \emph{il est \textbf{all\'{e}}} & \emph{er ist \textbf{gegangen}} \\
she went & \emph{elle est \textbf{all\'{e}e}} & \emph{sie ist \textbf{gegangen}} \\
they went & \emph{ils sont \textbf{all\'{e}s}} & \emph{sie sind \textbf{gegangen}} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{grammarlens}
French makes the participle agree with the subject. \textbf{In the perfect,
German makes it agree with nothing} --- \emph{gegangen} is \emph{gegangen} for
every person and every gender. German participles \emph{do} still take endings
when used as adjectives in front of a noun --- \emph{ein geschrieben\textbf{er}
Brief} (``a written letter''), \emph{der angekommen\textbf{e} Zug} (``the
arrived train''), \emph{die eingeschlafen\textbf{e} Katze} (``the fallen-asleep
cat''). It is specifically the perfect where the ending is gone. Old High German
did inflect the participle here, at least variably; German lost that, along with
most of its other adjective-like endings in this position.
\end{grammarlens}

\begin{culture}
And note what did \textbf{not} happen: German didn't get this from Latin. The
\emph{haben}-perfect and the \emph{sein}-perfect are native Germanic
developments that grew up \textbf{alongside} the Romance ones --- neighbouring
languages drifting the same way through centuries of contact, the same areal
spread that made the simple past retreat in French, German and Italian together.
Two things to carry forward: the \textbf{split} is parallel, the
\textbf{agreement} is not shared.
\end{culture}

Practise the contrast until the choice of helper is a reflex --- \emph{ich
\textbf{habe} gelernt} \ldots{} \emph{ich \textbf{bin} gegangen} --- and the
comparison that shows off German's one economy here: \emph{elle est
all\'{e}\textbf{e}} \ldots{} \emph{sie ist gegangen}, with no ending at all.

That is where this volume pauses. In sixteen chapters you have gone from
\emph{guten Tag} to two working past tenses, and the method has not changed
once: take the word apart, find the English cousin sleeping inside it, and let
the family tree do the memorising for you. The next volume picks the thread back
up with describing what you see.
