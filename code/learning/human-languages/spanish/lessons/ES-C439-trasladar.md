# trasladar — carried across

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C346-WHERE-14, ES-LEX-C355-WHERE-33, ES-LEX-C439-VIA-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say **la oficina**, **el piso** and **el andén**. All three are places
a thing or a person can be taken **from** one of and **to** another.

## You'll want to know: trasladar
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-C439-VIA-02]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C346-WHERE-14] -->

**trasladar** — to move something or someone from one place to another.
**trasladarse** — to move yourself, to relocate.

*Han trasladado la oficina al centro* — they have moved the office to the centre.
*Me traslado a Sevilla en enero* — I am relocating to Seville in January.

This is the formal word, the one on a letter from a bank or a clinic. For moving
house among friends you would hear *mudarse*, but the notice on the door will say
*trasladado*.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C439-VIA-02] -->

**tras-** from Latin *trans*, across, plus **-ladar** from *lātus*, carried.

| piece | meaning |
|---|---|
| tras- | across |
| -lad- | carried |

**Carried across.** English has the same two pieces bolted together in
**translate**, which is a text carried across into another language, and in
**relate**, **collate** and **dilate**.

## Why it's said this way
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C439-VIA-02] -->

*Lātus* is the odd piece, and it is worth a minute.

Latin's verb for carrying was *ferre*. Its past participle was not *ferred* or
anything built from *fer-*; it was **lātus**, from a completely different root
that had been absorbed into the same verb long before anyone wrote it down. A
verb whose forms come from two unrelated stems is called **suppletive**, and
English has the famous one: *go* and *went*, where *went* was borrowed whole from
*wend*.

So **trasladar** carries a fossil. The *-lad-* in it is the past participle of a
Latin verb whose present tense left no trace in the Spanish word at all.
