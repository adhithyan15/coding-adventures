\chapter{Sounds \& Letters}
\label{ch:sounds-and-letters}

Spanish reuses the Latin alphabet, so this chapter is short --- there is no
new writing system to learn, only new sounds and a small set of rules for
predicting stress. (Later volumes in this series, for Arabic, Hindi, Tamil,
Kannada, Telugu, and Malayalam, will need a much longer version of this
chapter, teaching an entirely new script letter by letter. Spanish is the
easy case.)

\section{The Five Vowel Sounds}

Spanish has exactly five vowel sounds, and --- unlike English --- each
letter only ever makes one of them.

\begin{vocabtable}
a & --- & like the ``a'' in \emph{father}, always \\
e & --- & like the ``e'' in \emph{bed}, always \\
i & --- & like the ``ee'' in \emph{see}, always \\
o & --- & like the ``o'' in \emph{note}, but shorter, no glide \\
u & --- & like the ``oo'' in \emph{food}, always \\
\end{vocabtable}

The letter that trips up English speakers most is \textbf{i} --- it sounds
like English \emph{ee}, not English \emph{i}. The practical payoff of this
regularity: once these five sounds are automatic, almost any unfamiliar
Spanish word can be pronounced correctly just by reading it, which is not
true of English at all.

\section{Consonants That Differ From English}

Most Spanish consonants are close enough to their English counterparts to
need no special attention. A handful genuinely don't:

\begin{vocabtable}
\~{n} & --- & like the ``ny'' in \emph{canyon}; \emph{a\~{n}o} (year) \\
ll & --- & like the ``y'' in \emph{yes} (Latin-America default); \emph{llamar} \\
rr & --- & a rolled/trilled r, genuinely unlike English r; \emph{perro} (dog) \\
j & --- & like ``h'' in \emph{hot}, but with more throat friction; \emph{jueves} \\
h & --- & always silent, no exceptions; \emph{hola} is \emph{OH-la} \\
\end{vocabtable}

A single \textbf{r} between vowels is a quick tap, not rolled: \emph{pero}
(``but'') and \emph{perro} (``dog'') are a real minimal pair, and mixing
them up is a classic --- and sometimes embarrassing --- mistake.

\section{Stress \& Written Accents}

Every Spanish word has a syllable that carries more stress than the
others, exactly as in English --- but Spanish has a small, reliable rule
set for predicting which one, plus a written mark for when the rule gets
overridden. Three facts cover almost every word in the language:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item If a word ends in a vowel, \textbf{n}, or \textbf{s}, stress falls
    on the \textbf{second-to-last} syllable: \emph{ca-SA}, \emph{ha-BLAN},
    \emph{li-BROS}.
  \item If a word ends in any other consonant, stress falls on the
    \textbf{last} syllable: \emph{ha-BLAR}, \emph{es-pa-\~{N}OL},
    \emph{ciu-DAD}.
  \item A written accent mark (\'{}) overrides both rules --- stress goes
    exactly where the mark is. \emph{hablo} (``I speak'') vs.\ \emph{habl\'{o}}
    (``he/she spoke'') differ only by that mark, and it carries real
    grammatical weight, not decoration.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{etymology}
This is also why \emph{d\'{i}as} (as in \emph{buenos d\'{i}as}, Chapter~1)
carries a written accent: \emph{d\'{i}a} descends from Latin \emph{dies}
(``day''), and the accent mark is there specifically to keep the stress on
the \emph{i} where the default syllable-counting rule would otherwise put
it.
\end{etymology}

every subsequent chapter's Spanish text exercises these sounds and this
stress rule continuously, so a separate review cycle would be redundant.
