# Unit 7: Days of the Week

## Warm-up

[PAUSE 2s] Out loud: count *uno* to *diez*. Today's unit closes out Week 1 —
and it's the richest etymology set yet, because Spanish weekday names are
almost all Roman god names hiding in plain sight.

## New Material

**lunes** — Monday
> ← Latin *dies Lunae* ("day of the Moon"). Cognate with English *lunar*,
> *lunatic* (originally "moon-affected").

**martes** — Tuesday
> ← Latin *dies Martis* ("day of Mars," the war god). Cognate with English
> *martial* and the month *March*.

**miércoles** — Wednesday
> ← Latin *dies Mercurii* ("day of Mercury," god of trade and messages).
> Cognate with English *mercurial* (quick-changing, like the god/the metal)
> and *commerce*.

**jueves** — Thursday
> ← Latin *dies Iovis* ("day of Jupiter"/Jove). Cognate with English
> *jovial* — Jupiter's astrological influence was supposedly good-humored.

**viernes** — Friday
> ← Latin *dies Veneris* ("day of Venus," goddess of love). Cognate with
> English *venerate*, *venereal*.

**sábado** — Saturday
> Breaks the pattern: ← Latin *sabbatum*, itself from Hebrew *Shabbat* ("day
> of rest"). Direct cognate with English *sabbath*. Rome's own day-name for
> this slot (*dies Saturni*, "day of Saturn" — which is where English
> *Saturday* comes from instead) lost out to the Judeo-Christian name in
> Spanish.

**domingo** — Sunday
> Also breaks the pattern: ← Latin *dies Dominica* ("the Lord's day," a
> Christian reframing of *dies Solis*, "day of the Sun"). Cognate with
> English *dominion*, *dominant*, and the Spanish honorific *don*.

## Wrap-up Recall

[PAUSE 3s] Which two weekday names *don't* come from Roman planetary gods,
and why? ... *sábado* (Hebrew *Shabbat*) and *domingo* (Christian "the
Lord's day") — both reflect a later religious reframing of the Roman
calendar.
