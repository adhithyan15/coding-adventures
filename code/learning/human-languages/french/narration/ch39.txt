French, chapter 39: Where Two Words Melt Into One.
6 lessons. You can do the first 3 of them in the car; after that you will want to have stopped.


Lesson 1 of 6.
à — one letter, and the accent that saves it.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say il a and il y a. The a in both is the verb. This lesson takes the other one.
[your turn — recall: two chapters back, the three question words you took last — comment, combien, pourquoi]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

The word, taken apart.
à — to, at.
à Paris — to Paris, in Paris., à trois heures — at three o'clock.
Latin ad gave it, and the d wore off, leaving a single letter. English kept the same ad in a hundred borrowed words — advance, admit, adhere — where it still means toward.
The grave accent has one job and no other: it separates this word from a, the verb. Same letter, same sound, and only the mark on the page says which is meant. French does this three times over — a/à, ou/où, la/là — and you have already met the third.
You have owned the clock since it was taught and had no way to say at a time. Now you have one.

[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: the three pairs the accent separates — "a/à", "ou/où", "la/là"]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: ask for a time, then answer — "Quelle heure est-il? — À midi."]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: the three modals, each pointed at a time — "Je veux partir à midi. Je peux partir à midi. Je dois partir à midi." — and the second verb never moving]

Lesson 2 of 6.
de — "of," and the word already hiding in du.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say du pain. You were told two words are inside it. Name them.

The word, taken apart.
de — of, from.
le café de Paris — the café of Paris., le frère de Marie — Marie's brother.
That second line is worth a moment. French owns nothing with an apostrophe: it says the brother of Marie, and it has said so since Latin. English is the odd one here — the possessive 's is a Germanic survival that Latin's daughters never had, so Marie's brother is the outlier and le frère de Marie is the ordinary European shape.
Before a vowel it elides, exactly as le does:
le livre d'Anne — Anne's book.
And you have been saying de since the food chapter without meeting it alone: du pain is de + le, and de l'eau is de with nothing to contract.

Guided Practice.
[your turn — say: possession, the French way — "le frère de Marie", "la mère de Anne"]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: elided — "le café d'Anne"]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: the two you already had — "du pain", "de l'eau"]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: the pair — "à Paris" and "de Paris"]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
How does French say "Marie's brother"? (Le frère de Marie — of Marie.) Which language invented the apostrophe possessive? (English, and none of Latin's daughters has one.) What two words are inside du? (De and le.)


Lesson 3 of 6.
au, aux — where à and le melt together.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
De plus le gave you du. Predict what à plus le gives before you read on.

The word, taken apart.
au = à + le., aux = à + les.
au café — to the café., aux cafés — to the cafés.
à la maison — no contraction, because la is not le.
Two of the four combinations melt and two do not, and the split is the same one du already showed you: le and les contract, la and l' stay apart.
The u in au was an l. French turned al into au across the whole language, and once you have seen it you find it everywhere:
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
older shape: bel. modern: beau.
older shape: cheval. modern: chevaux.
older shape: journal. modern: journaux.
So the x on aux and the x on chevaux are the same story, and a plural that looks irregular is an l that turned into a u eight centuries ago.
The x of aux is silent alone and comes back as a z before a vowel — aux amis is oh-ZAH-mee, the liaison you have had since the numbers.

Guided Practice.
[your turn — say: the four — "au café", "aux cafés", "à la maison", "à l'eau"]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: the pair that melt the same way — "du café", "au café"]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: the liaison — "aux amis", oh-ZAH-mee]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: the l that became a u — "bel, beau; cheval, chevaux"]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
What is au made of? (À plus le.) And aux? (À plus les.) Which two never contract? (La and l'.) What was the u of au before it was a u? (An l, the same one inside chevaux.)


Lesson 4 of 6.
de la — the half of the partitive that was missing.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, because the lesson points at something written down. You can listen to all of it now and come back to the parts that need looking at once you have stopped.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say "some bread" and "some water". Both are yours. Now try "some milk" — le lait is a le-word, but la viande, meat, is not.

The word, taken apart.
de la — some, in front of a la-word.
du lait — some milk., de la viande — some meat., de l'eau — some water.
Three shapes, one rule, and it is a rule you already own from two other places: de plus the article, contracting only where le and les contract.
That is worth stating plainly, because it looks like three separate things to memorise and is one:
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
the noun takes: le. some…: du.
the noun takes: la. some…: de la.
the noun takes: l'. some…: de l'.
The food chapter taught the first and third and stopped, because every noun on its table happened to be a le-word or a vowel-word. The middle line was never missing from the language, only from the table.

Guided Practice.
[your turn — say: the three shapes — "du lait, de la viande, de l'eau"]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: the table you already had — "du pain, du vin, du fromage"]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: the two drinks — "du café, du thé"]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: article first, then the partitive — "le lait becomes du lait"]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: pointed at, then measured — "ce lait", then "du lait"; "cette eau", then "de l'eau"]

Which shape goes with a la-word? (De la.) With a le-word? (Du.) Why does de la not contract? (Only le and les ever contract — the same rule behind du and au.)


Lesson 5 of 6.
des — the plural of un, and the last shape of some.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
un café is a café. Say the plural. English drops the word entirely — cafés — and French cannot.

The word, taken apart.
des — some; and the plural of un and une.
un café becomes des cafés, une amie becomes des amies.
Il y a des cafés. — There are cafés.
des is de + les, contracted exactly as du is de + le. So it completes two systems at once and adds no new machinery to either.
Here English is the language with the hole. English has a and no plural of it, so it says books, cafés, friends with nothing in front. French will not leave the slot empty, and a sentence with a bare plural noun in it sounds unfinished to a French ear. That is the single commonest mistake an English speaker makes in French, and it is a missing word rather than a wrong one.
The s is silent, and it comes back as a z before a vowel: des amies is day-ZAH-mee.

[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: existence and identity, in the plural — "Il y a des cafés." "Ce sont des amis."]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
