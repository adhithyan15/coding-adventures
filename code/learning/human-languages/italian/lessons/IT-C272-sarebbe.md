# sarebbe — it would be

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[IT-GRAMMAR-CONDIZIONALE-01] -->

*potere*. (*Potrei avere un caffè?*)

## You'll want to know: sarebbe
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[IT-LEX-SAREBBE-01]; assesses=[IT-LEX-ESSERE-02, IT-LEX-C156-QUAL156-01] -->

**sarebbe** — "it would be", "he or she would be".

**essere** does not build its condizionale on *esser-*. It uses a stem of its
own, **sar-**, and then the endings are the usual ones: **sarei**, **saresti**,
**sarebbe**, **saremmo**, **sareste**, **sarebbero**. *Sarebbe* is the one
you will use most, because it judges a situation rather than a person:

> *Sarebbe difficile.* — It would be difficult.
> *Sarebbe più comodo in treno.* — It would be more comfortable by train.

## Grammar Lens: sarebbe bello
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[IT-GRAMMAR-SAREBBE-BELLO-02]; assesses=[IT-LEX-C72-AGG1-05, IT-LEX-C60-VERBI5-03, IT-GRAMMAR-SE-PRESENTE-01] -->

**Sarebbe bello** + infinitive — "it would be lovely to":

> *Sarebbe bello viaggiare insieme.* — It would be lovely to travel together.

That is a wish with the person left out, softer than *vorrei* and less
private than *spero di*. And it sits naturally after a real condition, as the
judgement on what follows:

> *Se piove, sarebbe più comodo prendere l'autobus.* — If it rains, it would
> be more comfortable to take the bus.

The *se* half stays in the plain present: it may well rain. The *sarebbe* half
is your verdict on the branch.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[] -->

**sarebbe** is built on **essere**, inherited from Latin *esse* (EtymDB 2.1):
the stem *sar-* is the one essere uses for what has not happened yet, and
**-ebbe** is the worn Latin *habuit*, "he had", of the last lesson. So
*sarebbe* began as something like "he had to be". Neither source records forms
this late, and the history of *sar-* itself is not settled, so the stem is
described here rather than traced.
