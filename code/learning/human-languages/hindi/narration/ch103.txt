Hindi, chapter 103: What I Know and What I Want.
6 lessons. You can do the first 3 of them in the car; after that you will want to have stopped.


Lesson 1 of 6.
मैं जानता हूँ (maiṁ jāntā hūṁ) — the half that was missing.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say I do not know — you have had that sentence since the chapter on नहीं. Now try to say the opposite of it.

You'll want to know: जानता.
मैं जानता हूँ (maiṁ jāntā hūṁ)। — I know.
Nothing about that is new except the permission.

The word, taken apart.
You have been saying मैं नहीं जानता for a very long time. The negative lesson used it as its example sentence, and everything in it was explained except the last word.
[a table of 1 row, read aloud]
what you had: मैं नहीं जानता. what you have now: मैं जानता हूँ (maiṁ jāntā hūṁ).
Take नहीं out and one thing has to come back: हूँ. A Hindi present needs its to be on the end, and the negative sentence dropped it — which is ordinary and which is why the affirmative does not look like the negative with a word removed.
The verb is जानना, and its ज is the letter drawn two chapters before this one. It was on the page long before that, inside जी and आज.

Grammar Lens: a word carried and never handed over.
This is worth naming because it is a real gap and not a quirk of your memory. The negative lesson's headword names jāntā and glosses it as the masculine present of to know — but everything that lesson actually teaches is about नहीं: its Sanskrit root, its Indo-European cousins, its two jobs.
The word rode along in an example and was never handed over. You could deny knowing something and not claim it, which is a strange shape for a vocabulary to have.

Lesson 2 of 6.
क्या आप जानते हैं? (kyā āp jānte haiṁ?) — asking it.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say I know. Then say the little word that turns a Hindi statement into a yes-or-no question.

You'll want to know: जानते हैं.
क्या आप जानते हैं? (kyā āp jānte haiṁ?) — Do you know?

The word, taken apart.
Every piece is old and the assembly is regular:
[a table of 4 rows, read aloud]
piece: क्या. job: marks the whole thing as a question.
piece: आप. job: the respectful you.
piece: जानते. job: the verb, agreeing with आप.
piece: हैं. job: to be, also agreeing with आप.
आप is grammatically plural, which is why both the verb and to be take their plural endings even when you are speaking to one person. That is not a new rule — it is the same respect-as-plural the track has used since the second chapter, turning up on a new verb.

Grammar Lens: where क्या sits.
क्या goes at the front, and the rest of the sentence keeps its ordinary order behind it. English reshuffles a question — you know becomes do you know — and Hindi does not move anything at all.
So a yes-or-no question costs one word and no rearrangement, which makes it one of the cheapest things in the language to build once you can say the statement.

Lesson 3 of 6.
चाहिए (cāhiye) — wanting a thing rather than an action.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say the verb for wanting, the one you met with सकना. Then try to use it to want a cup of tea rather than to want to do something.

You'll want to know: चाहिए.
चाहिए (cāhiye) — is wanted, is needed.
It is built from the same root as चाहना, and it does a job that verb cannot.

The word, taken apart.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
form: चाहता / चाहती. what follows it: another verb.
form: चाहिए (cāhiye). what follows it: a thing.
चाहना wants an action. It was taught alongside the rule that a Hindi verb can swallow another verb whole, and that is exactly what it does: I want to go.
चाहिए (cāhiye) wants a noun. I want tea. English uses one word for both jobs and hides the difference; Hindi uses two forms and makes it visible.
The other difference is that चाहिए (cāhiye) does not change. It has no masculine and feminine, no singular and plural — the same five letters whoever is speaking and whatever is wanted. After a chapter of endings that agree with everything in sight, that is a relief worth noticing.

Grammar Lens: the ए on the end.
The word ends in ए, the standing vowel drawn a few chapters back — not the sitting mark ◌े, because there is no consonant after हि for the vowel to ride on.
That is the rule from the letter lesson doing its first real work: which of the two shapes a word uses is decided by whether a consonant is there to carry the vowel, and a word ending in a vowel sound has nothing behind it.

Lesson 4 of 6.
मुझे चाय चाहिए (mujhe cāy cāhiye) — the wanter is not the subject.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, because the lesson points at something written down. You can listen to all of it now and come back to the parts that need looking at once you have stopped.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say I like tea in Hindi. Look at the first word you said, and keep it in mind.

You'll want to know: मुझे … चाहिए.
मुझे चाय चाहिए (mujhe cāy cāhiye)। — I want tea.
Not मैं. मुझे — to me.

The two words.
This is the liking sentence again, with one word swapped:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
Hindi: मुझे चाय पसंद है. word for word: to me, tea is pleasing.
Hindi: मुझे चाय चाहिए (mujhe cāy cāhiye). word for word: to me, tea is wanted.
The person is in the dative both times, and the thing is what the sentence is really about. Hindi treats wanting and liking as things that happen to you rather than things you do, and puts the person on the receiving end of both.
That is not a Hindi peculiarity. The proxy language reaches the same shape in me gusta, and Italian in mi piace — three languages in two unrelated families building the same idea the same way. What makes Hindi's version worth a lesson is that चाहिए (cāhiye) extends it to wanting, where those languages switch back to an ordinary verb.

Grammar Lens: what agrees with what.
चाहिए (cāhiye) does not change, so there is nothing to agree. The sentence has no word that bends, which is unusual enough to be a useful landmark: if you hear चाहिए (cāhiye), stop looking for agreement.
And मुझे carries झ, the breathy partner drawn a few chapters back. It was in your mouth from the liking chapter onwards and only recently on your hand.

Lesson 5 of 6.
cāh kī tālikā — wanting, on one page.

Warm-up.
[pause 3 seconds]
Name the two forms of the wanting verb, and say what each one wants after it.

Grammar Lens: one root, two forms.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
form: चाहता / चाहती. what follows: a verb. example: मैं जाना चाहता हूँ.
form: चाहिए. what follows: a thing. example: मुझे चाय चाहिए (mujhe cāy cāhiye).
Ask what comes next, not what it means. Both translate into English as want, so the meaning will not tell the two apart — the shape of what follows will, every time.

Grammar Lens: who sits where.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
sentence: मैं जाना चाहता हूँ. the person is: मैं — the subject.
sentence: मुझे चाय चाहिए (mujhe cāy cāhiye). the person is: मुझे — the dative.
sentence: मुझे चाय पसंद है. the person is: मुझे — the dative.
Two of the three put the person on the receiving end, and the one that does not is the one where the person is genuinely doing something — going. Wanting a thing and liking a thing happen to you; wanting to act is something you do, and Hindi draws that line with the case of the first word.

Grammar Lens: the verb that was only ever denied.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
what you can now say: मैं जानता हूँ (maiṁ jāntā hūṁ). what you had before: only मैं नहीं जानता.
what you can now say: क्या आप जानते हैं? (kyā āp jānte haiṁ?) what you had before: nothing.
The affirmative needed no new grammar at all — it needed somebody to introduce the word, which the negative lesson used and never taught.

Lesson 6 of 6.
आपको क्या चाहिए? (āpko kyā cāhiye?) — answering at a counter.

Warm-up.
[pause 3 seconds]
Say to you in its respectful form, and the word that marks a question. Both have been yours for a while.

The exchange.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
speaker: A. Hindi: आपको क्या चाहिए? (āpko kyā cāhiye?)
speaker: B. Hindi: मुझे चाय चाहिए (mujhe cāy cāhiye)।.
The two lines are the same sentence with the person swapped — आपको for मुझे — and क्या standing where the answer will go.

How to answer.
A listening item built on this keys on the noun between the two fixed words. Everything else in B's reply is predictable:
The first word is the dative person — it tells you who, not what.
The last word is चाहिए (cāhiye) — it never changes, so it carries no information.
The answer is whatever sits between them.
That is why the item is answerable even if the middle word is the only one you catch. Listen to the middle.

Grammar Lens: the chapter in one sentence.
Hindi puts the person on the receiving end of wanting, and gives the wanting word no endings at all — so the sentence's whole meaning lives in the noun in the middle and the case of the word in front.
क्या आप जानते हैं? (kyā āp jānte haiṁ?) works the other way round: there the endings do all the agreeing, because somebody is genuinely doing the knowing.
