Not to be read before the lessons --- they teach reading through real words. This

\emph{Sanskrit}.

\section*{The three facts}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Consonants carry a built-in ``a.''} \sk{क} \emph{ka}, \sk{न}
    \emph{na}, \sk{म} \emph{ma} --- left to right under a connecting top line.
  \item \textbf{A vowel sign changes it.} \sk{का} \emph{k\=a}, \sk{कि} \emph{ki},
    \sk{की} \emph{k\=\i}, \sk{कु} \emph{ku}, \sk{के} \emph{ke}, \sk{को}
    \emph{ko}. A standalone (word-initial) vowel has its own letter: \sk{अ}
    \emph{a}, \sk{आ} \emph{\=a}, \sk{इ} \emph{i}\ldots
  \item \textbf{A \emph{halant} removes the vowel}, and the bare consonant joins
    the next as a \textbf{conjunct}: \sk{स्} $+$ \sk{त} $\to$ \sk{स्त},
    \sk{न} $+$ \sk{य} $\to$ \sk{न्य}.
\end{enumerate}

\section*{What Sanskrit needs even from a Devanagari reader}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The visarga \sk{ः} (\d{h}).} A soft breathed echo of the
    preceding vowel at a word's end --- \emph{namask\=ara\d{h}}, \emph{r\=ama\d{h}}.
    It usually marks the masculine-singular ending, so it is grammatical as well
    as phonetic.
  \item \textbf{The anusv\=ara \sk{ं} (\.{m}/\.{n}).} A dot above the line
    nasalising the vowel or standing for a nasal consonant before another --- very
    frequent in Sanskrit.
  \item \textbf{The vocalic \d{r} \sk{ऋ} (as in \d{r}\d{g}veda, "R\d{g} Veda").}
    Sanskrit treats \d{r} as a \emph{vowel} --- it is the \d{r} of
    \emph{m\=at\d{r}} ``mother'' and \emph{pit\d{r}} ``father,'' the words that
    proved Sanskrit's kinship with Latin \emph{m\=ater}/\emph{pater} and English
    \emph{mother}/\emph{father}.
  \item \textbf{Sandhi.} Sanskrit fuses words at their seams by fixed sound-rules:
    \emph{su} $+$ \emph{\=agata} $\to$ \emph{sv\=agata}. The lessons flag each
    fusion where it happens.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Sanskrit in the family}

Sanskrit is the classical language of India --- the tongue of the Vedas, of
K\=alid\=asa, of Hindu, Buddhist, and Jain learning --- and the ancestor of the
modern Indo-Aryan languages (Hindi, Marathi, Punjabi, Bengali, and more). It is a
senior branch of \textbf{Indo-European}, sister to Greek, Latin, and the Germanic
languages including English. That is why this curriculum treats it, with Latin, as
a \emph{taproot}: its words reach both east into South Asia and west into Europe,
and the lessons trace both directions.
