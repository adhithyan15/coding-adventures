# las cinco vocales — five letters, five sounds, and no exceptions

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-ORTH-C425-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Name the five vowels in Spanish. You have been saying them since the
first chapter without being told they were a closed set.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-ORTH-C425-03]; assesses=[] -->

**las cinco vocales** — "the five vowels": **a, e, i, o, u**.

*Vocal* is from Latin *vox*, "voice" — a vowel is the part of a word you can
actually sing. The same root gives *voz*, *vocabulario* and English *vocal*.

Here is the part that makes Spanish kind to a reader: **each of the five has
one sound, and it keeps it.** English *a* is different in *cat*, *car*, *cake*
and *about*. Spanish *a* is the same in every word that has ever been written.

## Grammar Lens: the two marks a vowel can carry
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-ORTH-C425-03, ES-ORTH-C425-01] -->

Only vowels take marks in Spanish, and there are exactly two:

| mark | name | what it does |
|---|---|---|
| á é í ó ú | la tilde, el acento | marks which syllable is stressed |
| ü | la diéresis | makes a silent **u** speak |

**La tilde** never changes the sound of the vowel — it only says *stress this
one*. That is why it can tell two identical words apart: *el* is *the*, *él* is
*he*; *si* is *if*, *sí* is *yes*.

**La diéresis** appears on **ü** and nowhere else. In *guerra* the **u** is
silent; in *pingüino* the two dots say *pronounce me*. Two dots, one job.

Note the vocabulary trap: Spanish *tilde* means the **accent mark**. The squiggle
over **ñ** is *la virgulilla*, and English calls that one the tilde. The two
languages use the word for different marks.
