\chapter{Greetings}
\label{ch:greetings}
\hlchaptermodality{1}

The Malayalam greetings and the words that oil a first conversation --- with a
writing ramp gentle enough for a first encounter with the script. You hear and
use a meaningful word first. Then your eye and hand meet no more than three
shapes at once. A tiny familiar unit reaches dictation before you assemble the
whole greeting.

\section{\emph{Namaskāram} --- first hear the greeting}
\label{lesson:namaskaram}

\textbf{Listen first.} \emph{Namaskāram}. Hear five beats:
\emph{na-ma-s-kā-ram}. Say it once as a respectful greeting. The spelling is
deliberately absent here; sound and meaning come before decoding.

\begin{cousinweb}
\emph{Namaskāram} is \textbf{Sanskrit}: \emph{namas} (``a bow'')
$+$ \emph{kāra} (``a making,'' root \emph{kṛ}, ``to do''), with a Malayalam
ending $=$ ``the making of a bow.'' The same word as Hindi \emph{namaskār}.
Worth marking, because Malayalam is otherwise the \textbf{closest sister of
Tamil} --- the two split only about a thousand years ago --- yet it absorbed an
enormous Sanskrit layer (the old \emph{Maṇipravāḷam} style mixed the two
freely), so its formal greeting is Sanskrit even as its everyday core stays
deeply Tamil-like.
\end{cousinweb}

\begin{cognates}
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
Language & ``Hello'' & Source \\
\midrule
\textbf{Malayalam} & \emph{namaskāram} & Sanskrit \\
Telugu & \emph{namaskāram} & Sanskrit \\
Kannada & \emph{namaskāra} & Sanskrit \\
Hindi & \emph{namaste} & Sanskrit \\
Tamil & \emph{vaṇakkam} & \textbf{native Dravidian} \\
English & \emph{hello} & Germanic \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Even Tamil's closest sister took the Sanskrit greeting; Tamil alone kept its
own.
\end{cognates}

\begin{culture}
Said with the palms pressed together and a small bow --- the gesture \emph{is}
the word. Both \textbf{hello and goodbye}, any time, to almost anyone.
\end{culture}

\section[Na and ma --- observe and trace]{\ml{ന} and \ml{മ} --- two sounds you already know}
\label{lesson:ml-w01-na-ma-trace}

\begin{sounds}
Malayalam reads left to right. A bare consonant already carries a short
\emph{a}: \ml{ന} is \emph{na}; \ml{മ} is \emph{ma}. Together, \ml{നമ} is
\emph{na-ma}, the opening you just heard. Keep the model visible. Finger-trace
\ml{ന} once, then \ml{മ}, then \ml{നമ}. Say the two sounds after your finger
stops. Two shapes are enough for this lesson.
\end{sounds}

\section[Na-ma --- guided copy]{\ml{നമ} --- copy with the model visible}
\label{lesson:ml-w01-na-ma-guided-copy}

Look at \ml{നമ}. Say \emph{na-ma}. Copy the two-shape unit once while the model
stays visible, looking back whenever you need. Compare the two shapes, circle
one place to adjust, and copy it once more. This is supported practice, not a
memory test.

\section[Na-ma --- delayed copy]{\ml{നമ} --- look, hide, and copy}
\label{lesson:ml-w01-na-ma-delayed-copy}

Look at \ml{നമ} for three seconds. Cover it, wait one breath, and write the
two-shape unit. Uncover the model and compare left to right. If either shape
drifted, trace it once and retry. Nothing new is introduced; only the delay is
new.

\section[Na-ma --- dictation]{\ml{നമ} --- write two known sounds}
\label{lesson:ml-w01-na-ma-dictation}

Hide the model. Hear or say \emph{na-ma}, then write \ml{നമ} from sound. Check
after your pencil stops. You have now carried a meaningful piece through
tracing, guided copy, delayed copy, and dictation.

\section[Sa, chandrakkala, ka]{\ml{സ}, \ml{്}, \ml{ക} --- three shapes make \ml{സ്ക}}
\label{lesson:ml-w01-sa-chandrakkala-ka}

\begin{sounds}
\ml{സ} is \emph{sa}; \ml{ക} is \emph{ka}. The small \emph{chandrakkala}
\ml{്} removes the built-in \emph{a}. Put it after \ml{സ}: \ml{സ്}, just
\emph{s}. Join that to \ml{ക}: \ml{സ്ക}, \emph{ska}. Trace the three pieces,
then copy \ml{സ്ക} once with the model visible. Stop there; three shapes are
enough.
\end{sounds}

\section[Long aa, ra, and final m]{\ml{ാ}, \ml{ര}, \ml{ം} --- three shapes finish the sound}
\label{lesson:ml-w01-aa-ra-anusvaram}

\begin{sounds}
The vowel sign \ml{ാ} changes \ml{ക} \emph{ka} to \ml{കാ} \emph{kā}.
\ml{ര} is \emph{ra}. The anusvāram \ml{ം} supplies the final nasal sound.
Trace only today's three shapes, then assemble and copy \ml{കാരം},
\emph{kā-ram}, with the model visible.
\end{sounds}

\section[Namaskāram --- assemble and read]{\ml{നമസ്കാരം} --- every shape is already yours}
\label{lesson:ml-w01-namaskaram-read}

Now the whole written greeting may appear. Assemble it from practised groups:

\begin{center}
\ml{നമ} $+$ \ml{സ്ക} $+$ \ml{ാരം} $\longrightarrow$ \ml{നമസ്കാരം}
\end{center}

Read each group, then read \ml{നമസ്കാരം}, \emph{namaskāram}. Keep the word
visible and copy it once in those same three groups. This lesson adds no new
shape.

\section[Namaskāram --- dictation]{\ml{നമസ്കാരം} --- write the known greeting from sound}
\label{lesson:ml-w01-namaskaram-dictation}

Hide the model. Hear \emph{namaskāram} once and write the whole greeting. Then
uncover \ml{നമസ്കാരം} and check group by group: \ml{നമ}, \ml{സ്ക},
\ml{ാരം}. A mismatch sends you back to one tiny group, never into a rushed
whole-word drill.

\section{\ml{നന്ദി} \emph{(nandi)} --- ``thank you,'' and the Tamil kinship made visible}
\label{lesson:nandi}

\begin{sounds}
\ml{ന} is \emph{na}; \ml{ന്ദി} is ``ndi'' --- a \textbf{conjunct} of \ml{ന}
(na) $+$ \ml{ദ} (da) into ``nd,'' with the ``i'' sign. Read \ml{ന·ന്ദി} $\to$
\emph{nandi}.
\end{sounds}

\begin{cousinweb}
\ml{നന്ദി} (\emph{nandi}) is \textbf{native Dravidian}, from the same root
\emph{nal / nan} (``good'') as Tamil \emph{naṉṟi}. Malayalam and Tamil split
from one language about a thousand years ago, so their oldest words are
near-twins --- ``thanks'' is a perfect case: Malayalam \emph{nandi}, Tamil
\emph{naṉṟi}. Meanwhile Kannada and Telugu use the Sanskrit \emph{dhanyavāda},
so the map of ``thanks'' traces the family's fault-line exactly.
\end{cousinweb}

\begin{cognates}
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
Language & ``Thanks'' & Note \\
\midrule
\textbf{Malayalam} & \ml{നന്ദി} \emph{nandi} & \textbf{native} (root \emph{nal}) \\
Tamil & \emph{naṉṟi} & the \emph{same} native root \\
Kannada & \emph{dhanyavāda} & Sanskrit \\
Telugu & \emph{dhanyavādamulu} & Sanskrit \\
Hindi & \emph{dhanyavād} / \emph{shukriyā} & Sanskrit / Perso-Arabic \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Tamil and Malayalam on the native side; Kannada and Telugu on the Sanskrit
side.
\end{cognates}

\section{\ml{അതെ} \emph{(athe)} --- ``yes,'' literally ``that [is so]''}
\label{lesson:athe}

\begin{sounds}
\ml{അ} is the \textbf{independent vowel} ``a'' (word-initial). \ml{തെ} is
``the'' --- \ml{ത} (\emph{tha}, a soft \emph{th}) with the ``e'' sign, which is
written \emph{in front} of its consonant but read after. Read \ml{അ·തെ} $\to$
\emph{athe}.
\end{sounds}

\begin{cousinweb}
\ml{അതെ} (\emph{athe}) is native Malayalam, grown from \ml{അത്} (\emph{athu}),
``that.'' To say ``yes,'' Malayalam essentially says \textbf{``that [is
so],''} affirming by pointing at the truth of what was said. Its partner is
\emph{alla}, ``[it is] not that.''
\end{cousinweb}

\begin{grammarlens}
Malayalam's yes/no are demonstratives in disguise: \emph{athe} (``that [is
so]'') vs.\ \emph{alla} (``not that''). Like its sisters, Malayalam also answers
by \textbf{echoing the verb} (\emph{vannu}, ``[I] came,'' for ``did you
come?''). You agree by confirming \emph{that} is how things are.
\end{grammarlens}

\section{\ml{ഇല്ല} \emph{(illa)} --- ``no,'' shared almost exactly with Tamil}
\label{lesson:illa}

\begin{sounds}
\ml{ഇ} is the independent vowel ``i'' (word-initial). \ml{ല്ല} is ``lla'' --- a
conjunct of \ml{ല} (la) $+$ \ml{ല} (la), a held ``ll.'' Read \ml{ഇ·ല്ല} $\to$
\emph{illa}.
\end{sounds}

\begin{cousinweb}
\ml{ഇല്ല} (\emph{illa}) is built on the ancient Dravidian root \emph{il},
``not-being'' --- so, exactly like Tamil \emph{illai}, it means \textbf{``[it]
is not, [there] is not,''} serving as the everyday ``no.'' Its positive twin is
\ml{ഉണ്ട്} (\emph{uṇṭŭ}), ``there is.''
\end{cousinweb}

\begin{grammarlens}
\textbf{Dravidian negates by \emph{being}.} Where English inserts \emph{not},
Malayalam --- like Tamil and Kannada --- swaps in the negative verb of
existence: \emph{pustakam \textbf{uṇṭŭ}} (``there \emph{is} a book'') $\to$
\emph{pustakam \textbf{illa}} (``there \emph{is not} a book''). Strongest across
the Tamil--Malayalam pair: Tamil \emph{illai}, Malayalam \emph{illa}, Kannada
\emph{illa} (Telugu was the outlier, with \emph{lēdu}).
\end{grammarlens}

\begin{cognates}
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
Language & ``No / isn't'' & Note \\
\midrule
\textbf{Malayalam} & \ml{ഇല്ല} \emph{illa} & native, root \emph{il-} \\
Tamil & \emph{illai} & the \emph{same} root \\
Kannada & \emph{illa} & the \emph{same} root \\
Telugu & \emph{lēdu} & native, a \emph{different} root \\
Hindi & \emph{nahīṁ} & Indo-European (unrelated) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\end{cognates}

\section[Śari --- ``okay'']{\ml{ശരി} \emph{(śari)} --- ``okay,'' the whole-family word once more}
\label{lesson:sari}

\begin{sounds}
\ml{ശ} is ``śa'' (a \emph{sh}-sound). Having borrowed so much Sanskrit,
Malayalam keeps the full set of Sanskrit sibilants (\emph{ś, ṣ, s}) as separate
letters --- more than Tamil, which uses one. \ml{രി} is \emph{ri}. Read
\ml{ശ·രി} $\to$ \emph{śari}.
\end{sounds}

\begin{cousinweb}
\ml{ശരി} (\emph{śari}) is the pan-Dravidian ``correct, right'' $\to$ ``okay,
agreed'' --- the verbal nod of Malayalam. The same family word as Tamil and
Kannada \emph{sari} and Telugu \emph{sarē}; Malayalam just spells its opening
sound with \ml{ശ} (\emph{ś}). Doubled, \emph{śari śari} $=$ ``fine, fine.''
\end{cousinweb}

\begin{grammarlens}
\textbf{One word, four scripts.} \emph{sari / sarē / śari} is the clearest
single proof of how close these four languages are: one word, one meaning,
four different scripts. Catching a shared word change its clothes but not its
sense is the fastest way to feel the whole family at once --- the reason to
learn the four side by side.
\end{grammarlens}

\section{Recap, and how Malayalam says goodbye}
\label{lesson:ml-c01-practice}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}llll@{}}
\toprule
Read & & Meaning & Origin \\
\midrule
\ml{നമസ്കാരം} & \emph{namaskāram} & hello & Sanskrit \\
\ml{നന്ദി} & \emph{nandi} & thank you & native (= Tamil \emph{naṉṟi}) \\
\ml{അതെ} & \emph{athe} & yes & native (``that is so'') \\
\ml{ഇല്ല} & \emph{illa} & no / isn't & native (= Tamil \emph{illai}) \\
\ml{ശരി} & \emph{śari} & okay & native (= \emph{sari}) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

How \textbf{Tamil-like} the native core is: four of the five everyday words are
shared with Tamil --- because Malayalam is Tamil's closest sister --- while only
the formal greeting reached for Sanskrit.

\begin{culture}
\textbf{Nearly the Tamil words exactly.} Malayalam, too, will not say a bare
``I'm leaving.'' The everyday goodbye is \ml{പോയി വരാം} (\emph{pōyi varām}),
``having gone, I [will] come [back]'' --- set beside Tamil's \emph{pōy
varugiṟēṉ} and Kannada's \emph{hōgi baruttēne}: the same sentence, the same
worldview, and in Malayalam and Tamil almost the same \emph{words}.
\end{culture}

That completes the four Dravidian first chapters. Next chapter: introducing
yourself --- \emph{enṟe pēru\ldots} (``my name\ldots'') --- and Malayalam's
\emph{nī} / \emph{niṅṅaḷ} (familiar / respectful ``you'').
