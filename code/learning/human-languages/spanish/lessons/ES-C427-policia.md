# el policía — where the article changes the meaning

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C427-AGENT-02] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say *el comprador* and *la compradora*, where the article follows the
person. The word in this lesson does something different, and it is worth
noticing.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-C427-AGENT-03]; assesses=[] -->

**el policía** — "the police officer." Greek **pólis**, "the city," through
Latin *politia*, "the administration of a state."

The root is unusually productive, and every word on it is about the city:

| word | the city in it |
|---|---|
| **polít**ica | the running of the city |
| metró**polis** | the mother-city |
| **polic**ía | the keeping of the city's order |

So a police officer is named after **the city**, not after policing. The
institution came first and the person was named from it.

## Grammar Lens: the article does the distinguishing
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C427-AGENT-03, ES-LEX-C427-AGENT-02] -->

This is the fact a reading paper can test, and it catches people:

| | meaning |
|---|---|
| **el** policía | a police officer, a man |
| **la** policía | the police **force** — or a woman officer |

The word does not change. **The article carries the whole difference**, and *la
policía* is ambiguous between the force and a female officer in a way that only
context settles.

Compare it with the **-dor** pair from the last two lessons, where the ending
moved and the article merely followed. Here the ending is fixed at **-ía** and
the article is doing all the work.
