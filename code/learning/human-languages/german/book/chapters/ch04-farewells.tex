\chapter{Farewells}
\label{ch:farewells}

You can open a German conversation; now close it. German parts formally with a
promise to \emph{see each other again}, casually with a word that has travelled
across three languages, and softly with \emph{bis\ldots} --- ``until\ldots'' ---
plus a word for time. \emph{Practice lessons: \texttt{lessons/GE-C04-*.md}.}

\section{\emph{auf Wiedersehen} --- ``goodbye,'' i.e.\ ``till we see again''}
\label{lesson:auf-wiedersehen}

\begin{sounds}
\emph{owf VEE-der-zay-en}. German \emph{w} says \emph{v}, and \emph{ie} is a
long \emph{ee}, so \emph{Wieder} $=$ \emph{VEE-der}. Nouns are capitalized in
German, so \emph{Wiedersehen} takes a capital W.
\end{sounds}

The formal goodbye is \textbf{auf Wiedersehen}, and it is the exact twin of
French \emph{au revoir}: both mean ``until we see each other again.'' It breaks
cleanly into three pieces:

\begin{morphologybox}
\textbf{auf} $=$ ``on, upon'' (cognate of English \emph{up} / \emph{of}).\\[3pt]
\textbf{wieder} $=$ ``again'' --- not, as you might guess, kin to
\emph{withering}, but to the \emph{with-} of \textbf{withdraw}; think
``back.''\\[3pt]
\textbf{sehen} $=$ ``to see'' --- a \textbf{straight cognate of English see}
(both from Germanic \emph{*sehwan}).
\end{morphologybox}

So \emph{auf Wiedersehen} $=$ ``on the seeing-again'' $\to$ ``until we see each
other again.'' Put it beside the other two tracks and the picture is lovely:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
Language & Goodbye & Metaphor \\
\midrule
German & \emph{auf Wiedersehen} & ``till we \textbf{see} again'' \\
French & \emph{au revoir} & ``till the \textbf{re-seeing}'' \\
Spanish & \emph{adi\'{o}s} & ``to \textbf{God}'' \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{culture}
German and French shake hands on a \emph{re-seeing}; Spanish commends you to
God. (\emph{Wiedersehen} even works as a noun on its own --- \emph{ein
Wiedersehen} is ``a reunion.'')
\end{culture}

\section{\emph{tsch\"{u}ss} --- the casual ``bye'' that used to say ``to God''}
\label{lesson:tschuss}

\begin{sounds}
\emph{tsch\"{u}ss} starts with the \emph{ch} of English \textbf{ch}urch:
\emph{ch\"{u}ss}. The \textbf{\"{u}} is the front-rounded vowel --- say
\emph{ee} with rounded lips --- so \emph{tsch\"{u}ss} is roughly
\emph{chuess}, short and clipped.
\end{sounds}

Between friends, Germans rarely say the full \emph{auf Wiedersehen} --- they
say \textbf{tsch\"{u}ss}, a breezy ``bye.'' And it hides one of the best
secrets in this whole chapter.

\begin{etymology}
\emph{tsch\"{u}ss} looks purely German, but trace it back and it leaves the
country. French \textbf{adieu} (``to God'') $\to$ borrowed into
\textbf{Walloon} (a language of southern Belgium) as \textbf{adj\^{u}s} $\to$
carried north into \textbf{Low German} dialects as \textbf{atsch\"{u}s /
atsch\"{u}ss} $\to$ clipped to modern \textbf{tsch\"{u}ss}.\\[3pt]
So the chain is \emph{adieu} $\to$ \emph{adj\^{u}s} $\to$ \emph{atsch\"{u}ss}
$\to$ \emph{tsch\"{u}ss} --- and since \emph{adieu} is \emph{\`{a} Dieu}, ``to
God,'' the casual German \emph{tsch\"{u}ss} is a far-travelled cousin of French
\textbf{adieu} (``to God'') and Spanish \textbf{adi\'{o}s} (``to God''): all
three the same little blessing, just worn down more and more. The friendliest,
most throwaway German goodbye is secretly the most solemn one.
\end{etymology}

\begin{culture}
You will also hear \textbf{tschau} --- that one is Italian \emph{ciao},
borrowed straight.
\end{culture}

\begin{grammarlens}
\textbf{Register.} \emph{tsch\"{u}ss} --- friends, family, peers, casual shops;
never in a formal meeting. \emph{auf Wiedersehen} --- strangers, elders,
officials, the telephone. It is the same split you have drilled all along
(\emph{du} vs \emph{Sie}): pick the parting to match the relationship.
\end{grammarlens}

\section{\emph{bis bald} --- ``see you soon''}
\label{lesson:bis-bald}

\begin{sounds}
\emph{bis} $=$ \emph{biss} (short \emph{i}). \emph{bald} $=$ \emph{balt} --- a
final \emph{d} always hardens to \emph{t} in German (final devoicing). The
whole phrase: \emph{biss BALT}.
\end{sounds}

German's soft goodbyes all start with \textbf{bis}, ``until'' --- the German
version of Spanish \emph{hasta} and French \emph{\`{a}}. First up: \textbf{bis
bald}, ``until soon'' $\to$ ``see you soon,'' the twin of \emph{hasta pronto}
and \emph{\`{a} bient\^{o}t}.

\begin{morphologybox}
\textbf{bis} $=$ ``until, up to'' --- German's farewell preposition. Just as
Spanish builds \emph{hasta luego} / \emph{hasta ma\~{n}ana} and French builds
\emph{\`{a} bient\^{o}t} / \emph{\`{a} demain}, German builds \emph{bis} $+$ a
time word.\\[3pt]
\textbf{bald} $=$ ``soon.''
\end{morphologybox}

\begin{cousinweb}
\textbf{bald} (``soon'') $\leftarrow$ Old High German \emph{bald}, which meant
``\textbf{bold}, quick, brave'' --- and that is the \emph{same word as English}
\textbf{bold}. The sense drifted: what is done \emph{boldly} is done
\emph{quickly} $\to$ \emph{soon}. So German ``soon'' and English ``bold'' are
one word; German kept the speed, English kept the courage.
\end{cousinweb}

\begin{grammarlens}
\textbf{The \emph{bis\ldots} family.}
\end{grammarlens}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
German & ``Until\ldots'' & Spanish / French twin \\
\midrule
\emph{bis bald} & soon & \emph{hasta pronto} / \emph{\`{a} bient\^{o}t} \\
\emph{bis sp\"{a}ter} & later & \emph{hasta luego} / \emph{\`{a} plus tard} \\
\emph{bis morgen} & tomorrow & \emph{hasta ma\~{n}ana} / \emph{\`{a} demain} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\emph{bis sp\"{a}ter} --- ``until later'' --- uses \emph{sp\"{a}ter},
``later,'' the comparative of \emph{sp\"{a}t}, ``late.'' \emph{bis morgen}
comes next.

\section{\emph{bis morgen} --- ``see you tomorrow'' (and \emph{Morgen} comes back)}
\label{lesson:bis-morgen}

\begin{sounds}
The German \emph{r} is the guttural one, made at the back: \emph{morgen} $=$
\emph{MOR-gen}. Whole phrase: \emph{biss MOR-gen}.
\end{sounds}

Parting for the day: \textbf{bis morgen}, ``until tomorrow.'' It brings back
\textbf{Morgen} --- the ``morning'' from your very first chapter (\emph{Guten
Morgen!}) --- now doing a second job.

\begin{morphologybox}
\textbf{bis} $=$ ``until'' (last lesson).\\[3pt]
\textbf{morgen} $=$ ``tomorrow'' \emph{and} ``morning,'' exactly the double
meaning of Spanish \emph{ma\~{n}ana} and French \emph{demain}. German simply
uses the one word for both: \emph{Guten Morgen!} $=$ ``Good \textbf{morning}!'';
\emph{bis morgen} $=$ ``until \textbf{tomorrow}.'' When it means ``morning'' it
is a capital-M noun, \emph{der Morgen}; as ``tomorrow'' it is lowercase,
\emph{morgen}.
\end{morphologybox}

\begin{cousinweb}
\textbf{Morgen} is a straight relative of English \textbf{morning} and the old
word \textbf{morrow} --- ``see you on the \emph{morrow}'' is \emph{bis morgen}
almost word for word, and ``to\emph{morrow}'' is literally ``to (the) morrow.''
So all three tracks make ``tomorrow'' out of ``morning'': German
\textbf{Morgen}, Spanish \textbf{ma\~{n}ana} ($\leftarrow$ Latin \emph{m\=ane}),
French \textbf{demain} ($\leftarrow$ \emph{d\=e m\=ane}). Same idea, three
routes.
\end{cousinweb}

\begin{grammarlens}
\textbf{The \emph{bis\ldots} farewells, complete.} You now have German's full
soft-goodbye set --- \emph{bis bald} (soon), \emph{bis sp\"{a}ter} (later),
\emph{bis morgen} (tomorrow) --- plus \emph{auf Wiedersehen} (formal) and
\emph{tsch\"{u}ss} (casual).
\end{grammarlens}

\section{Saying goodbye, start to finish}
\label{lesson:ch4-practice}

No new words. The farewells now close a real conversation and fold back into
everything since \emph{hallo}.

\noindent
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.30\linewidth}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}X@{}}
\toprule
You're\ldots & Say \\
\midrule
formal / to a stranger & \emph{Auf Wiedersehen.} \\
casual / to a friend & \emph{Tsch\"{u}ss.} \\
meeting again soon & \emph{Bis bald.} \\
leaving for the day & \emph{Bis morgen.} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}

\noindent Everything from Chapters \ref{ch:greetings}--\ref{ch:farewells}, in
one exchange:

\begin{center}\itshape
--- Hallo! Ich hei\ss{}e Anna. Wie hei\ss{}en Sie?\\
--- Ich hei\ss{}e Peter. Freut mich.\\
--- Wie geht es Ihnen?\\
--- Danke, gut. Und Ihnen?\\
--- Sehr gut. Also\ldots bis morgen, Peter.\\
--- Bis morgen, Anna. Auf Wiedersehen!
\end{center}

\noindent Between friends, that last line becomes \emph{Tsch\"{u}ss, bis
morgen!}

\noindent
\begin{tabular}{@{}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.30\textwidth}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.63\textwidth}@{}}
\toprule
Built this chapter & \\
\midrule
\emph{auf Wiedersehen} & the formal goodbye (``on the seeing-again'';
\emph{sehen} $=$ \emph{see}; twin of \emph{au revoir}) \\
\emph{tsch\"{u}ss} & casual bye (secretly \emph{adieu}, ``to God,'' cousin of
\emph{adi\'{o}s}) \\
\emph{bis bald}\slash{} \emph{sp\"{a}ter}\slash{} \emph{morgen} & soon / later / tomorrow
--- \emph{bis} $+$ a time, like \emph{hasta} and \emph{\`{a}} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}

Which German goodbye promises a ``re-seeing,'' and which language matches it?
\emph{Auf Wiedersehen}; French \emph{au revoir}. What does the casual
\emph{tsch\"{u}ss} secretly mean? ``To God.'' You can now hold a whole German
exchange, hello to goodbye. Next chapter: the first verbs, and your first
self-built sentence.
