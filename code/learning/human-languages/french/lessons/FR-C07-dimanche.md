# dimanche — Sunday

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Six days have ended in **-di**. The seventh breaks the pattern in
public — and once you see how, it is not a break at all.

## Sounds you'll need
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[FR-LEX-DIMANCHE-11]; assesses=[] -->

- `ch-sh` — the French **ch** is *sh*, as in English *machine*. Never *ch* as in
  *church*.
- `nasal-an` — the **an** is nasal, the same open nasal as the **en** of
  **vendredi**: French spells that one sound two ways.
- Together: **dimanche** = *dee-MAHNSH*.

## Grammar Lens: the ending that moved to the front
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[] -->

Look at the first two letters. **Di**manche. That is the **same *diēs*** every
other day of the week ends with, sitting at the front instead.

> **diēs Dominica** → *di-manche* → **dimanche**

Latin let the two words come in either order, and for this one day the order
that survived is "day of the Lord" rather than "the Lord's day." So the pattern
did not break; it flipped. All seven days still carry *diēs*, and *dimanche* is
the only one that wears it where you can see it first.

## Why it's said this way: two different rewrites
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[FR-ETYMON-DIMANCHE-DOMINICA-12]; assesses=[] -->

*Dominica* comes from **Dominus**, "lord, master" — which English holds in
**dominion**, **dominate**, and, worn much smoother, **dame**.

English kept **Sun**day, the star. French dropped it entirely for "the Lord's
day." Put beside *samedi*, that makes the weekend two different rewrites of the
same Roman week:

- **samedi** — the planet replaced by the **Sabbath**, a day of rest
- **dimanche** — the Sun replaced by the **Lord**, a day of worship

Five days of Roman sky, then two days where a later faith wrote over it. The
French week is a record of both.
