Lesson 1 of 5.
nūptiae, nūptiārum — a wedding.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for an envoy, then the Latin for a clerk.

You'll want to know: nūptiae, nūptiārum.
nūptiae, nūptiārum — "a wedding".

The word, taken apart.
nūptiae is built on nūpta, "a bride" (Lewis & Short), the participle of nūbō, "to marry" (EtymDB 2.1). English nuptial comes from it.

coniūnx, coniugis — "a husband or wife, a spouse".

The word, taken apart.
coniūnx is built on coniungō, "to join together", from con-, "together", and iungō, "to join": one joined in marriage. Its root is the one in iugum, "a yoke".

Lesson 3 of 5.
parēns, parentis — a parent.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a wedding, then the Latin for a husband or wife.

You'll want to know: parēns, parentis.
parēns, parentis — "a parent".

The word, taken apart.
parēns is the present participle of pariō, "to bring forth, to give birth to" (EtymDB 2.1): the one who brings forth. It is a different word from pārēns, "obeying", from pāreō, "to obey".

Lesson 4 of 5.
nepōs, nepōtis — a grandson.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a husband or wife, then the Latin for a parent.

You'll want to know: nepōs, nepōtis.
nepōs, nepōtis — "a grandson".

The word, taken apart.
nepōs is inherited, usually traced to Proto-Indo-European nepōts, "grandson, nephew" (EtymDB 2.1); German Neffe, "nephew", is a cousin (Lewis & Short). English nephew comes from nepōs through French.

Lesson 5 of 5.
neptis, neptis — a granddaughter.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a parent, then the Latin for a grandson.

You'll want to know: neptis, neptis.
neptis, neptis — "a granddaughter".

The word, taken apart.
neptis is inherited, usually traced to Proto-Indo-European neptih2, "granddaughter, niece" (EtymDB 2.1), the feminine partner of nepōs, "grandson" (Lewis & Short). English niece comes from it through French.
