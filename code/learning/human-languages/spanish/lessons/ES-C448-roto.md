## You'll want to know: roto
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-C448-MUDA-05]; assesses=[ES-LEX-ROMPER] -->

**roto** — broken, torn, in pieces.

*Un vaso roto* — a broken glass. *Los pantalones están rotos* — the trousers
are torn. *He roto el plato* — I've broken the plate.

It agrees like any adjective: *roto, rota, rotos, rotas*.

## Grammar Lens: the participle that lost its middle
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C448-MUDA-05, ES-LEX-ROMPER] -->

A regular *-er* verb would give *romp-ido*. Spanish says **roto**, and it is
one of a small group that behave this way:

| verb | the participle you expect | the one Spanish uses |
|---|---|---|
| romper | *rompido* | **roto** |
| escribir | *escribido* | **escrito** |
| abrir | *abrido* | **abierto** |

These short forms are the older ones. They came straight down from Latin
participles that were already short, and because the verbs are used so
constantly, the regular pattern never got a grip on them. The same thing
happens in English, where *wrote* survives beside *worked*.

One form covers both jobs: *he roto el plato* after *haber*, and *el plato
está roto* as a plain description.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C448-MUDA-05, ES-LEX-C448-MUDA-04] -->

Latin **rumpere**, to burst, with participle **ruptus** — and *ruptus* is what
became *roto*.

English borrowed the same participle whole: a **rupture**, an **eruption**,
something **abrupt** that breaks off. Spanish let it wear down into a
two-syllable everyday word, and kept *ruptura* alongside for the borrowed,
formal sense.

Keep the pair from the lesson before this one in view. *Roto* is *ruptus*,
bursting. *Estropeado* is *turpis*, shameful. Spanish named the two kinds of
failure after two different pictures, and the pictures still sort them.
