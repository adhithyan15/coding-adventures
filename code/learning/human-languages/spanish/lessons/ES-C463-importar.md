# importar — to matter

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-IMPORTANTE, ES-LEX-C443-ANIMO-02] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say **importante** and **interesar**. The first has a verb inside
it, and that verb behaves like the second.

## You'll want to know: importar
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-C463-GUIA-03]; assesses=[ES-LEX-IMPORTANTE] -->

**importar** — to matter, to be of concern.

*No importa* — it doesn't matter. *¿Te importa si abro la ventana?* — do you
mind if I open the window? *Me importa mucho* — it matters a lot to me.

It also means to import goods, which is the literal sense, but the everyday
one is mattering.

## Grammar Lens: it works like gustar and interesar
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C463-GUIA-03, ES-LEX-C443-ANIMO-02] -->

The thing that matters is the **subject**, and the person is an indirect
object — the same shape as *interesar*:

| Spanish | word for word |
|---|---|
| *me interesa el arte* | art interests me |
| *me importa el resultado* | the result matters to me |
| *no me importan los horarios* | the timetables don't matter to me |

The verb agrees with the **thing**, not the person: *importan* for plural
timetables. English puts the person first and Spanish puts the thing, which is
why *I don't mind* comes out as *no me importa* — literally "it doesn't matter
to me".

That makes **¿te importa…?** the standard polite request: you are asking
whether a thing would matter to them, not asking permission.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C463-GUIA-03, ES-LEX-IMPORTANTE] -->

Latin **importare**: **in-**, in, plus **portare**, **to carry**. To carry
something **in**.

Importing goods is carrying them in, and that is the whole of what Rome meant
by it. **Mattering** is the later half, invented inside the Romance languages
long after Latin — you met that split under *importante*, and this is the verb
it was cut from. A thing that *imports* brings weight in with it.

*Portare* is a wide family: **transportar**, **exportar**, English **portable**
and the **porter** who carries your bags. Keep it apart from its look-alike
*porta*, a **gate** — the word inside *la puerta*, *el puerto* and **el
pasaporte**, which carries you through a gate rather than carrying anything
itself. You were warned about this pair at *la puerta*, where English's two
*porter*s turned out to be two different words.
