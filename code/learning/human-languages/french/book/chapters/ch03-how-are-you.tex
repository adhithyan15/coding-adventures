\chapter{How Are You?}
\label{ch:how-are-you}

Now the small talk that follows a name --- thanking, answering a thank-you, and
asking how someone is doing. French asks that last question with a verb of
\emph{motion}: not ``how are you?'' but ``how does it \emph{go}?''

\begin{center}\itshape
--- Comment allez-vous?\\
--- Tr\`{e}s bien, merci. Et vous?\\
--- Comme ci, comme \c{c}a.
\end{center}

Each piece is taken apart first, then assembled.

\emph{Practice lessons: \nolinkurl{lessons/FR-C03-*.md}.}

\section{\emph{merci} --- ``thank you,'' and it means \emph{mercy}}
\label{lesson:merci}

\begin{sounds}
\emph{mehr-SEE}, with the soft guttural French \emph{r} made at the back of the
throat (never a tapped or rolled \emph{r}). The stress falls at the end:
\emph{mehr-\textbf{SEE}}.
\end{sounds}

\begin{cousinweb}
\textbf{merci} (``thank you'') $\leftarrow$ Latin \emph{merc\=es} (``wages,
reward, price, that which is earned''). Saying \emph{merci} originally invoked a
\textbf{reward} owed for a kindness --- and, in old religious use, a plea for
divine \textbf{mercy}, a reward you have not earned.\\[3pt]
That one root \emph{merc-} (``pay, trade, reward'') pays out a large English
family: \textbf{mercy}, \textbf{merci}ful (the reward/pity sense);
\textbf{mercenary} (a soldier who fights for \emph{wages}); and, through Latin
\emph{merc\=ar\={\i}} (``to trade''), \textbf{merch}ant, \textbf{comm}erce,
\textbf{mark}et, \textbf{merc}antile. Even \textbf{Mercury}, the Roman god of
merchants, is family.
\end{cousinweb}

\begin{culture}
\textbf{Three sisters, three pictures of gratitude.} The Romance languages each
chose a different metaphor for ``thank you.'' French \emph{merci} comes from
\emph{merc\=es}, a \textbf{reward} (and so, mercy). Spanish \emph{gracias} comes
from Latin \emph{gr\=atia}, \textbf{grace} or favour. Portuguese
\emph{obrigado} comes from Latin \emph{oblig\=atus}, ``I am \textbf{obliged}.''
Same feeling, three quite different pictures --- French hands you a reward,
Spanish hands you grace, Portuguese declares itself in your debt.
\end{culture}

\section{\emph{de rien} --- ``you're welcome'': ``of nothing''}
\label{lesson:de-rien}

\begin{sounds}
\emph{duh ryan}, where that final syllable is \textbf{nasal}: the air goes out
through the nose and there is no hard \emph{n} at the end. Two things to keep:
the nasal ending of \emph{rien}, and the guttural French \emph{r} at the back of
the throat.
\end{sounds}

\begin{cousinweb}
\textbf{de} (``of, from'') $\leftarrow$ Latin \emph{d\=e}. \textbf{rien}
(``nothing'') $\leftarrow$ Latin \emph{rem}, the accusative of \emph{r\=es}, ``a
thing.''\\[3pt]
In Latin \emph{rem} meant a \textbf{thing}, something real. French kept using it
inside negations --- \emph{ne \ldots rien}, ``not \ldots a thing'' --- until the
word for \emph{thing} absorbed the negation around it and \textbf{rien came to
mean ``nothing'' all by itself}. Latin \emph{r\=es} also gives English
\textbf{real}, \textbf{re}public (\emph{r\=es p\=ublica}, ``the public thing''),
and \textbf{rebus}. So \emph{rien} is, at the root, a \emph{real thing} that
learned to mean nothing at all.
\end{cousinweb}

\begin{culture}
\textbf{The same move, twice.} Spanish did the identical thing with
\emph{nada}, which comes from Latin \emph{n\=ata}, ``a born thing'' --- again a
\emph{thing} word that collapsed into a \emph{nothing} word. The two replies sit
side by side:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
Language & ``You're welcome'' & The ``nothing'' word \\
\midrule
French & \emph{de rien} & \emph{rien} $\leftarrow$ \emph{rem}, ``a thing'' \\
Spanish & \emph{de nada} & \emph{nada} $\leftarrow$ \emph{n\=ata}, ``a born thing'' \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Two sister languages, one instinct: wave the favour off as no \emph{thing} at
all.
\end{culture}

\section{\emph{aller} --- ``to go,'' the verb behind ``how are you?''}
\label{lesson:aller}

\begin{sounds}
The infinitive ending \emph{-er} is a clean ``ay'': \emph{aller} $=$
\emph{ah-LAY}. The doubled \emph{ll} is just one \emph{l} sound, and the
\emph{r} of the ending is silent.
\end{sounds}

\begin{cousinweb}
\textbf{aller} (``to go'') is one of the great messes of French: it is
\textbf{suppletive}, meaning it was stitched together out of \textbf{three}
different Latin verbs, and the seams still show in its everyday forms.\\[3pt]
\emph{je vais} (``I go'') $\leftarrow$ Latin \emph{v\=adere}, ``to go, to
rush'' $\to$ English \textbf{invade}, \textbf{evade}, \textbf{wade} --- all
going-words.\\[3pt]
\emph{nous allons} (``we go''), and the infinitive \emph{aller} itself,
$\leftarrow$ Latin \emph{ambul\=are}, ``to walk'' $\to$ English \textbf{amble},
\textbf{ambul}ance (a walking hospital), \textbf{pre}amble,
\textbf{perambul}ate.\\[3pt]
\emph{j'irai} (``I will go'') $\leftarrow$ Latin \emph{\={\i}re}, ``to go'' $\to$
English \textbf{exit} (\emph{ex-it}, ``he goes out''), \textbf{transit},
\textbf{init}ial.\\[3pt]
So one French verb carries three separate Latin roots for motion.
\end{cousinweb}

\begin{grammarlens}
\textbf{``Going'' used for ``being.''} You do not need to conjugate
\emph{aller} yet --- only to recognize one form: \emph{\c{c}a va}, ``it goes''
(the \emph{va} is the \emph{v\=adere} piece). Many languages borrow a verb of
motion to talk about a \emph{state}: English asks ``how are you getting on?'' or
``how's it going?'' French made exactly that the standard question. Spanish
instead asks with \emph{estar}, ``to stand'' (\emph{\textquestiondown c\'{o}mo est\'{a}s?}).
One language stands, the other walks --- two different pictures of a life.
\end{grammarlens}

\section{\emph{comment \c{c}a va?} --- ``how are you?''}
\label{lesson:comment-ca-va}

\emph{comment} (how, \S\ref{lesson:comment}) $+$ \emph{\c{c}a} (it, this) $+$
\emph{va} (goes, from \emph{aller}, \S\ref{lesson:aller}) $=$ \textbf{Comment
\c{c}a va?} --- word for word, ``How does \emph{it} go?'' and the single most
useful question in spoken French.

\emph{\c{c}a} means ``it, this,'' worn down from Latin \emph{ecce hoc},
``behold this.'' The two-word version, \emph{\c{C}a va?} (``It goes?''), is what
people actually say --- and you can answer it with the very same two words:
\emph{\c{C}a va.} (``It goes'' $=$ ``I'm fine.'')

\begin{grammarlens}
\textbf{A ladder of three registers.} French offers a neat climb from casual to
formal. \emph{tu} is the familiar ``you'' (a friend, a peer); \emph{vous} is the
formal or plural ``you'' (a stranger, an elder, a group).

\begin{center}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}ll>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}X@{}}
\toprule
Register & Question & Literally \\
\midrule
Casual, universal & \emph{Comment \c{c}a va?} (or \emph{\c{C}a va?}) & ``How does \emph{it} go?'' \\
Informal, one friend & \emph{Comment vas-tu?} & ``How go \emph{you}?'' \\
Formal or plural & \emph{Comment allez-vous?} & ``How go \emph{you} (vous)?'' \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

\emph{vas} and \emph{allez} are simply the \emph{tu} and \emph{vous} forms of
\emph{aller}. For a stranger or a boss, reach for \emph{Comment allez-vous?}
\end{grammarlens}

\begin{sounds}
\emph{comment} ends in a nasal vowel and a silent \emph{t}: \emph{ko-mahn}. But
in \emph{Comment allez-vous} that silent \emph{t} wakes up and glues onto the
following vowel --- this is called \textbf{liaison} ---
\emph{ko-mahn-ta-lay-VOO}.
\end{sounds}

\section{\emph{comme ci, comme \c{c}a} --- the classic French shrug}
\label{lesson:comme-ci-comme-ca}

Someone asks \emph{\c{C}a va?} and you are neither great nor bad. The perfect
answer --- a phrase English borrowed whole --- is \textbf{comme ci, comme
\c{c}a}, literally ``like this, like that.'' A verbal shrug of the shoulders.

\begin{cousinweb}
\textbf{comme} (``like, as'') $\leftarrow$ Latin \emph{qu\=omodo}, ``in what
manner'' --- the \emph{same} root as \emph{comment} (``how'',
\S\ref{lesson:comment}). One Latin word, two French children: \emph{comment} is
``in what manner?'' asked as a question, \emph{comme} is ``in the manner of,
like.''\\[3pt]
\textbf{ci} (``here, this'') $\leftarrow$ Latin \emph{ecce hic}, ``behold
here.'' \textbf{\c{c}a} (``there, that'') $\leftarrow$ Latin \emph{ecce hoc},
``behold this'' --- the very same \emph{\c{c}a} as in \emph{\c{C}a va?}
\end{cousinweb}

So you are literally waving a hand: \emph{a bit like this way, a bit like that
way} --- neither up nor down.

\begin{grammarlens}
\textbf{Your answer set for \emph{\c{C}a va?}} Recall \emph{bien}, ``well''
(\S\ref{lesson:bien}, from Latin \emph{bene}); \emph{\c{C}a va bien} is ``it's
going well.''

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}
\toprule
Answer & Sense \\
\midrule
\emph{(Tr\`{e}s) bien, merci} & (Very) well, thank you \\
\emph{\c{C}a va} & Fine, okay (``it goes'') \\
\emph{Comme ci, comme \c{c}a} & So-so \\
\emph{Pas mal} & Not bad \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\end{grammarlens}

\begin{culture}
Every language has its shrug, and each paints it differently: Spanish says
\emph{m\'{a}s o menos} (``more or less'') or \emph{regular}; Italian says
\emph{cos\`{\i} cos\`{\i}} (``so so''); French says \emph{comme ci, comme
\c{c}a} (``like this, like that''). Same gesture, three pictures.
\end{culture}

\section{The whole exchange}
\label{lesson:ch3-practice}

No new words --- this chapter's atoms assemble into a real exchange, run once
formally and once informally. The glue is \emph{et vous? / et toi?}, ``and
you?'' (\emph{et} $=$ ``and,'' from Latin \emph{et}; \emph{toi} is the stressed
form of \emph{tu}). It bounces the question straight back, exactly as Spanish
does with \emph{\textquestiondown y usted? / \textquestiondown y t\'{u}?}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Formal} --- a stranger, an elder:

\begin{center}\itshape
--- Bonjour. Comment allez-vous?\\
--- Tr\`{e}s bien, merci. Et vous?\\
--- Comme ci, comme \c{c}a.\\
--- [after a small favour] Merci! --- De rien.
\end{center}

\noindent\textbf{Informal} --- a friend:

\begin{center}\itshape
--- Salut! \c{C}a va?\\
--- \c{C}a va, et toi?\\
--- Comme ci, comme \c{c}a.
\end{center}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}
\toprule
French & English \\
\midrule
\emph{Merci.} & Thank you. \\
\emph{De rien.} & You're welcome. \\
\emph{Comment allez-vous?} & How are you? (formal) \\
\emph{Comment vas-tu?} & How are you? (informal) \\
\emph{\c{C}a va?} & How's it going? \\
\emph{\c{C}a va bien.} & It's going well. \\
\emph{Comme ci, comme \c{c}a.} & So-so. \\
\emph{Pas mal.} & Not bad. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Every piece came from a root: \emph{merci} from \emph{merc\=es} (``reward,
mercy''), \emph{rien} from \emph{rem} (``a thing''), \emph{aller} from
\emph{ambul\=are}, \emph{v\=adere} and \emph{\={\i}re}, \emph{comme} from
\emph{qu\=omodo}. Next chapter: the farewells --- \emph{au revoir}, \emph{\`{a}
bient\^{o}t}, \emph{\`{a} demain}.
