# બહેન — the sister who is not "sister"'s cousin

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GU-LEX-BHAI, GU-ETYMON-BHAI-BROTHER] -->

[PAUSE 2s] **ભાઈ** is English **brother**, unbroken, down to the root. Do not
assume the sibling pair matches. This chapter's last word breaks the pattern.

## You'll want to know first — one word
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[GU-LEX-BAHEN]; assesses=[] -->

> **બહેન** — *bahen* — **sister**

## The letters in this word
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[] -->

**બ** *ba*, **હ** *ha*, **ે** the *e* sign above the consonant, then **ન**
*na*. *Ba-hen*, every letter already yours from earlier chapters.

## The word, taken apart — a different root entirely
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[GU-ETYMON-BAHEN-BHAGA]; assesses=[GU-LEX-BHAI, GU-LEX-MITRA] -->

Here is the surprise: **બહેન** is **not** *bhāī*'s counterpart the way English
*sister* is *brother*'s. English *sister*, Latin *soror*, and Sanskrit
**svasṛ** all descend from one shared PIE root, \**swésōr*. Gujarati's word for
sister does not use that root at all.

**bahen** comes from Sanskrit **bhaginī**, through Prakrit *bhaginī*. Scholars
connect *bhaginī* to **bhaga**, "good fortune, a share" — the same *bhaga*
behind *bhāgya*, "fate" — plus a feminine suffix, so "sister" may originally
have meant something closer to "**the one who shares**." That connection is
debated rather than settled, the way *roṭlī*'s root was: some scholars accept
the *bhaga* link, others have argued against it. What is certain is what it is
**not**: unlike *bhāī*, *bahen* is no relation to English *sister* at all —
and unlike the disputed-but-real *mitra*/*meherbānī* pair, this dial lands on
"unrelated," full stop.
