\chapter{Bread, Water, Wine}
\label{ch:food}

Three words from the table, and three different histories. Two of them German
speakers have always had; one of them Rome carried north together with the
plant it came from. On the way, the last of German's three genders finally
appears. \emph{Practice lessons: \texttt{lessons/GE-C11-*.md}.}

\section{\emph{das Brot} --- bread, and the third gender}
\label{lesson:brot}

\begin{sounds}
\emph{broht}, with a long \emph{o} as in English \emph{boat}; the German
\emph{r} is made far back, in the throat.
\end{sounds}

Where the Romance sisters all say a version of Latin \emph{p\=anis} (French
\emph{pain}, Italian \emph{pane}), German goes its \textbf{own Germanic way}:
\emph{Brot}.

\begin{cousinweb}
\textbf{das Brot} (``bread'') $\leftarrow$ old Germanic \emph{*braudam} --- the
same word that became English \textbf{bread}. No Latin here: this is a
\textbf{native} word, like \emph{Vater} and \emph{Wasser}, out of the Germanic
core the two languages share.
\end{cousinweb}

\begin{grammarlens}
\textbf{The third gender --- \emph{das}.} You have met \textbf{der}
(masculine, \emph{der Vater}) and \textbf{die} (feminine, \emph{die Mutter}).
German has a \textbf{third}: \textbf{das}, the \textbf{neuter} --- and
\emph{Brot} is one of its nouns: \textbf{das Brot}. So the article set is now
complete: \textbf{der / die / das}.
\end{grammarlens}

\begin{culture}
German nouns are also always \textbf{Capitalized} --- \emph{Brot},
\emph{Vater}, \emph{Wasser} --- a rule no other language in this book follows.
\end{culture}

\section[\emph{Wasser} and \emph{Wein}]{\emph{das Wasser}, \emph{der Wein} --- one word Germans always had, one Rome brought}
\label{lesson:wasser-wein}

Two drinks, two very different histories. \emph{Wasser} is a word Germanic
speakers have \textbf{always had}; \emph{Wein} is one Rome \textbf{carried
north} with the grapevine. Side by side, they show the difference between an
\emph{inherited} word and an ancient \emph{loan}.

\begin{sounds}
In German the letter \textbf{w} is pronounced \textbf{v}: \emph{Wasser} $=$
\emph{VAH-ser}, \emph{Wein} $=$ \emph{vine}. And \textbf{ei} is always said
like English \emph{eye}.
\end{sounds}

\begin{cousinweb}
\textbf{das Wasser} (``water'') --- the Grimm's-law twin of English
\textbf{water}: the same Germanic root \emph{*watar}, the same core meaning,
both native. Like \emph{Brot} and \emph{Vater}, no Latin needed --- and neuter,
\textbf{das} Wasser.
\end{cousinweb}

\begin{etymology}
\textbf{der Wein} (``wine'') --- here is the twist: \emph{Wein} is \textbf{not}
a native Germanic word. It was \textbf{borrowed from Latin \emph{v\={\i}num}}
in Roman times, when the legions brought \textbf{viticulture} north of the
Alps. The Germanic tribes took the drink \emph{and its name} --- which is
exactly why English \textbf{wine}, German \textbf{Wein}, and Latin
\emph{v\={\i}num} all match: they are one \textbf{loan}, not three cousins.
(Masculine: \emph{der} Wein.)
\end{etymology}

\begin{culture}
\textbf{The lesson in one line:} \emph{Wasser} is a word Germans \textbf{always
had}; \emph{Wein} is a word Rome \textbf{gave} them --- the vine came with a
vocabulary.
\end{culture}
