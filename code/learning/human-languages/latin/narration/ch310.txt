Latin, chapter 310: Vērum aut falsum.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
iūrō, iūrāre — to swear (an oath).

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a patron, then the Latin for evidence.

You'll want to know: iūrō, iūrāre.
iūrō, iūrāre — "to swear (an oath)".
Testis iūrāvit sē vēra dīcere. The witness swore that he was telling the truth.
iūrō, iūrāre, iūrāvī, iūrātum, with accusative and infinitive. English jury comes from it through French.

The word, taken apart.
iūrō is built on iūs, "right, law" (EtymDB): to bind oneself by law. English jury comes from it through French.

Lesson 2 of 5.
periūrium, periūriī — perjury, a false oath.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for evidence, then the Latin for to swear.

You'll want to know: periūrium, periūriī.
periūrium, periūriī — "perjury, a false oath".
Periūrium grave crīmen erat. Perjury was a serious crime.
Neuter, from periūrus, 'oath-breaking': per- here means 'wrongly'. English perjury.

The word, taken apart.
periūrium is built on periūrus, "oath-breaking" (EtymDB), from per-, here "wrongly", and the root of iūrō, "to swear". English perjury comes from it.

Lesson 3 of 5.
mentior, mentīrī — to lie, to tell a lie.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to swear, then the Latin for perjury.

You'll want to know: mentior, mentīrī.
mentior, mentīrī — "to lie, to tell a lie".
Servus mentītus est; nam pōculum ipse frēgerat. The slave lied, for he had broken the cup himself.
mentior, mentīrī, mentītus sum, a deponent. Its noun mendācium, 'a lie', is taught.

The word, taken apart.
mentior is probably built, says Lewis & Short, on the root of mēns, "mind", with a first sense "to invent, to make up"; this is not certain.

Lesson 4 of 5.
arguō, arguere — to prove; to accuse.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for perjury, then the Latin for to lie.

You'll want to know: arguō, arguere.
arguō, arguere — "to prove; to accuse".
Haec epistula eum mentītum esse arguit. This letter proves that he lied.
arguō, arguere, arguī, argūtum: to make clear, and so to prove or to charge. English argue.

The word, taken apart.
arguō first meant "to make clear"; Lewis & Short connect it with the root of argentum, "silver", and Greek argos, "bright". English argue comes from it through French.

Lesson 5 of 5.
indicium, indiciī — information, evidence; a sign.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to lie, then the Latin for to prove.

You'll want to know: indicium, indiciī.
indicium, indiciī — "information, evidence; a sign".
Servus cōnsulī indicium dedit. A slave gave information to the consul.
Neuter. Indicium facere: to give information. A clue in a story is also an indicium.

The word, taken apart.
indicium is built on index, "one who points out, an informer" (EtymDB), and so on the verb meaning "to point out": a pointing out.
