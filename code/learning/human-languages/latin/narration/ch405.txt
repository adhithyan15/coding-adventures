Latin, chapter 405: Counting and Dictation.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
abacus, abacī — a counting board; a sideboard.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for geometry, then the Latin for a first principle.

You'll want to know: abacus, abacī.
abacus, abacī — "a counting board; a sideboard".
Puer in abacō numerōs computat. The boy works out sums on the counting board.
abacus, abacī m., borrowed from Greek abax, a board. Pebbles moved along lines on the board. English abacus.

The word, taken apart.
abacus was borrowed from Greek abax, abakos "a board, a counting table" (EtymDB; Lewis & Short). English took it over as abacus.

Lesson 2 of 5.
calculus, calculī — a pebble, a counter; a reckoning.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a first principle, then the Latin for a counting board.

You'll want to know: calculus, calculī.
calculus, calculī — "a pebble, a counter; a reckoning".
Calculīs numerābat. He was counting with pebbles.
calculus, calculī m., a little stone. Calculum pōnere, to reckon. English calculate and calculus.

The word, taken apart.
calculus is a small form of calx, calcis "a stone, lime" (Lewis & Short): a little pebble used for counting. English calculate comes from it.

Lesson 3 of 5.
ēnumerō, ēnumerāre — to count out, to list.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a counting board, then the Latin for a pebble.

You'll want to know: ēnumerō, ēnumerāre.
ēnumerō, ēnumerāre — "to count out, to list".
Omnia beneficia eius ēnumerāvit. He listed all his kindnesses.
ēnumerō, ēnumerāre, ēnumerāvī, ēnumerātum, ē + numerō. English enumerate.

The word, taken apart.
ēnumerō is built from ē "out" and numerō "to count", from numerus "a number" (Lewis & Short). English enumerate comes from it.

Lesson 4 of 5.
dictō, dictāre — to say repeatedly; to dictate.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a pebble, then the Latin for to count out.

You'll want to know: dictō, dictāre.
dictō, dictāre — "to say repeatedly; to dictate".
Magister versūs discipulīs dictābat. The teacher used to dictate verses to the pupils.
dictō, dictāre, dictāvī, dictātum, built on dīcō: to say over and over, for someone to take down. English dictate.

The word, taken apart.
dictō is built on dīcō "to say" (EtymDB; Lewis & Short), with the ending -tō of a repeated act. English dictate comes from it.

Lesson 5 of 5.
pēnsum, pēnsī — a task, a day's work.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to count out, then the Latin for to say repeatedly.

You'll want to know: pēnsum, pēnsī.
pēnsum, pēnsī — "a task, a day's work".
Ancillae pēnsum suum perfēcērunt. The maids finished their day's work.
pēnsum, pēnsī n., the participle of pendō, to weigh: the wool weighed out to a spinner each day, then any set task.

The word, taken apart.
pēnsum is the participle of pendō "to weigh out" (EtymDB; Lewis & Short): the wool weighed out for a day's spinning.
