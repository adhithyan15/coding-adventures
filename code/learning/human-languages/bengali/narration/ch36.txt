Lesson 1 of 5.
কে (-ke) — the one it happens to.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Which group is ভাই in and which is জামা in? Then say our family.
[your turn — recall: two chapters back, the word for he on the careful rung — tini]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

You'll want to know: কে.
ভাইকে — bhāike — to the brother, or the brother, as the one it is done to.
বন্ধুকে — bôndhuke — the friend, as the one it is done to.
Another ending on the back of the noun, and it marks something new: that this person is not doing the action but receiving it. English marks that with word order alone; Bengali marks it on the word.
And it is choosy in a way you can now predict. কে (-ke) goes on জীব nouns — people — and not on things:
ভাইকে — yes.
জামাকে — no; a shirt stays জামা.
The animacy split you learned three chapters ago is doing this too. That one question keeps paying.

Grammar Lens: the same slot, still one at a time.
Line the endings up and the system is visible:
[a table of 4 rows, read aloud]
ending: -র. what it says: whose. goes on: anything.
ending: -কে (-ke). what it says: the one it happens to. goes on: living nouns.
ending: -দের. what it says: whose, plural. goes on: living nouns.
ending: -টা. what it says: the one we mean. goes on: things, mostly.
All four sit in the same slot at the back of the noun, and the rule from the last chapter still holds: one at a time. That is why -দের exists at all, and why you will never see two of these stacked.

Lesson 2 of 5.
আমাকে (āmāke) — me.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Put the object ending on ভাই. Then say I and my.

You'll want to know: আমাকে.
আমাকে — āmāke — me, to me.
A pronoun is a living noun, so it takes the living noun's ending — the third time this book has said that and the third time it has been true without an exception:
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
job: I. the word: আমি. ending: —.
job: my. the word: আমার. ending: -র.
job: me. the word: আমাকে (āmāke). ending: -কে (-ke).
Same stem আমা-, three endings, no memorisation.

Grammar Lens: the frame that had no filler.
Chapter nine taught something odd and important: Bengali does not say I like tea. It says, near enough, tea is good to me — the liking is not something you do, it is something that happens to you.
That frame needs a to me, and the book had none. It has one now:
আমাকে চা ভালো লাগে — āmāke chā bhālo lāge — I like tea (literally, tea feels good to me).
A whole family of Bengali sentences works this way — liking, needing, feeling cold, being hungry — and every one of them was out of reach for want of this word.

Lesson 3 of 5.
তোমাকে (tomāke) — you, as the one it happens to.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, because the lesson points at something written down. You can listen to all of it now and come back to the parts that need looking at once you have stopped.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say me. Then say the plain and the careful words for you.

You'll want to know: তোমাকে.
তোমাকে — tomāke — you, as the one it is done to.
And with the third person, built from the stem you already met in তার:
তাকে — tāke — him, her.
Now the whole table, and it is worth looking at as a table rather than as a list:
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
person: আমি. possessive: আমার. object: আমাকে (āmāke).
person: তুমি. possessive: তোমার. object: তোমাকে (tomāke).
person: সে. possessive: তার. object: তাকে.
Three stems, three endings, nine words, nothing irregular.

Grammar Lens: what English makes you memorise and Bengali does not.
The English column is I, my, me — he, his, him, and there is no rule in it: a learner memorises each cell. The Bengali column is one stem with -র or -কে (-ke) stuck on. Nothing about তাকে has to be learned once তার is known.
The careful rungs behave, too: আপনাকে and তাঁকে.
And you can now be polite to a specific person, which chapter twenty-two could not do — it taught দয়া করে with nobody to address it to.

Lesson 4 of 5.
কে (-ke) (ke) — who?

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, because the lesson points at something written down. You can listen to all of it now and come back to the parts that need looking at once you have stopped.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Put the object ending on ভাই. Then say you, as the one it is done to.

You'll want to know: কে standing alone.
কে (-ke)? — ke? — who?
সে কে (-ke)? — she ke? — who is he?
This is a different word from the ending you learned two lessons ago. They are spelled identically and they are not related, and Bengali tells them apart the way it tells ও apart from ও: by where they sit.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
where: stuck to the back of a noun. what it is: -কে (-ke), the object ending.
where: standing on its own. what it is: কে (-ke), who?
You now have both of the questions a stranger opens with. এটা কী? asks about a thing; কে (-ke)? asks about a person — and the two are told apart only by their vowel, so say them carefully.

Grammar Lens: the asking head, for the third time.
Chapter thirty laid out three heads on one frame — the relative য-, the demonstrative ত-, and the asking ক-. Every question word in this book starts with the last of them:
কী — what.
কেমন — how.
কখন — when.
কে (-ke) — who.
Four words, one letter at the front of each. And the answers come from the ত- column: তিনি, তার, তখন. Ask with ক-, answer with ত-.
