Tamil, chapter 76: But, and Or.
4 lessons. You can do the first 1 of them in the car; after that you will want to have stopped.


Lesson 1 of 4.
ஆனால் (āṉāl) — but.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Read சரியா. Then ask to be let inside.

You'll want to know: ஆனால்.
ஆனால் (āṉāl) — "but".
Chapter seventy-four joined two things. This joins two statements, and unlike -உம் it is a word of its own, standing between them.
தேநீர் வேண்டும், ஆனால் (āṉāl) பால் வேண்டாம்.
tēnīr vēṇḍum, āṉāl pāl vēṇḍām.
"I want tea, but I don't want milk."
Both halves are sentences you could already say. ஆனால் (āṉāl) sets the second against the first.
The word is built out of ஆகு, "to become": ஆனால் (āṉāl) began as "if that is so". A sentence that starts by granting the first half and then turns is exactly what but does.
In everyday speech the tail wears down and you will hear ஆனா. It is the same word.

What you've built.
Two sentences held together, with the second one pushing back.

Lesson 2 of 4.
அல்லது (alladu) — or.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, because the lesson points at something written down. You can listen to all of it now and come back to the parts that need looking at once you have stopped.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Ask to be let inside. Then say but in Tamil.

You'll want to know: அல்லது.
அல்லது (alladu) — "or".
Like ஆனால் (āṉāl), it is a word of its own and it stands between the two things it offers.
பால் அல்லது (alladu) தேநீர்.
pāl alladu tēnīr.
"Milk or tea."
Look at the shape of the word. அது is "that one", which you have had since chapter forty; அல்ல is a denial. அல்லது (alladu) is "it is not that" — and a list that says this, or if not that is an offer of a choice.
So Tamil's and is an ending, and Tamil's or is a small denial. Neither one is the word English would have led you to expect.
It works on two statements as well as two things:
நீங்கள் வரலாம், அல்லது (alladu) நான் வருகிறேன்.
"You may come, or I will come."

What you've built.
Two joins in two lessons: one that sets things against each other, one that offers a choice.

Lesson 3 of 4.
ஆனால் (āṉāl) — three shapes, all of them old.

Before we start: this one needs your hands, so it is not a driving lesson. You will want a pen and something to write on and your eyes, for letter shapes on the page. You can listen to everything else now — leave the sections called Script you'll notice: ஆனால் — the same long sound, written two ways and Guided Practice until you have stopped, and I will say so again when we reach it.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the page: say but, then say or.

Script you'll notice: ஆனால் — the same long sound, written two ways.
Read it in three pieces.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
piece: ஆ, ā, the free-standing long vowel, because the word opens on it.
piece: னா, ṉā, ன with the long ா sign.
piece: ல், l, ல with a புள்ளி, so it carries no vowel.
ஆ, னா, ல் becomes ஆனால் (āṉāl).
One word, and the long ā appears twice in it — first as a whole letter, then as a sign. That is the rule from இன்று and ஆம் shown twice inside a single word: at the front of a word a vowel needs its own shape, and anywhere after a consonant it becomes a mark.
The word ends on a புள்ளி, and that closing ல் is the piece everyday speech drops when it says ஆனா.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — read: ஆ — then னா — then ல்]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — read: ஆனால் (āṉāl)]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[once you have stopped driving — point: the two places the long ā is written, and say why they differ]
[your turn — recall: say āṉāl, then say alladu, then read ஆனால்]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — return to: say ām / illai, say sīkkiram and read பயம் — three distances back — then offer two of them with அல்லது (alladu)]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Why is the first ā a letter and the second a sign? (Because the word begins with the first one, and the second sits on ன.)


Lesson 4 of 4.
பால் வேண்டுமா, அல்லது தேநீர் வேண்டுமா? (pāl vēṇḍumā, alladu tēnīr vēṇḍumā?) — offering a choice.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Read ஆனால் (āṉāl). Then say or.

You'll want to know: two questions, one offer.
பால் வேண்டுமா, அல்லது தேநீர் வேண்டுமா? pāl vēṇḍumā, alladu tēnīr vēṇḍumā? — "Would you like milk, or would you like tea?"
Every piece is yours. வேண்டும் is "want", from chapter thirty-nine. The -ஆ on the end of each half is last chapter's question ending. அல்லது (alladu) sits between them.
Notice what Tamil does not do: it does not ask one question with two answers in it. It asks two whole questions and puts or between them. Each half could stand alone.
The shorter form drops the second verb, and a host at a doorway will say it that way:
பால் அல்லது (alladu) தேநீர்?
Answer it with the echo you learned for any yes-or-no question — name the one you want:
தேநீர்.

What you've built.
You can put a choice in front of somebody and take their answer.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — offer: pāl vēṇḍumā, alladu tēnīr vēṇḍumā?]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — offer short: pāl alladu tēnīr?]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — answer: name one of them]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — recall: say alladu, then read ஆனால் (āṉāl), then make the full offer]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — return to: read சரியா, say uṭaṉē and say varuttam — three distances back — then offer two of them with அல்லது (alladu)]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
How many questions does Tamil ask when it offers two things? (Two — one for each, with அல்லது (alladu) between.)
