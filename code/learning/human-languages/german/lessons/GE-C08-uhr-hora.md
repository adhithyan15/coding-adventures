# Uhr, taken apart

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GE-LEX-UHR-01, GE-GRAMMAR-NATIVE-VS-LOAN-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] You have twice met a language keeping some words and buying others.
Here is the third time, and it completes a picture.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[GE-ETYMON-UHR-HORA-01]; assesses=[GE-LEX-UHR-01] -->

*Uhr* comes from Latin ***hōra***, "hour" — the same word that became French
*heure*, Italian *ora* and English **hour**. It reached German through medieval
Latin and Low German *ūr(e)*.

Timekeeping arrived with Roman and monastic clocks, and the word rode in with
the machinery.

## Why it's said this way
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GE-GRAMMAR-NATIVE-VS-LOAN-01, GE-LEX-MONTAG-01, GE-LEX-EINS-01] -->

Three layers of the German day, three different origins:

| what | German | where it came from |
|---|---|---|
| the numbers | *eins, zwei, drei* | native Germanic |
| the weekdays | *Montag, Donnerstag* | Germanic gods |
| the clock | *Uhr* | Latin, from *hōra* |

German kept its own counting and its own gods. For the **machine that measures
hours** it reached for Rome.

That is the same rule you were given with the months and again with *Samstag*:
the words used every hour of every day are the hardest to displace, and the
words that arrive with an institution come in whole. A clock is an institution
arriving.
