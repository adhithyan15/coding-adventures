## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-C420-ARTS-01]; assesses=[] -->

**el teatro** — "the theatre." Latin *theatrum*, from Greek **théatron**,
"a place for viewing," built on *theáomai*, "to behold."

The Greeks named the building after **what the audience does**, not after what
happens on the stage. A theatre is a looking-place.

Now the payoff. That same *theá-* gives Greek *theōría* — "a viewing, a
contemplation" — which arrives in English as **theory**:

- **theat**re, amphi**theat**re — the looking-place.
- **theor**y, **theor**etical — what you see when you look hard at something.

A theory and a theatre are the same idea twice: **someone standing back and
watching.** Spanish keeps both, *teatro* and *teoría*, and they have never
stopped being cousins.

## Grammar Lens: two names, two ages
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C354-WHERE-28] -->

Set the two buildings beside each other:

| place | named after | age of the word |
|---|---|---|
| el teatro | what the audience does — viewing | ancient Greek |
| el cine | what the machine does — writing movement | about 1900 |

Both are masculine and both take *el*. One is among the oldest words in this
book and one is among the newest, and you learned them a chapter apart.
