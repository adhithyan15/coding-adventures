## You'll want to know: el principiante
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-C471-CURSO-04]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C471-CURSO-03] -->

**el principiante** — the beginner.

> Ahora voy con los **principiantes** y disfruto mucho más.

**La principiante** for a woman. It is the natural opposite of what you
learned a moment ago:

| the class | the person in it |
|---|---|
| un curso **avanzado** | un **principiante** |

## Grammar Lens: you could have built this one
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C471-CURSO-04, ES-FRIEND-ANTE-ANT] -->

[El estudiante](./ES-C09-estudiante.md) gave you the rule outright: **verb
plus -ante names the person doing it.** *Estudiar*, cut the *-ar*, add
*-ante*.

Here it is again, one step further back. From **el principio**, the
beginning, Spanish makes the verb **principiar**, to begin. Take that, cut the
*-ar*, add *-ante*:

> princip**iar** → princip**iante**

The one who is beginning. The ending has not changed its job since chapter
nine; the only new thing is the verb it is sitting on.

English wore the same ending down to *-ant*, which is why *participant*,
*assistant* and *inhabitant* all name the doer too.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C471-CURSO-04, ES-LEX-C348-CLOCK-11] -->

*El principio* already traced this for you: Latin **principium**, from
**princeps**, the one who takes first place — the same first-taking inside
English **prince**, **principal** and **principle**.

So a *principiante* is not somebody who is bad at the thing. The word says
only that they are at the **start** of it. Spanish has kept that neutral, and
so should you when you use it about yourself.
