Lesson 1 of 5.
कृपया (kṛpayā) — please (kindly).

Warm-up.
[pause 1 second]
First, the word you already use to thank someone: धन्यवाद.
[pause 2 seconds]
Hindi's dictionary word for "please" is really the word compassion in disguise — and it's more formal than you might expect.

You'll want to know — The word.
कृपया = please, literally "kindly / graciously." It's built on the Sanskrit noun कृपा (kṛpā), "grace, compassion, mercy" — so asking with kṛpayā means, at heart, "of your compassion."
That places Hindi in good company: Arabic asks min faḍlik ("from your grace"), and the Dravidian languages ask with daya ("compassion"). A whole swathe of Asia says please by naming the kindness it hopes for — a different instinct from French's "if it pleases you."
The first syllable hides a sound worth noticing: कृ is ka carrying the vocalic r (the ृ mark) — kṛ, the same ṛ vowel Sanskrit and the Dravidian scripts share. The next lesson takes that mark out of this word and writes it on its own.

You'll want to know — Be honest about how it's used.
Here's the catch a textbook often hides: कृपया is formal and bookish. You'll see it on signs and hear it in announcements ("kṛpayā mind the gap"), but in everyday speech Hindi speakers rarely say it. Real politeness usually rides on the verb instead — the respectful request ending -iye / -iyegā: baiṭhiye "please sit," suniye "please listen." And very often, people simply say the English word "please."
So learn kṛpayā to read it and to be formally polite — but know that the living "please" of Hindi is baked into the verb.

◌ृ (ṛ) — a small hook below the consonant.
It is a vowel sign, so it attaches to a consonant rather than standing on its own, and it replaces the a built into that consonant.
Worked through: क + ◌ृ (ṛ) = कृ.

Script: the name and the sound have come apart.
[once you have stopped driving — this part needs your eyes or your hands; if you are driving, skip ahead to the next part]
This mark is called the vocalic ṛ — a vowel made out of an r, which is a thing Sanskrit had and Hindi does not.
So a Hindi speaker says it as ri. You will see कृ written and hear kri. The spelling records where the word came from; the mouth does something simpler.
That is worth knowing early, because it explains a word that would otherwise look unreadable to you on the page and sound perfectly ordinary out loud.
[a table of 1 row, read aloud]
written: ◌ृ (ṛ). said: ri.
Nearly every word carrying this mark is one Hindi took from Sanskrit, which is why the mark is uncommon and the words that carry it are not.

Script you'll notice: अ.
[once you have stopped driving — this part needs your eyes or your hands; if you are driving, skip ahead to the next part]
अ — a.
It is an independent vowel — the shape the vowel a takes when a word begins with it, rather than the sign it becomes inside a word.
What it is made of:
a joined upper-and-lower left body.
a middle shoulder running into the right stem.
a right vertical stem.
the top shirorekhā.
You already say these, and every one of them has अ somewhere inside it:
अलविदा — goodbye (alvidā — a final farewell, Persian/Arabic-derived).

Writing: अ.
1. curve around the upper bowl, then continue down and around the lower bowl without lifting.
2. lift and sweep the middle shoulder right.
3. without lifting, climb up the right stem to the headline.
4. descend the right stem top-to-bottom.
5. lift and draw the top shirorekhā left-to-right.
Pen lifts: 2. The pen comes up 2 times and no more.
The source shows four movements; most native writers draw अ in three strokes (74% of HP Labs India's native-writer samples). This path keeps every movement in order and direction, and lifts only before the shoulder and the headline.
This is one attested teaching order and not a national standard — handwriting here is taught with school-to-school variation. Source: Saurmandal, ‘Devanagari अ stroke order.svg’, frames 1–4, Wikimedia Commons, 5 August 2023.

Script you'll notice: ◌े.
[once you have stopped driving — this part needs your eyes or your hands; if you are driving, skip ahead to the next part]
◌े — e.
It is a vowel sign. It is not a letter and never stands alone: it attaches to a consonant and replaces the a built into it with e. Replaces, not adds — the a is gone.
Where it sits: a stroke above the top bar.
Worked through: म + ◌े = मे — me.
You already say these, and every one of them has ◌े somewhere inside it:
नमस्ते namaste — hello / goodbye (namaste — "I bow to you").
शिरोरेखा shirorekhā — the head-line that Devanagari letters hang from.
मेरा merā — my.
मेरा नाम merā nām — assemble my name from known letters and show word spacing as two head-lines.
