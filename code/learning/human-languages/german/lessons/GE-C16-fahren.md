# fahren — "to travel, to drive"

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GE-LEX-KOMMEN-01, GE-GRAMMAR-PERFEKT-SEIN-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] *Gehen* is going on your feet. When there is a vehicle under you,
German changes the verb.

## You'll want to know: fahren
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[GE-LEX-FAHREN-01]; assesses=[GE-GRAMMAR-PERFEKT-SEIN-01] -->

> **fahren** — "to travel, to go by vehicle, to drive."

> **ich bin gefahren** — "I travelled, I drove."

German keeps two verbs where English makes do with *go*. You **gehen** on foot
and you **fahren** in anything with wheels — a car, a bus, a train, a bicycle.
Getting that pair right is one of the first things that makes a learner sound
German rather than translated.

It is motion, so the helper is *sein*.

## Sounds you'll need
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GE-SOUND-H-LENGTHEN-01, GE-LEX-FAHREN-01] -->

- `vowel-a-long` — the **a** is long and open, as in *war*.
- `h-silent-lengthening` — and the **h** after it is the silent stretcher you met
  in *gehen* and *sehen*: it is not breathed, it only holds the vowel. *FAH-ren*
  rhymes with the way *gehen* and *sehen* are built.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GE-LEX-FAHREN-01, GE-LEX-AUF-WIEDERSEHEN-01] -->

**fahren** is English **fare** — the old verb meaning "to travel," which English
demoted to a noun and then almost forgot. It is still there in:

| English word | what it is really saying |
|---|---|
| **farewell** | travel well |
| **thoroughfare** | a way you can travel through |
| **seafaring** | sea-travelling |
| the **fare** you pay | what the travelling costs |

So *auf Wiedersehen* and *farewell* are built the same way: both are a wish
handed over at the door, one about seeing again, one about travelling well.
