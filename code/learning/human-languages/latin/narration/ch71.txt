Latin, chapter 71: Husband, Wife, Children.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
marītus, marītī — a husband.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a grandmother, then the Latin for a family.

You'll want to know: marītus, marītī.
marītus, marītī — "a husband". English marital.

The word, taken apart.
marītus is probably inherited, from a Proto-Indo-European word for a young man of an age to marry, meryo-, "young man" (EtymDB 2.1). English marry comes from its family through French.

uxor, uxōris — "a wife".

The word, taken apart.
uxor, "wife", has no sure origin; Lewis & Short call it doubtful, and no later proposal has won agreement. English uxorious, "doting on one's wife", comes from it.

Lesson 3 of 5.
līberī, līberōrum — children.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a husband, then the Latin for a wife.

You'll want to know: līberī, līberōrum.
līberī, līberōrum — "children". Only in the plural.

The word, taken apart.
līberī, "children", is the plural of līber, "free" (EtymDB 2.1): the free-born members of a household, as against its slaves. līber is usually traced to Proto-Indo-European h1lewdheros, "free" (EtymDB 2.1).

Lesson 4 of 5.
īnfāns, īnfantis — a baby, an infant.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a wife, then the Latin for children.

You'll want to know: īnfāns, īnfantis.
īnfāns, īnfantis — "a baby, an infant". Literally "not speaking".

The word, taken apart.
īnfāns is built from in-, "not", and for, "I speak" (Lewis & Short): a baby is one who cannot yet speak. Its second part is fāns, "speaking". English infant comes from it.

Lesson 5 of 5.
patruus, patruī — an uncle (father's brother).

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for children, then the Latin for a baby.

You'll want to know: patruus, patruī.
patruus, patruī — "an uncle (father's brother)". Built on pater.

The word, taken apart.
patruus is built on pater, "father" (Lewis & Short): the uncle on the father's side. pater is usually traced to Proto-Indo-European ph2ter, "father" (EtymDB 2.1).
