Spanish, chapter 645: Una avería.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
el pinchazo — the puncture, the flat tyre.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Spanish for the belt, then the Spanish for to fasten.

You'll want to know: el pinchazo.
el pinchazo — "the puncture, the flat tyre".
Tuvimos un pinchazo cerca del pueblo. We had a puncture near the village.
In Mexico people say la ponchadura.

The word, taken apart.
pinchazo is built on pinchar "to prick". The origin of pinchar is not settled; it is often linked to Latin punctum "a prick".

Lesson 2 of 5.
averiarse — to break down.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Spanish for to fasten, then the Spanish for the puncture.

You'll want to know: averiarse.
averiarse — "to break down".
El autobús se averió en la autopista. The bus broke down on the motorway.
Machines se averían; a person se pone enfermo.

The word, taken apart.
averiarse is built on avería "breakdown", a word that came through Catalan or Italian and is usually traced to Arabic ʿawār "a defect".

Lesson 3 of 5.
el mecánico — the mechanic.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Spanish for the puncture, then the Spanish for to break down.

You'll want to know: el mecánico.
el mecánico — "the mechanic".
El mecánico viene en media hora. The mechanic is coming in half an hour.
As an adjective it means mechanical. A woman is la mecánica.

The word, taken apart.
mecánico is a learned borrowing from Latin mēchanicus "mechanical" (Lewis & Short), which comes from Greek mēkhanikós, built on mēkhanḗ "machine" (EtymDB).

Lesson 4 of 5.
el neumático — the tyre.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Spanish for to break down, then the Spanish for the mechanic.

You'll want to know: el neumático.
el neumático — "the tyre".
Hay que cambiar el neumático. The tyre has to be changed.
Spain says el neumático or la rueda; much of Latin America says la llanta.

The word, taken apart.
neumático is a learned borrowing from Latin pneumaticus "of air or wind" (Lewis & Short), from Greek pneumatikós, built on pneûma "air, breath" (EtymDB). A tyre is full of air.

Lesson 5 of 5.
pinchar — to get a puncture; to prick; to click.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Spanish for the mechanic, then the Spanish for the tyre.

You'll want to know: pinchar.
pinchar — "to get a puncture; to prick; to click".
Pinchamos una rueda en el camino. We got a puncture on the way.
In Spain pinchar is also to click on a screen. Mexico says ponchar for a puncture.

The word, taken apart.
The origin of pinchar is not settled. It is often linked to Latin punctum "a prick, a point", but the path is unclear.
