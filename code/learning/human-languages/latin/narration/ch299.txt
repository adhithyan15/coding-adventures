Latin, chapter 299: Nārrātiō.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
nārrātiō, nārrātiōnis — a narrative, a telling.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to come along, then the Latin for next.

You'll want to know: nārrātiō, nārrātiōnis.
nārrātiō, nārrātiōnis — "a narrative, a telling".
Nārrātiō senis longa sed iūcunda erat. The old man's story was long but pleasant.
Feminine, 3rd declension, from nārrō (taught). In a Roman speech, the nārrātiō set out the facts. English narration.

The word, taken apart.
nārrātiō is built on nārrō, "to tell, to relate" (EtymDB), which Lewis & Short link with gnārus, "knowing": making known. English narration comes from it.

Lesson 2 of 5.
commemorō, commemorāre — to recall, to mention.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for next, then the Latin for a narrative.

You'll want to know: commemorō, commemorāre.
commemorō, commemorāre — "to recall, to mention".
Senex victōriās veterēs commemorābat. The old man was recalling old victories.
commemorō, commemorāre, commemorāvī, commemorātum: to bring something fully to mind, and so to mention it. English commemorate.

The word, taken apart.
commemorō is built on memorō, "to bring to mind, to tell of", with con- (EtymDB); memorō comes from memor, "mindful". English commemorate comes from it.

Lesson 3 of 5.
memorō, memorāre — to tell of, to relate.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a narrative, then the Latin for to recall.

You'll want to know: memorō, memorāre.
memorō, memorāre — "to tell of, to relate".
Poētae multa dē bellīs memorant. Poets tell many things about wars.
memorō, memorāre, memorāvī, memorātum. Poetic and historical in tone; Vergil opens his poem by asking the Muse to tell him the causes.

The word, taken apart.
memorō is built on memor, "mindful" (EtymDB), which is inherited from a Proto-Indo-European root meaning "to remember". English memory comes from memoria, of the same family.

Lesson 4 of 5.
referō, referre — to report; to carry back.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to recall, then the Latin for to tell of.

You'll want to know: referō, referre.
referō, referre — "to report; to carry back".
Nūntius omnia quae vīderat ducī rettulit. The messenger reported to the leader all that he had seen.
referō, referre, rettulī, relātum, irregular like ferō. Ad senātum referre: to put a matter before the senate.

The word, taken apart.
referō is built on ferō, "to carry", with re-, "back" (EtymDB): to carry back, and so to bring back word. English refer comes from it, and relate from its participle.

Lesson 5 of 5.
inquam — I say (quoting words).

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to tell of, then the Latin for to report.

You'll want to know: inquam.
inquam — "I say (quoting words)".
'Venī mēcum,' inquit pater, 'et omnia vidēbis.' 'Come with me,' said father, 'and you will see everything.'
Defective: mostly inquam, inquis, inquit, set inside the words quoted. Inquit means 'he says' or 'he said'.

The word, taken apart.
inquam is an inherited verb that EtymDB traces to a Proto-Indo-European form enskʷe-, "to say". Only a few forms are in use (Lewis & Short).
