German, chapter 16: To Be, and the Past That Takes It.
3 lessons. The first lesson already needs your eyes or your hands, so save this one for when you have stopped.


Lesson 1 of 3.
sein — "to be".

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, because the lesson points at something written down. You can listen to all of it now and come back to the parts that need looking at once you have stopped.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Chapter 14 gave you haben. Here is its partner — and it is the most irregular verb in German, because it is not really one verb at all. It is three.

The forms.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
ich bin, wir sind.
du bist, ihr seid.
er/sie/es ist, sie/Sie sind.
Past: ich war, du warst, er war, wir waren.
Look at that with fresh eyes. Bin, ist, war — those do not look like forms of the same word, and they aren't.

Three roots, one verb.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
forms: ist, sind, seid, sein. from Proto-Indo-European: h₁es- — "to be". which also gave: Latin est, sunt; French est, sont.
forms: bin, bist. from Proto-Indo-European: bʰuH- — "to grow, become". which also gave: English be; Latin fuī.
forms: war, waren. from Proto-Indo-European: wes- — "to dwell, remain". which also gave: English was, were.
Three separate ancient verbs, each contributing a slice of the paradigm. When one verb's forms are filled in from another verb, that's suppletion — the same thing English does in go / went.
And notice bin/bist has a cousin abroad. Latin fuī is the source of Spanish fui, "I was / I went" — one of that language's pretéritos fuertes. German bin and Spanish fui descend from the same PIE root bʰuH- — one landed in the present, the other in the past.

Why "be" verbs are the messiest words in every language.
This is not bad luck. It is a rule:
The most-used words are the most irregular.
Regularity spreads by analogy — you meet an unfamiliar verb, you guess its forms from the pattern. But you never have to guess at "to be." You hear it a hundred times a day, so the odd old shapes are reinforced faster than analogy can sand them down. Rare words get regularized; common words are protected fossils.

Lesson 2 of 3.
Ich bin gegangen — the perfect with sein.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Chapter 15 built ich habe gelernt with haben. A smaller family uses sein.
[a table of 4 rows, read aloud]
ich bin gegangen, I went / have gone.
du bist gegangen, you went.
er ist gegangen, he went.
wir sind gegangen, we went.

The family.
The centre is a change of place or state with a clear result:
[a table of 4 rows, read aloud]
verb: gehen. perfect: bin gegangen. meaning: went.
verb: kommen. perfect: bin gekommen. meaning: came.
verb: fahren. perfect: bin gefahren. meaning: travelled.
verb: werden. perfect: bin geworden. meaning: became.
Ordinary activity without that result usually keeps haben: ich habe gearbeitet, ich habe getanzt.
A small learned group also takes sein: sein itself (bin gewesen), bleiben (bin geblieben), plus verbs such as gelingen (succeed), geschehen / passieren (happen), and begegnen (meet).
Build each perfect as:
present form of sein + past participle.
The participle stays at the end, just as it did with haben.

[your turn — say: “ich habe gelernt; ich bin gegangen”]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: “bin gekommen; bin geworden; bin geblieben”]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Which broad family takes sein? (Resultative motion/change.) Say “I went.” (Ich bin gegangen.) Name two learned exceptions. (Sein, bleiben, or geschehen.) Where does the participle go? (At the end.) Next: compare German agreement with French.


Lesson 3 of 3.
Gegangen — no agreement in the perfect.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
German and French both split their compound past between “have” and “be,” but their participles behave differently.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
subject: he. French: il est allé. German: er ist gegangen.
subject: she. French: elle est allée. German: sie ist gegangen.
subject: they. French: ils sont allés. German: sie sind gegangen.
In the German perfect, gegangen never changes for person, number, or gender. French preserves adjective-like agreement; German lost it in this position.
German participles still inflect when they stand before a noun as adjectives:
der angekommene Zug — the arrived train;
die eingeschlafene Katze — the fallen-asleep cat.
So the participle did not lose every adjective ending everywhere. It is specifically the perfect construction where agreement disappeared.
Old High German varied in this position. Modern German simplified it to one unchanging form.
The haben/sein split is a native Germanic development, not a borrowing from Latin. It grew alongside neighbouring Romance systems through centuries of contact. The auxiliary split is parallel; the agreement outcome differs.

[your turn — say: “er ist gegangen; sie ist gegangen”]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: “wir sind gegangen — same participle”]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: “der angekommene Zug — adjective ending returns”]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Does the participle agree inside the German perfect? (No.) Where can it still take endings? (Before a noun as an adjective.) Does French agree? (Yes.) Did German inherit its split from Latin? (No; it developed in parallel.)
