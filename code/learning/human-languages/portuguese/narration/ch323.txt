Portuguese, chapter 323: A consoada.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
a consoada — the Christmas Eve dinner.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Portuguese for to decorate, then the Portuguese for the decoration.

You'll want to know: a consoada.
a consoada — "the Christmas Eve dinner".
Na consoada comemos bacalhau com batatas. At Christmas Eve dinner we eat salt cod with potatoes.
A Portuguese tradition: the family supper on the night of 24 December.

The word, taken apart.
consoada is built on consoar "to have a light supper", which is usually traced to Latin cōnsōlārī "to comfort" (Lewis & Short); the path is not fully settled.

Montar o presépio — to set up the crib.

Lesson 3 of 5.
a passagem de ano — the New Year celebration, New Year’s Eve.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Portuguese for the Christmas Eve dinner, then the Portuguese for the nativity scene.

You'll want to know: a passagem de ano.
a passagem de ano — "the New Year celebration, New Year’s Eve".
Na passagem de ano, vimos o fogo de artifício. On New Year’s Eve we watched the fireworks.
Feliz Ano Novo! — Happy New Year! In Brazil: o réveillon.

The word, taken apart.
passagem came from French passage (EtymDB), and ano is inherited from Latin annus "a year": the passing from one year to the next.

o folar — "the Easter bread".
A madrinha oferece o folar ao afilhado. The godmother gives the Easter bread to her godson.
A sweet or savoury loaf, sometimes with whole eggs baked in.

The word, taken apart.
The origin of folar is not settled; EtymDB has the word with no source.

Lesson 5 of 5.
as amêndoas — the sugared almonds; almonds.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Portuguese for the New Year celebration, then the Portuguese for the Easter bread.

You'll want to know: as amêndoas.
as amêndoas — "the sugared almonds; almonds".
As crianças receberam amêndoas da madrinha. The children got sugared almonds from their godmother.
At Easter, as amêndoas are sugared or chocolate almonds.

The word, taken apart.
amêndoa goes back to Late Latin amyndala, from Latin amygdala, a borrowing from Greek amygdálē "an almond" (Lewis & Short).
