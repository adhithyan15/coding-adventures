Italian, chapter 263: The Scene --- Ero, Era, C'era.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
parlavo — the past that is still running.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say "I spoke" the way chapter fifteen built it. (Ho parlato.)
[pause 1 second]
That past has an edge: it happened, and it finished. Italian has a second past with no edge at all, and every story you tell from here on needs both of them.

Grammar Lens: one ending, three families.
Take the infinitive, drop its last two letters, -re, and add -vo for "I":
parla-re, parlavo — I was speaking, I used to speak ave-re, avevo — I had, I used to have dormi-re, dormivo — I was sleeping.
The vowel in front of the -v- is the family's own: -a-, -e-, -i-. Nothing else moves. The other persons keep that -v- and change only what comes after it: parlavo, parlavi, parlava, parlavamo, parlavate, parlavano — I, you, he or she, we, you all, they.
This is the imperfetto, and it is the most regular tense Italian owns: one recipe for almost every verb. The few that refuse it begin in the next lesson.
What it means is "was ‑ing", or "used to". Parlavo is speaking with no start and no finish in view, which is exactly what a story needs for its background.

The word, taken apart: why a v.
Latin already had this past, and it marked it with a b: Latin amābam, "I was loving", and habēbat, "he was having". Between two vowels that b softened to a v, the same softening that turned Latin habēre into avere. So habēbat comes down to aveva sound by sound.
One piece is younger. Old Italian said io parlava, with the same -a as lui parlava. The -o of parlavo was borrowed later from the present, parlo, so that "I" had an ending of its own. Neither EtymDB nor Lewis & Short records endings this late; that step is the usual explanation in histories of Italian, and it is given here as one.

Lesson 2 of 5.
era, c'era — the two words a scene is made of.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Build "I was speaking" from parlare. (Parlavo.) Now say "there is". (C'è.)
[pause 1 second]
Hold on to both. This lesson joins them.

You'll want to know: era.
One verb refuses last lesson's recipe, and it is the one you use most: essere. Its imperfetto is short, and it is old:
ero — I was, eri — you were, era — he, she or it was eravamo — we were, eravate — you all were, erano — they were.
Era is the form you will hear most, because a scene is mostly about things and places rather than about you:
La stazione era piccola. — The station was small. Ero stanco, ma ero contento. — I was tired, but I was happy.

You'll want to know: c'era.
You have c'è and ci sono, "there is" and "there are". Move them into the imperfetto and you have the way a scene opens:
c'era — there was, c'erano — there were C'era il sole. — The sun was out. (Literally, "there was the sun".) C'erano molte persone. — There were a lot of people.
Same ci, same apostrophe where it meets a vowel. Only the verb has moved into the past.

The word, taken apart.
era from Latin erat, "he was, she was" — inherited, with only the final t worn away. Latin's past of "to be" was irregular already, eram, erās, erat, and it never had the b of amābam, so it never took the -v- either. That is why this one form looks nothing like parlava. (Lewis & Short, under sum.)
The ero for "I" got its -o late, on the same usual account as parlavo: old Italian said io era. EtymDB's only row for an Italian era is the noun "an era, an age", a different word altogether, from Latin aera.

Lesson 3 of 5.
faceva freddo — the weather, made in the past.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say "the sun was out" the way last lesson built it. (C'era il sole.) Then "I was tired". (Ero stanco, or ero stanca.)

You'll want to know: faceva freddo.
English says the weather was cold. Italian says it made cold:
Faceva freddo. — It was cold. Faceva caldo. — It was hot.
Faceva is the imperfetto of a verb meaning "to make, to do", and for heat and cold that is the verb Italian reaches for. It needs no subject: nobody is doing the making.
Keep era for things. Il caffè era freddo — the coffee was cold — is about the coffee. Faceva freddo is about the whole day.
And rain takes this chapter's plain recipe: piovere gives pioveva, "it was raining". Put three of them together and you have a whole morning before anything has happened in it:
Faceva freddo e pioveva. Non c'era il sole.

The phrase, taken apart.
faceva from Latin faciēbat, "he was making", the imperfect of facere, "to make, to do" — inherited, with the -ēba- worn to -eva, the same b to v as habēbat and aveva. That facere is the root of English fact, factory and effect. (Lewis & Short, under facio.)

Lesson 4 of 5.
dormivo / ho dormito — the same sleep, told two ways.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Paint a cold morning in two short sentences. (Faceva freddo. Non c'era il sole.)
[pause 1 second]
Nothing has happened in it yet. This lesson is about what happens.

Grammar Lens: the scene and the events.
Take one fact, that you slept, and let the tense decide what kind of fact it is:
Dormivo. — I was sleeping. (no edge: what was going on) Ho dormito. — I slept. (an edge: a thing that happened and ended).
Neither is more correct. They answer different questions, and a told story needs both, in a division of labour:
The imperfetto paints the scene: what was going on, what things were like. The passato prossimo drops the events into it: what happened.
Faceva freddo. Dormivo. Mia madre ha telefonato. — It was cold. I was asleep. My mother phoned.
Mentre, "while", puts both into one sentence. Mentre dormivo, mia madre ha telefonato. The mentre half is the scene, still running; the other half is the event that cut into it.
English has no separate ending for this. It leans on was ‑ing and used to and lets the context carry the rest. Italian settles it in the ending.

The word, taken apart: why "imperfect".
imperfetto is a learned word from Latin imperfectus, "unfinished" (EtymDB 2.1): in-, "not", plus perfectus, "carried all the way through", which is itself per-, "through", plus facere, "to do, to make" (Lewis & Short). That facere is the one inside last lesson's faceva.
So the name is a description of the job. The imperfetto is the not-carried-through past: unfinished in time, with no edge on it. English perfect still means finished all the way through.

Lesson 5 of 5.
Review — the scene, and what cut into it.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Build "I was reading" from leggere. (Leggevo.) Then say "I was tired". (Ero stanco, or ero stanca.)

What you've built.
Four lessons, and every piece of a scene is in your hands. The recipe, -vo, on any regular verb: parlavo, leggevo, dormivo. The one verb that keeps its Latin shape: ero, era, c'era. The weather, made rather than being: faceva freddo. And the division of labour: the imperfetto paints, the passato prossimo drops the events in.
[pause 2 seconds]
The joint between the two is mentre. Whatever follows it is the scene, still running; the rest of the sentence is the event.
Mentre leggevo, mia madre ha telefonato. Mentre aspettavo l'autobus, ha cominciato a piovere.
The second one is worth hearing twice. The rain is an event here, because it began: ha cominciato. Once it is falling, it is scene again: pioveva.
