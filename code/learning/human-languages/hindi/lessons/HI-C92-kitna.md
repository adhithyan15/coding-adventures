# कितना — a word let out of one sentence

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[HI-CONCEPT-C19-UMR-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say the question you learned for asking somebody's age. One word in
it means something on its own, and you have never used it anywhere else.

## You'll want to know: कितना
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[HI-LEX-C92-DEG-04]; assesses=[] -->

**कितना** (*kitnā*) — how much, how many.

**कितनी किताबें?** — How many books?

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[HI-LEX-C92-DEG-04, HI-LEX-C91-NOUN-02] -->

You met it inside the age question, where it was one syllable of a memorised
lump. It is a word, and it behaves like every **-आ** word in this language:

| what is being counted | the form |
|---|---|
| a masculine thing | कितना |
| a feminine thing, or a plural | कितनी |

**कितनी किताबें** is feminine and plural, so the question word bends to match
its noun — which is the same agreement you have now seen on adjectives, verbs,
the past copula, the continuous and the hedge.

**Hindi does not split *how much* from *how many*.** English needs two phrases
because it sorts nouns into countable and uncountable; Hindi asks one question
and lets the noun answer.

## Grammar Lens: the shape of a quantity question
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[HI-LEX-C92-DEG-04, HI-LEX-C92-DEG-01] -->

| question | answer |
|---|---|
| कितनी किताबें? | दो किताबें। |
| कितनी हिंदी? | थोड़ी। |

The second pair is the one a listening paper sets: a quantity question, and a
hedge for an answer. Both words in that exchange bend to the same feminine
noun, which is why they rhyme.
