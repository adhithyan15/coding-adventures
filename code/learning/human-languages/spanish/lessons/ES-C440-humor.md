# el humor — the fluid you are in

## You'll want to know: el humor
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-C440-FIESTA-05]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C438-CUERPO-05] -->

**el humor** — mood, and also humour.

*Estoy de buen humor* — I am in a good mood. *Está de mal humor* — he is in a
bad mood. *Tiene mucho sentido del humor* — she has a great sense of humour.

The **h** is silent here as everywhere in Spanish: *el u-MOR*. And note the preposition —
Spanish says you are **de** a mood, of it, not *in* it.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C440-FIESTA-05] -->

Latin **ūmor** meant one thing only: **moisture, a fluid**. English keeps that
original sense in **humid**.

Medieval medicine held that a body ran on four fluids, and that whichever one
you had most of set your temperament. Your *humour* was literally which liquid
you were full of. The theory is long dead, and the word survived it — so when a
Spanish speaker says *estar de buen humor*, the sentence still says *to be of a
good fluid*, and nobody notices.

## Why it's said this way
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C440-FIESTA-05] -->

One Spanish word covers what English splits into **mood** and **humour**, and
the split happened in English rather than the join happening in Spanish.

| Spanish | English needs |
|---|---|
| estar de buen humor | to be in a good **mood** |
| sentido del humor | sense of **humour** |

English narrowed *humour* onto the funny sense and borrowed *mood* from its own
older stock to carry the rest. Spanish never divided the work, so *el humor*
still holds both — the state you are in, and the thing that makes other people
laugh.

That is worth noticing whenever an English pair maps to one Spanish word. The
usual cause is not that Spanish is vaguer; it is that English acquired a second
word from somewhere and gave it half the job.
