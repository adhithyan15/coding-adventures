# a destra, a sinistra — following the answer

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[IT-LEX-SCUSI-01, IT-GRAMMAR-PER-ANDARE-A-02] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Stop a stranger and ask the way to the station. (*Scusi, per andare
alla stazione?*)

[PAUSE 1s] Asking was the easy half. Now the answer comes back, and it comes
fast.

## You'll want to know: a destra, a sinistra
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[IT-LEX-DESTRA-SINISTRA-01]; assesses=[IT-LEX-C91-LUOGHI91-01] -->

**a destra** — "on the right, to the right". **a sinistra** — "on the left,
to the left". With **dritto**, "straight on", which you already have, that is
every direction a street can send you.

> *La stazione è a destra.* — The station is on the right.
> *La prima a sinistra.* — The first on the left.

That second one is a whole answer in four words: *la prima*, "the first", is
the ordinal you learned in chapter twenty-six, agreeing with the street it
leaves unsaid.

## Grammar Lens: vada dritto, giri a destra
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[IT-GRAMMAR-GIRI-A-DESTRA-02]; assesses=[IT-LEX-C209-VERBI209-03, IT-LEX-C89-LUOGHI89-05, IT-LEX-C89-LUOGHI89-04, IT-GRAMMAR-POI-01] -->

A stranger answering a *Lei* question answers in the *Lei* form, and the
directions are instructions:

> **Vada** dritto, **poi giri** a destra al semaforo.
> — Go straight on, then turn right at the traffic lights.

**giri** is *girare*, "to turn", with the same *-i* as **scusi**: the polite
instruction of an *-are* verb. **vada** is *andare*'s irregular one, "go".
You need to **understand** these two, not build them; the rest of the answer
is words you know: *dritto*, *poi*, *al semaforo*, *all'incrocio*.

A long answer comes in the order you will walk it, and *poi* marks each new
piece.

## The phrase, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[] -->

**destra** goes back to Latin *dextera*, "the right hand", from *dexter*,
"right" (Lewis & Short). English **dexterous** is the same word: skilled, as
the right hand is. **sinistra** is Latin *sinistra*, "the left hand"; Lewis &
Short call the origin of *sinister* doubtful. Roman augurs counted the left
as the lucky side, but Latin poets already used *sinister* for "unlucky,
adverse" (Lewis & Short), and that is the sense English **sinister** kept. EtymDB 2.1 lists
both Italian words but gives neither a direct Latin parent.
