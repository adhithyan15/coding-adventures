The word, taken apart.
ਪਰ (par) joins two clauses the way ਅਤੇ does, and warns that the second will pull against the first:
(first sentence) ਪਰ (par) (second sentence) — "… but …"
It works best with the chapter before it, because the sharpest contrast a beginner can draw is between something that is and something that ਨਹੀਂ.
ਪਰ (par) is the inherited word — it came down with the language rather than being borrowed into it. That matters more than it usually would, because the next lesson is a second word for but that arrived from somewhere else entirely, and this book already teaches one such pair without saying so.

The word, taken apart.
ਲੇਕਿਨ — lekin — but.
The same job, the same position, and a completely different history. ਲੇਕਿਨ (lekin) is Arabic lākin, carried into Punjabi through Persian — the road that also brought this book several of its greetings and farewells.
So Punjabi has two words for but: one that grew here and one that arrived. You can use either. They are not in competition and neither is more correct.
What they are is a pair, and this book has been quietly teaching you another one since its first chapter without ever pointing at it. The next lesson but one is about that.

Script — two signs, and a caution worth more than the signs.
ਪ — pa, ਰ — ra.
Both old, and the word is two letters long. The caution is the real lesson.
ਪਰ (par) are also the first two letters of ਪਰਿਵਾਰ, family, which this book taught many chapters ago. So when you meet these two signs on a page, check that they stand alone — inside a longer word they are not this word at all.
That is not a hypothetical. It is the reason a search of this corpus for but came back with hits and no actual occurrences.

You have owned that first pair since page one and have never been told which to choose.

The pattern, taken apart.
Both pairs are the same shape, and it runs through the whole language:
ਧੰਨਵਾਦ, ਪਰ (par) — the inherited and Sanskritic side.
ਸ਼ੁਕਰੀਆ, ਲੇਕਿਨ (lekin) — the Perso-Arabic side.
Punjabi has drawn on both streams for centuries, so a great many everyday ideas have two perfectly good words. What the choice carries:
The Sanskritic word leans formal and written, and is the one you will most often see in print and hear in institutional or Sikh religious settings.
The Perso-Arabic word leans everyday and spoken, and is more usual in Pakistani Punjabi and in Muslim communities.
Both are understood everywhere by everyone. Choosing the other one is never an error, and it is never taken as one.
So this is not a rule about correctness. It is a rule about what your choice tells the listener — which is the same thing ਤੂੰ against ਤੁਸੀਂ told them, and the same thing the book's three greetings told them.
