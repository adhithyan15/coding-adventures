German, chapter 13: Colours.
2 lessons. All 2 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 2.
schwarz, weiß — and the word German lent away.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
German's colour words are home-grown — no Latin here. But one of them has a sibling that emigrated: a German word for "shining" became the word for white in French, Italian and Portuguese.

schwarz.
schwarz ("black") from Proto-Germanic swartaz. Its English cousin survives in swarthy ("dark-complexioned"). Further back it's related to Latin sordēs ("dirt") — the root of English sordid. Black and grubby, from one idea.

weiß.
weiß ("white") from Proto-Germanic hwītaz = exactly English white. Same word, different spelling clothes.
Note the ß (Eszett): it marks a sharp s after a long vowel. Weiß rhymes with English vice, not vice-versa's second half. In Switzerland you'll see it written weiss — the letter is optional there.

The word that emigrated.
German also has blank — "shiny, polished, bare." That word, in its Frankish form blank ("shining"), was borrowed into Romance, where it pushed Latin's own albus out and became:
[a table of 4 rows, read aloud]
language: French. "white": blanc.
language: Italian. "white": bianco.
language: Portuguese. "white": branco.
language: German. "white": blank — still just "shiny".
This is the reverse of the usual flow. Latin normally lends to Germanic (Fenster from fenestra, Wein from vīnum, from Chapter 11). Here Germanic lent to Latin's daughters — and German kept the original meaning while they turned it into a colour.

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Are German's colour words borrowed from Latin? (No — both are native Germanic.) What English word is schwarz's cousin? (Swarthy — and, further off, sordid.) What is weiß in English, letter for letter? (White.) Which German word became French blanc? (Blank, "shining" — Germanic lending into Romance for once.) Next: rot and blau, and one more word that travelled.


Lesson 2 of 2.
rot, blau — cousins and emigrants.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Two more native German colours. One is related to the French word for red — genuinely, not by borrowing. The other is a second German word the Romance languages took.

rot — an ancient cousin.
rot from Proto-Germanic raudaz from PIE h₁rewdʰ-. That root is one of the oldest colour words we can reconstruct, and it went everywhere:
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
language: German. word: rot.
language: English. word: red, rust, ruddy.
language: Latin. word: ruber, rubeus becomes French rouge, ruby, rubric.
So rot and rouge are cousins, not borrowings — they split apart thousands of years before either language existed. Red is the colour humans name first, after black and white.

blau — the second emigrant.
blau from Proto-Germanic blēwaz. Like blank in the last lesson, this word was borrowed into Romance:
[a table of 4 rows, read aloud]
language: German. "blue": blau.
language: French. "blue": bleu.
language: Italian. "blue": blu.
language: English. "blue": blue — taken from French, not from German.
English is the odd one here: it had Germanic blāw available but borrowed the French form instead. The word went Germanic becomes French becomes English, coming home in disguise.
Portuguese went a different way entirely — its blue is azul, from Arabic — a second, entirely separate road to blue, and the one Spanish took too.

The chapter in one table.
[a table of 4 rows, read aloud]
German: schwarz. source: Germanic swartaz. did Romance borrow it?: no.
German: weiß. source: Germanic hwītaz. did Romance borrow it?: no — but blank went instead.
German: rot. source: Germanic raudaz. did Romance borrow it?: no — Latin had its own cousin.
German: blau. source: Germanic blēwaz. did Romance borrow it?: yes — bleu, blu.
