Lesson 1 of 6.
capotar — to overturn, to flip over.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Portuguese for to collide, then the Portuguese for the mishap.

You'll want to know: capotar.
capotar — "to overturn, to flip over".
O carro capotou duas vezes. The car flipped over twice.
Of cars and other vehicles.

The word, taken apart.
capotar is usually traced to French capoter "to overturn"; EtymDB has no entry, and the origin is not settled.

Lesson 2 of 6.
a travagem — the braking.

Before the new one: say the Portuguese for the mishap, then the Portuguese for to overturn.

You'll want to know: a travagem.
a travagem — "the braking".
Ouvimos uma travagem e depois um barulho enorme. We heard someone brake hard and then a huge bang.
European Portuguese; in Brazil, a freada.

The word, taken apart.
travagem is built on travar "to brake, to hold back", from trave "a beam", which comes from Latin trabs "a beam" (Lewis & Short).

danificar — "to damage".
A chuva danificou o telhado. The rain damaged the roof.
Formal; in speech, estragar.

The word, taken apart.
danificar is a learned word from Late Latin damnificāre "to injure", from damnum "loss" and facere "to make" (Lewis & Short).

Lesson 5 of 6.
o estrago — the harm, the mess; the ruin.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Portuguese for the damage, then the Portuguese for to damage.

You'll want to know: o estrago.
o estrago — "the harm, the mess; the ruin".
O estrago na cozinha foi enorme. The mess in the kitchen was huge.
Built on estragar, ‘to spoil’. Fazer estragos — to do damage.

The word, taken apart.
estrago is built on estragar "to spoil", which is usually traced to Vulgar Latin stragāre, from Latin strāgēs "destruction" (Lewis & Short).
