# el accidente — what falls on you

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C356-CLOCK-24, ES-LEX-C448-MUDA-05] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say **la ocasión** and **roto**. A chance and a breakage are about
to meet in one Latin verb.

## You'll want to know: el accidente
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-C454-CORTE-03]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C454-CORTE-02] -->

**el accidente** — the accident.

*Ha habido un accidente en la vía* — there has been an accident on the track.
*Un accidente de tráfico* — a road accident. *Un accidente laboral* — an
accident at work, the phrase on every insurance form.

Spanish says *tener un accidente*, and the adverb is **por accidente** — by
accident, meaning unintentionally.

## Grammar Lens: -cc- is two sounds, not one
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C454-CORTE-03, ES-LEX-C454-CORTE-02] -->

**accidente** has a double **c**, and unlike English the two letters do
different jobs:

| letters | sound |
|---|---|
| first **c** (before *c*) | **k** |
| second **c** (before *i*) | **th** in Spain, **s** elsewhere |

So the word is **ak-thi-DEN-te**, or **ak-si-DEN-te**. The rule underneath is
one you already use: a Spanish **c** is hard before *a*, *o*, *u* and another
consonant, and soft before *e* and *i*.

The same double turns up in *acción*, *dirección* and *lección*. Once you hear
the **k** in front of the soft one, these stop being awkward.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C454-CORTE-03, ES-LEX-C356-CLOCK-24] -->

Latin **accidere**: **ad-**, towards, plus **cadere**, **to fall**. A thing
that falls upon you.

And **la ocasión** is the same *cadere*, with a different prefix — something
that falls **to** you. Put the pair side by side and Spanish has a small map
of luck:

| word | what falls, and how |
|---|---|
| **la ocasión** | a chance falls your way |
| **el accidente** | a misfortune falls upon you |

Neither was chosen by anybody. That is the whole idea the root carries, and it
is why *accidental* in English still means unplanned rather than harmful.

The same *cadere* is in **caer**, to fall, and in English **cadence**,
**decay** and **cascade**.
