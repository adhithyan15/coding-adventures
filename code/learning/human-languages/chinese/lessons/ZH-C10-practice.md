# Practice — thanks, and the answer

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ZH-LEX-BUXIE] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say **bú xiè**. Rising, then falling — and you know why the first one
rises.

## The exchange
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ZH-DIALOGUE-THANK]; assesses=[ZH-LEX-XIEXIE, ZH-LEX-BUXIE, ZH-TONE-SANDHI-BU] -->

> **A:** 早上好
>
> *zǎoshang hǎo* — good morning.
>
> **B:** 早上好
>
> *zǎoshang hǎo* — good morning.
>
> **A:** 谢谢
>
> *xièxie* — thank you.
>
> **B:** 不谢
>
> *bú xiè* — you're welcome.
>
> **A:** 再见
>
> *zàijiàn* — goodbye.

Six lines, and **one new character** paid for two of them.

## What you've built — one character, a whole exchange
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ZH-DIALOGUE-THANK, ZH-TONE-SANDHI-BU] -->

This chapter cost you 谢 and nothing else. Out of it came the thanks (**谢谢**,
the same character twice) and the reply (**不谢**, a negator you already had).
That is what a system does for you that a phrasebook cannot: pieces recombine.

Three things you did here without being led:

- you **doubled** a character to make a word, and knew the second half would go
  light;
- you **negated** a word to make its answer, the same move that turned 是 into
  不是;
- and you **predicted a tone change** from a rule, before hearing the word said.

That last one is the real marker. Early on you were told what 你好 sounds like.
Now you are told a rule and you work out what a word sounds like. Those are
different kinds of knowing, and only the second one travels to words nobody has
taught you.
