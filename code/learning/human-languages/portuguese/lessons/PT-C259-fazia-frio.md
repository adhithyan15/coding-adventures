# fazia frio — the weather, made in the past

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[PT-LEX-HAVIA-02, PT-LEX-TINHA-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say "there were a lot of people" the way last lesson built it.
(*Havia muitas pessoas.*) Then "I was twenty". (*Tinha vinte anos.*)

## You'll want to know: fazia frio, fazia calor
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[PT-LEX-FAZIA-FRIO-01]; assesses=[PT-LEX-C65-ADJ2-05, PT-LEX-C59-TEMPO-01] -->

English says the weather **was** cold. Portuguese says it **made** cold:

> **Fazia frio.** — It was cold.
> **Fazia calor.** — It was hot.

*Fazia* is the imperfeito of **fazer**, "to make, to do", on the plain *-er*
recipe: *faz-er*, *fazia*. **frio** is "cold" and **calor** is "heat", so the
second one says, word for word, "it was making heat". Neither needs a subject:
nobody is doing the making.

Rain has a verb of its own. You have **a chuva**, "the rain", and its verb
**chover** gives **chovia**, "it was raining", on the same recipe.

Keep **estava** for things, with the adjectives you already own. *O café
estava frio* — the coffee was cold — is about the coffee; *estava quente*, it
was hot, about a cup or a room. *Fazia frio* is about the whole day. Put three
of these together and you have a morning before anything has happened in it:

> *Fazia frio e chovia. Não havia sol.*

## The phrase, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[] -->

**fazer** is inherited from Latin *facere*, "to make, to do" (EtymDB 2.1), and
**fazia** goes back to its past *faciēbat*, "he was making" (Lewis & Short,
under *facio*). That *facere* is the root of English **fact** and **factory**.

**frio** is inherited from Latin *frīgidus*, "cold" (EtymDB 2.1), worn short in
the mouth, and English **frigid** borrowed the same word whole. **calor** is
Latin *calor*, "heat", kept almost letter for letter (EtymDB 2.1; Lewis &
Short). **chover** is inherited from spoken Latin *plovere* (EtymDB 2.1), a
reshaping of classical *pluere*, "to rain" (Lewis & Short, under *pluo*).
