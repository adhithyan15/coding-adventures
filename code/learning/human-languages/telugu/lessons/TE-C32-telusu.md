# తెలుసు (telusu) — "to know," the verb with no room for a person

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[TE-GRAMMAR-C32-UNDU-02] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Five verbs, and every one ended on *-ānu*, the piece that means "I."
The last verb of the chapter has no such piece — and Chapter 6 already handed you
the sentence it makes.

## You'll want to know: తెలుసు
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[TE-LEX-C32-TELUSU-01]; assesses=[] -->

> **తెలుసు** — *telusu* — **it is known**

Not "to know" in the English sense. It comes from **తెలియు** (*teliyu*), "to
become clear, to be apparent" — a thing *telusu*-es to you the way a shape
resolves out of fog. Its negative is **తెలియదు** (*teliyadu*), "it is not
known."

The verb is Dravidian: DEDR 3433 files *teliyu* with Tamil *teḷi*, "to become
clear". Tamil's everyday match is **தெரி** (*teri*); Kannada and Malayalam use
*gottu* and *aṟiyām*. All four build the sentence the same way.

## Grammar Lens: no person on the verb, so the person moves
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[TE-GRAMMAR-C32-TELUSU-02]; assesses=[] -->

> **నాకు తెలుసు.** — *nāku telusu* — "I know."

**తెలుసు** never changes shape. Only the little dative word in front of it moves:

| the knower | the sentence |
|---|---|
| I | *nāku telusu* |
| you | *nīku telusu* |
| he | *ataniki telusu* |

Five verbs put the person in the last piece, which is why *nēnu* could be
dropped. Here there is no such piece, so the dative word is the one thing you
**cannot** drop. Telugu treats knowing as something that **happens to** you, not
something you do — and Tamil builds it identically, *enakkut teriyum*.

Now the distinction Chapter 6 could not yet draw. **నాకు తెలుగు వచ్చు** uses
*vaccu*, "comes" — Telugu **comes to** you, and that is a **skill**, a language
you can actually use. **నాకు తెలుసు** is a **fact** you hold. Two dative
sentences, two verbs, two kinds of knowing.
