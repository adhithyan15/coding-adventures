Arabic, chapter 24: See You Tomorrow.
2 lessons. You can do the first 1 of them in the car; after that you will want to have stopped.


Lesson 1 of 2.
إلى الغد (ilā l-ghad) — "see you tomorrow," the same ilā again.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
You already have the pattern: ilā + a future point = "see you [then]." Here it is again, with "tomorrow" filling the slot.

You'll want to know — The two pieces, one already familiar.
إلى الغد (ilā l-ghad) = "until tomorrow":
إلى (ilā) — "until," the exact same word from ilā l-liqāʾ ("see you [later]").
الغد (al-ghad) — "tomorrow," root gh-d-w (غ-د-و).
Note ل is a sun letter, so al-ghad would assimilate — but غ (ghayn) is a moon letter, so here the l stays put: al-ghad, not doubled the way al-liqāʾ and as-salāma were.

Why it's said this way — The root: another "morning becomes tomorrow" story.
Gh-d-w's core sense is "to go out at dawn, early morning" — and "tomorrow" grows directly out of that "early morning" sense. This is not a one-off coincidence: this exact shift also happened in Latin, where māne ("morning") became French's demain and Spanish's mañana ("tomorrow"). Two completely unrelated language families — Semitic and Indo-European — independently building "tomorrow" out of "morning."

[your turn — say: "ilā l-ghad" — "until tomorrow"]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: the reused piece — ilā, already known from ilā l-liqāʾ]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: the root echo — gh-d-w, "dawn/morning" becomes "tomorrow," the same shift as Latin māne becomes mañana/demain]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
What word does ilā l-ghad share with ilā l-liqāʾ? (Ilā, "until.") What does the root gh-d-w literally mean, before it comes to mean "tomorrow"? ("To go out at dawn, early morning.") Is this morning-to-tomorrow shift unique to Arabic? (No — Latin's māne ("morning") independently became French's demain and Spanish's mañana, "tomorrow," the same way.)


Lesson 2 of 2.
Every way to leave — both farewells taken apart down to the letters — the throat sound, the loop that wears four coats, a pattern that turns peace into safety, and the root that opened the book closing it.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, for letter shapes on the page. You can listen to everything else now — leave the sections called Script: the throat sound and the shape-shifter and Script: مع السلامة, letter by letter until you have stopped, and I will say so again when we reach it.

Warm-up.
[pause 4 seconds]
Give both Arabic farewells and the literal meaning of each, without reading on. Then spell the longer one, right to left.

Script: the throat sound and the shape-shifter.
ع ʿayn is two pieces: an open-mouth curve on top, like a small c facing left, and a bowl beneath it when the letter stands alone or ends a word. It opens a third dots-family — the bowl and the hook-and-tail were the first two — and its dotted partner is غ ghayn.
ه hāʾ is the opposite kind of letter: one closed loop, no dots, and the most shape-shifting thing in the alphabet. It wears four genuinely different coats depending on where it sits in the word.

Script: مع السلامة, letter by letter.
[once you have stopped driving — write: مع — mīm, its round head joining left into ʿayn]
[once you have stopped driving — write: السلامة — alif, lām, then sīn, lām, alif, mīm, then the tied ة]
Both letters of مع were already yours. And السلامة is not a long new word: it is الـ plus the very first word you ever wrote, plus a tied tāʾ on the end.

Grammar Lens: one pattern, one rule.
Arabic does not only add endings; it pours a root into a shape. The root s–l–m poured into faʿāla turns peace into the state of safety — salām becomes salāma.
Then the sun-letter rule bites: س is a sun letter, so the l of الـ assimilates to it. Written الس, said as-salāma.
مع maʿa is a preposition and it stands alone, unlike الـ and بـ, which fasten onto the next word.

You'll want to know: the other farewell.
إلى اللقاء ilā l-liqāʾ is إلى (until) plus اللقاء (the meeting), from the root l–q–y, to meet. So Arabic does not say goodbye; it says until the meeting — the same move Spanish makes with hasta luego, until later.
Two shapes in it run past the writing set: the إ with its hamza below, and the ق qāf. Read them for now.

The exchange.
[your turn — say: greet, give your name, ask after them, and part — the whole arc, to a man]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: part with safety — maʿa s-salāma]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: part until the meeting — ilā l-liqāʾ]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — recall: the root that both opens the book's greeting and closes its farewell]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
That last one is the joke the language plays on you: s–l–m is the root of the first thing you learned to say and the last. salām, salāma, islām, muslim — peace, safety, wholeness, all one family.

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 4 seconds]
Give the three pieces of السلامة. (الـ + salām + ة.) Give the root inside اللقاء and its sense. (l–q–y, to meet.) Name the letter that wears four different coats. (ه.) And say whether مع attaches to the next word. (No — it stands alone.)
