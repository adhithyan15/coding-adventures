# सकना (*saknā*) — to be able to

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[HI-LEX-COME, HI-CONCEPT-C05-BOLNA-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Two verbs you own: **आना**, to come, and **बोलना**, to speak. You can
say that you speak. You cannot say that you **can** speak, and until you can, a
whole examiner's question — *can you come tomorrow?* — has no answer.

## You'll want to know: सकना
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[HI-LEX-SAKNA-01]; assesses=[HI-CONCEPT-C05-BOLNA-01] -->

> **सकना** — *saknā* — **to be able to; can, may**

> **मैं हिंदी बोल सकता हूँ.** — *maiṁ hindī bol saktā hūṁ.* —
> **I can speak Hindi.**

Hindi has no separate words for *can*, *could*, *may* and *be able to*. All four
run through this one verb, which is why it is worth more than its size.

## Grammar Lens: सकना never stands alone
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[HI-GRAMMAR-SAKNA-STEM-01]; assesses=[HI-LEX-COME, HI-CONCEPT-C07-NAHIN-01, HI-GRAMMAR-VERB-FINAL-01] -->

One move does it: **strip the -ना off the first verb and put सकना behind it.**

- **बोलना** → **बोल सकता हूँ** — I can speak
- **आना** → **आ सकता हूँ** — I can come

The bare stem carries the meaning and **सकना** carries the grammar, so it is
**सकना** that takes the ending and sits last, exactly where the verb belongs.
**सकना** can never appear on its own: there is no Hindi sentence that is just
*maiṁ saktā hūṁ*.

Negate it and you have the most useful excuse in the language:
**मैं कल नहीं आ सकता.** — *I can't come tomorrow.*
