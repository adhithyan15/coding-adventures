# ఉండు (uṇḍu) — "to be," and the negative that is a different verb

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Thirty-one chapters of words. Now the engine room. Telugu glues a
verb together one piece at a time, seams showing, and this first verb carries
the machine.

## You'll want to know: ఉండు
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[TE-LEX-C32-UNDU-01]; assesses=[] -->

> **ఉండు** — *uṇḍu* — **to be, to exist, to stay**

Chapter 5 met it as "to live somewhere." Here it is the verb-machine itself: **ఉండ్-** (the stem) + **-త-** (tense) + **-ాను** ("I") →
**ఉంటాను** (*uṇṭānu*), "I stay, I will be."

One form is already in your mouth: **ఉన్నాను** (*unnānu*), "I am — right now,"
the shape hiding inside *bāgunnānu* and *mīru elā unnāru?*

Telugu is alone in this word. Tamil says **இரு** (*iru*), Kannada **ಇರು**
(*iru*), Malayalam **ഇരിക്കുക** (*irikkuka*) — one verb, three sisters. Telugu went
its own way, as it did with *lēdu*.

## Sounds you'll need: the retroflex ండ
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[] -->

**ఉ** is the standing vowel *u*. **ండు** is the retroflex cluster *ṇḍ* with the
*u* sign — tongue tip curled back, then released. Both shapes are familiar from Chapter 5.

## Grammar Lens: Telugu's "be" cannot be made negative
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[TE-GRAMMAR-C32-UNDU-02]; assesses=[] -->

There is no negative of *uṇḍu*. To say something is **not** there, Telugu drops
the verb and picks up a second one, **లే-** (*lē-*) — the root behind Chapter 1's
*lēdu*. It takes the very same person-endings:

| who | is | is not |
|---|---|---|
| I | *unnānu* | *lēnu* |
| he | *unnāḍu* | *lēḍu* |
| it | *undi* | *lēdu* |

So *lēdu* was never a bare word for "no." It is the **it**-form of a full verb,
and you can now run it through its persons.

This is where Telugu parted from its sisters. Tamil negates *iru* with **இல்லை**
(*illai*), Kannada with **ಇಲ್ಲ** (*illa*), Malayalam with **ഇല്ല** (*illa*) — one
shared root, *il-*. Telugu dropped it and grew *lē-* instead.
