# los vaqueros — trousers named after the man who wore them

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C360-REF-19, ES-LEX-C360-REF-16] -->

[PAUSE 2s] You can say *el pantalón* for trousers in general. Name the one kind
of trousers almost everybody in the room is wearing, and see whether you have a
word for it.

## You'll want to know: los vaqueros
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-C430-VIDA-02]; assesses=[] -->

**los vaqueros** — jeans.

Always plural, the way *pantalones* is, because the garment has two legs.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C430-VIDA-02] -->

**La vaca** is a cow, from Latin **vacca**. A **vaquero** is the man who looks
after them — a cowherd, and in the Americas a **cowboy**.

Jeans arrived in Spain as the trousers those men wore, and Spanish named them
after **the wearer** rather than the cloth. English went the other way: *jeans*
comes from **Genoa**, the Italian port whose hard cotton the trousers were cut
from, and *denim* from *de Nîmes*, the French town. Three languages, three
different things noticed about the same garment.

Some regions say **los tejanos** instead — "the Texans" — which is the same
thought arriving from a different direction.

## Grammar Lens: garments that come in pairs
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C430-VIDA-02, ES-LEX-C360-REF-19] -->

| garment | usual number |
|---|---|
| los vaqueros | plural |
| los pantalones | plural |
| la camisa | singular |

Anything with two legs tends to arrive plural. Spanish tolerates *el pantalón*
in the singular, and does **not** do the same for *vaqueros* — you will hear the
plural every time.

The **v** and the **b** sound alike in Spanish, so *vaquero* opens with the same
soft sound as *bueno*. Spelling keeps the Latin **v**; the mouth stopped
distinguishing them centuries ago.
