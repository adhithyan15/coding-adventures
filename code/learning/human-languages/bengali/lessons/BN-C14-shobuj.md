# সবুজ (shobuj) — "green," and five colors, counted up

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[BN-LEX-C14-SHADA-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] One color left, and it is a second loan from the same language
that gave লাল its gem — but this time the loan travelled somewhere you
already have a word for.

## You'll want to know: সবুজ
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[BN-LEX-C14-SHOBUJ-01]; assesses=[] -->

> **সবুজ** — *shôbuj* — **green**

সবুজ চা (*shôbuj chā*) — green tea.

## The letters in this word
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[] -->

**সবুজ** is **স** (*sô*, tilted to *sh*, keeping its own inherent vowel
since nothing joins it) plus **ব** (*bô*) with the vowel sign **ু** (*u*),
plus **জ** (*j*, silent at the end): *shô-bu-j*.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[] -->

**সবুজ** is borrowed from Persian **sabz**, "green" — the very same Persian
word that gave Hindi and Urdu their word for "vegetable," **sabzī**,
literally "the green things." লাল's own Persian gem-name arrived by the
same route, from the same trading world, into a different corner of the
vocabulary.

Step back and count the five colors this small set has taught: two Persian
loans (**লাল**, **সবুজ**), two Sanskrit words kept recognizably close to
their root (**নীল**, **কালো**), and one worn down by sound change
(**সাদা**). A more even split between inherited and borrowed than the food
words or the family words showed — color, it turns out, came into Bengali
from everywhere at once.
