Portuguese, chapter 492: Uma família alargada.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
o cunhado — brother-in-law.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Portuguese for to fall in love, then the Portuguese for to hug.

You'll want to know: o cunhado.
o cunhado — "brother-in-law".
O meu cunhado é casado com a minha irmã. My brother-in-law is married to my sister.
A cunhada — sister-in-law.

The word, taken apart.
cunhado is inherited, through Old Portuguese cunnado, from Latin cognātus "related by blood" (EtymDB).

Lesson 2 of 5.
o padrasto — stepfather.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Portuguese for to hug, then the Portuguese for brother-in-law.

You'll want to know: o padrasto.
o padrasto — "stepfather".
Ela dá-se muito bem com o padrasto. She gets on very well with her stepfather.
Built on Latin pater "father", not on pai.

The word, taken apart.
padrasto is inherited from Latin patraster (EtymDB), built on pater "father"; in Latin inscriptions the word named a father-in-law (Lewis & Short), and in Portuguese it settled on a stepfather.

Lesson 3 of 5.
a madrasta — stepmother.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Portuguese for brother-in-law, then the Portuguese for stepfather.

You'll want to know: a madrasta.
a madrasta — "stepmother".
A minha madrasta trata-me como uma filha. My stepmother treats me like a daughter.
A neutral word in everyday use, though fairy tales made a madrasta má a stock villain.

The word, taken apart.
madrasta is usually traced to Late Latin mātrastra "stepmother", built on māter "mother" (Lewis & Short).

Lesson 5 of 5.
o parente — relative.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Portuguese for stepmother, then the Portuguese for stepson.

You'll want to know: o parente.
o parente — "relative".
Nas festas de família juntamos todos os parentes. At family parties we get all our relatives together.
Not English parent: it means any relative. Parents are os pais.

The word, taken apart.
parente is inherited from Latin parentem "a parent" (EtymDB), from parere "to give birth"; in Portuguese it widened to any relative.
