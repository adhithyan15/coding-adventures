Latin, chapter 448: To snore, To make a noise, To make a din, Drinker, Hoarse.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
stertō, stertere — to snore.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for sleepless, then the Latin for to lie down.

You'll want to know: stertō, stertere.
stertō, stertere — "to snore".
Hospes ēbrius tōtam noctem stertit. The drunken guest snores all night.
stertō, stertere, stertuī. English stertorous 'heavy and snoring' comes from it.

The word, taken apart.
The origin of stertō is not settled: Lewis & Short compare Greek darthanō 'to sleep', but EtymDB gives no parent. English stertor and stertorous come from it.

Lesson 2 of 5.
strepō, strepere — to make a noise, to clatter, to roar.

Warm-up.

Before the new one: say the Latin for to lie down, then the Latin for to snore.

You'll want to know: strepō, strepere.
strepō, strepere — "to make a noise, to clatter, to roar".
In viā plaustra strepunt, et nēmō dormīre potest. In the street the wagons clatter, and nobody can sleep.
strepō, strepere, strepuī, strepitum. Any loud noise: wheels, voices, water.

The word, taken apart.
The origin of strepō is not settled: Lewis & Short give its senses, to rattle, rumble, roar, but no etymology, and EtymDB gives no parent.

Lesson 3 of 5.
obstrepō, obstrepere — to make a din, to drown out with noise.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to snore, then the Latin for to make a noise.

You'll want to know: obstrepō, obstrepere.
obstrepō, obstrepere — "to make a din, to drown out with noise".
Virī ēbriī in caupōnā obstrepunt, et hospitēs queruntur. Drunken men make a din in the inn, and the guests complain.
obstrepō, obstrepere, obstrepuī, obstrepitum, often with a dative: to roar at someone. English obstreperous comes from it.

The word, taken apart.
obstrepō is built from ob- 'against' and strepō 'to make a noise' (Lewis & Short). English obstreperous comes from obstreperus, built on it.

Lesson 4 of 5.
pōtor, pōtōris — a drinker, a heavy drinker.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to make a noise, then the Latin for to make a din.

You'll want to know: pōtor, pōtōris.
pōtor, pōtōris — "a drinker, a heavy drinker".
In tabernā pōtōrēs multum vīnum bibunt. In the tavern the drinkers drink a lot of wine.
Third declension, masculine: pōtor, pōtōris m., from the same root as pōtus 'drunk'.

The word, taken apart.
pōtor is built on the root of pōtō 'to drink' with the suffix -tor 'one who' (Lewis & Short). Greek pinō 'to drink' is a cousin of that root.

Lesson 5 of 5.
raucus, rauca, raucum — hoarse; harsh-sounding.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to make a din, then the Latin for a drinker.

You'll want to know: raucus, rauca, raucum.
raucus, rauca, raucum — "hoarse; harsh-sounding".
Post noctem longam vōx caupōnis rauca est. After a long night the innkeeper's voice is hoarse.
An adjective of three endings. English raucous comes from it.

The word, taken apart.
raucus is built on rāvis 'hoarseness' with an adjective suffix (EtymDB); Lewis & Short trace it to a root meaning 'to make a loud noise'. English raucous comes from it.
