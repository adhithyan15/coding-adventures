Lesson 1 of 2.
avere — "to have".

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, because the lesson points at something written down. You can listen to all of it now and come back to the parts that need looking at once you have stopped.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Italian's workhorse verb — and the home of the language's only silent letter, which is there for a reason worth knowing.

You'll want to know: the forms.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
ho, abbiamo, blank.
hai, avete, blank.
ha, hanno, blank.
Look at which forms carry an h: ho, hai, ha, hanno. And which don't: abbiamo, avete.

Grammar Lens: why silent h survives.
Italian threw the Latin h away almost completely — it is never pronounced, and uomo ( from homō) and erba ( from herba) simply dropped it.
But in avere it stayed, because without it these words would collide:
[a table of 4 rows, read aloud]
with h: ho — I have. without h: o. meaning: "or".
with h: hai — you have. without h: ai. meaning: "to the".
with h: ha — he/she has. without h: a. meaning: "to".
with h: hanno — they have. without h: anno. meaning: "year".
Four minimal pairs, all extremely common. The h is silent, does nothing to the sound, and is kept only so your eye can tell the words apart. Spelling doing a job pronunciation can't — hold onto hanno vs anno; it comes back in the next lesson.

The word, taken apart.
avere from Latin habēre, the same source as French avoir. English took it as a whole family: habit (what you have regularly), inhabit, exhibit ("hold out"), prohibit ("hold back").
Note abbiamo — the bb is the old habē- resurfacing in the noi form, while ho wore down to a single vowel.

[pause 3 seconds]

Lesson 2 of 2.
ho venti anni — having your years.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Italian states age with avere, not essere. And the silent h from last lesson stops being trivia here — it becomes the difference between two real sentences.

You'll want to know: the phrase.
Quanti anni hai? — "How many years do you have?" Ho venti anni. — "I have twenty years."
Essere is wrong here. Sono venti anni does not mean "I am twenty."

The word, taken apart: anno.
anno ("year") from Latin annus becomes English annual, anniversary, annals, per annum. Chapter 9's months and seasons came from the same calendar vocabulary.
Note the double n: a-nno, held. Italian's double consonants are real length, not decoration.

Grammar Lens: h earns its keep.
Now put the last lesson to work:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
Hanno venti anni. means "They have twenty years."
anno means "year".
Hanno and anno sound exactly the same. Only the silent h tells them apart — and in a sentence about years, both words show up at once. That is precisely why Italian kept a letter it never pronounces.

Grammar Lens: who has and who is.
[a table of 6 rows, read aloud]
language: Italian, ho venti anni — have.
language: French, j'ai vingt ans — have.
language: Spanish, tengo veinte años — have.
language: Portuguese, tenho vinte anos — have.
language: German, ich bin zwanzig Jahre alt — be.
language: English, I am twenty — be.
Romance has its years; Germanic is its years.

Which verb does Italian use for age? (Avere, never essere.) What does ho venti anni literally say? ("I have twenty years.") Where does anno come from? (Latin annus becomes annual/anniversary.)
