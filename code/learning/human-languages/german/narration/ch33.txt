German, chapter 33: Sorry.
2 lessons. All 2 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 2.
Entschuldigung — “sorry” / “excuse me”.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
You already know bitte does triple duty for "please," "you're welcome," and "here you go." German's word for sorry is just as hard-working: Entschuldigung covers both "excuse me" and "I'm sorry."

The word, taken apart.
ent- = a "removal / undoing" prefix — the same shape as in entdecken ("un-cover" becomes discover) or entfernen ("un-distance" becomes remove).
Schuld = "guilt, fault, blame" (also, separately, "debt" — a Schuld can be money owed or wrong done).
-igung = a noun-forming suffix, roughly "-ing/-ation."
So Entschuldigung is literally "an un-guilting" — the act of removing blame. Saying it hands the guilt back: I release you from any fault (when getting someone's attention or brushing past) or I ask you to release me from mine (when apologizing).
Schuld has a striking cousin: it traces to the same ancient Germanic root as English shall and should — a verb that originally meant "to owe." Schuld (what is owed, guilt) and shall (what one is obliged to do) are, at root, the same idea of an obligation outstanding.

Why it's said this way: Everyday repair and deeper regret.
For deeper regret — real sorrow, not a bump in the hallway — German often reaches for es tut mir leid, literally "it does to-me sorrow":
es = "it," tut = "does" (from tun, "to do"), mir = "to me," Leid = "sorrow, suffering" (a cousin of English loath, both from a root meaning "hateful, painful").
Use Entschuldigung for the everyday sorry/excuse-me (stepping on a foot, interrupting), and es tut mir leid when you mean it more — condolences, a real regret. The two overlap; native speakers move between them by feel.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: "Schuld" — guilt/fault — then "Entschuldigung," sorry / excuse me]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: the deeper phrase — "es tut mir leid," it pains me / I'm so sorry]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
What does Entschuldigung literally mean, piece by piece? ("Un-guilting" — ent- "away" + Schuld "guilt/fault.") What English word shares Schuld's ancient root? (Shall/should — both from a root meaning "to owe.") What's the deeper phrase for real regret? (Es tut mir leid, "it does to me sorrow.")


Lesson 2 of 2.
danke, bitte, Entschuldigung — and the sizes each of them comes in.

Warm-up.
[pause 3 seconds]
Thank someone. Then say the word that answers it. Both are two syllables ending in the same unstressed -e — say them back to back and let that ending stay weak.

Guided Practice: three sizes, smallest first.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: the plain one — danke]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: the one that upgrades it with an adjective — danke schön]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: the warmest everyday one — vielen Dank]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
Notice what changed in the third. Danke is a verb form — I thank — but vielen Dank is the noun, capitalised, with viel agreeing in front of it. That is why viel has an ending there and schön does not: one is describing a noun, the other is not.

Guided Practice: the word that answers everything.
[pause 1 second]
bitte is four words in one coat. Say it four times, meaning something different each time:
[your turn — say: please, asking for something]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: you're welcome, answering a thank-you]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: here you go, handing something over]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: pardon?, when you did not catch it]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: and the two shortest answers there are — ja, nein]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Why it's said this way.
Entschuldigung is built out of Schuld, guilt or fault, with a prefix that undoes it: an un-guilting. The word is a request that the blame be removed, which is why it works both for bumping into someone and for getting their attention in the first place.
That is worth holding against English, where sorry and excuse me split the job in two. German runs one word across both, and the size of the apology is carried by your voice rather than by a different word.

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Why is it vielen Dank and not viel Dank? (Dank is a noun, so viel takes an ending.) What is inside Entschuldigung? (Schuld, guilt — the word asks for it to be undone.) Name two things bitte can mean.
