Latin, chapter 357: What I Think and Judge.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
arbitror, arbitrārī — to be of the opinion, to judge.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a ditch, then the Latin for a rampart.

You'll want to know: arbitror, arbitrārī.
arbitror, arbitrārī — "to be of the opinion, to judge".
Arbitror eum vērum dīcere. I believe he is telling the truth.
Deponent: arbitror, arbitrārī, arbitrātus sum, passive in form and active in meaning. Like putō it takes the accusative and infinitive. English arbitrate comes from it.

The word, taken apart.
arbitror is built on arbiter "an eyewitness, an umpire" (Lewis & Short; EtymDB): to judge a thing as one who saw it. English arbiter, arbitrate and arbitrary come from the same family.

Lesson 2 of 5.
exīstimō, exīstimāre — to reckon, to consider; to value.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a rampart, then the Latin for to be of the opinion.

You'll want to know: exīstimō, exīstimāre.
exīstimō, exīstimāre — "to reckon, to consider; to value".
Quid dē hōc librō exīstimās? What do you think of this book?
exīstimō, exīstimāre, exīstimāvī, exīstimātum. With the accusative and infinitive: exīstimō tē errāre, I reckon you are wrong.

The word, taken apart.
exīstimō is built from ex "out" and aestimō "to value, to put a price on" (Lewis & Short): to weigh a thing up and give the result. English estimate and esteem come from aestimō.

Lesson 3 of 5.
cēnseō, cēnsēre — to give an opinion, to propose; to assess.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to be of the opinion, then the Latin for to reckon.

You'll want to know: cēnseō, cēnsēre.
cēnseō, cēnsēre — "to give an opinion, to propose; to assess".
Ego cēnseō pācem meliōrem esse quam bellum. In my opinion peace is better than war.
cēnseō, cēnsēre, cēnsuī, cēnsum. The verb of a senator stating his view in the senate, and of the officer who counted and valued the citizens. English census comes from it.

The word, taken apart.
cēnseō is inherited from Proto-Italic kensēō "to assess, to judge" (EtymDB), and has a Sanskrit cousin meaning "to declare". English census comes from it.

Lesson 4 of 5.
opīnor, opīnārī — to suppose, to imagine.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to reckon, then the Latin for to give an opinion.

You'll want to know: opīnor, opīnārī.
opīnor, opīnārī — "to suppose, to imagine".
Ille, opīnor, nōn veniet. He will not come, I suppose.
Deponent: opīnor, opīnārī, opīnātus sum. Dropped into a sentence, opīnor softens it, like English I suppose. English opine comes from it.

The word, taken apart.
opīnor comes from an old adjective opīnus "expecting" (EtymDB), which survives in necopīnus "unexpected" (Lewis & Short). English opine and opinion come from it.

Lesson 5 of 5.
opīniō, opīniōnis — an opinion, a belief; a reputation.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to give an opinion, then the Latin for to suppose.

You'll want to know: opīniō, opīniōnis.
opīniō, opīniōnis — "an opinion, a belief; a reputation".
Haec est opīniō mea, nōn tua. This is my opinion, not yours.
opīniō, opīniōnis f., third declension. It is also what people think of you: bona opīniō, a good name. English opinion.

The word, taken apart.
opīniō is the noun built on opīnor "to suppose" (EtymDB; Lewis & Short), and English opinion comes from it through French.
