## Grammar Lens: three accents, three different jobs
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C452-REPARTO-01, ES-LEX-C452-REPARTO-04] -->

Two of this chapter's words carry a written accent, and they carry it for
opposite reasons:

| word | why the mark is there |
|---|---|
| almac**é**n | stress on the last syllable, ending in *n* |
| c**ó**modo | stress three syllables from the end |

Spanish defaults to the second-to-last syllable for a word ending in a vowel,
*n* or *s*. *Almacén* breaks that by going later; *cómodo* breaks it by going
earlier. Both need a mark to say so, and *ruta* and *seguimiento* need none
because both obey the default.

That is why *almacenes* loses the accent while *cómodos* keeps it. The mark
is a **correction to a rule**, not a decoration attached to a word, so it
appears and disappears as the word changes shape.

## Grammar Lens: two of the four were already yours
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C452-REPARTO-02, ES-LEX-C452-REPARTO-03] -->

| new word | what it was built on |
|---|---|
| **la ruta** | *roto* — Latin *via rupta*, a broken way |
| **el seguimiento** | *seguir* plus the *-miento* of *entrenamiento* |
| **cómodo** | *el modo*, a measure, with *com-* in front |

*La ruta* is the one to keep. You learned *roto* for a plate in two halves,
and the same Latin participle names a road — because a road is what you get
when something in the way has been broken through.

Only **el almacén** arrived from outside the family, and it came from Arabic
with its article still stuck to the front.
