Lesson 1 of 8.
ఒకటి, రెండు, మూడు, నాలుగు, ఐదు — one to five.

Before we start: you can do this one in the car, but part of it needs your eyes. For that part you will want your eyes, for letter shapes on the page. You can listen to everything else now. Warm-up.

Four of these five are old Dravidian friends. The very first one is Telugu going its own way.

You'll want to know: The five.
[a table of 5 rows, read aloud]
numeral: ౧ (1). word: ఒకటి. said: okaṭi.
numeral: ౨ (2). word: రెండు. said: reṇḍu.
numeral: ౩ (3). word: మూడు. said: mūḍu.
numeral: ౪ (4). word: నాలుగు. said: nālugu.
numeral: ౫ (5). word: ఐదు. said: aidu.
The symbols ౧ ౨ ౩ ౪ ౫ are Telugu's own digits.

The word, taken apart - The odd one, then the family.
One is where Telugu leaves the family. Its cousins agree — Tamil oṉṟu, Kannada ondu, Malayalam onnu, all from an old oṉ- — but Telugu says okaṭi, a different formation altogether. It's the number to memorise fresh rather than reason across from Tamil.
From two onward, the family closes ranks again:
[a table of 4 rows, read aloud]
2. Tamil: iraṇṭu. Telugu: reṇḍu.
3. Tamil: mūṉṟu. Telugu: mūḍu.
4. Tamil: nāṉku. Telugu: nālugu.
5. Tamil: aintu. Telugu: aidu.
Reṇḍu has simply dropped the opening vowel of iraṇṭu; mūḍu is mūṉṟu with its cluster smoothed to a single ḍ. Same numbers, a Telugu polish.

[once you have stopped driving — this part needs your eyes or your hands; if you are driving, skip ahead to the next part]
Script you'll notice: A Telugu shape to notice.
Nearly every Telugu word here ends in ‑u (reṇḍu, mūḍu, nālugu) — the language's strong preference for open, vowel-final syllables. Even where Tamil ends hard, Telugu tends to round the word off with a vowel.

Script you'll notice: ఐ.
ఐ — ai.
It is an independent vowel — a vowel that is a letter in its own right, not a mark hung on a consonant.
ఐదు aidu — five.
Say aidu slowly and listen to the opening. Your mouth does not hold one position: it starts low and slides up toward i. Two sounds in one movement, which is what the romanization's two letters are trying to show.
Telugu writes that whole movement with one character. English needs a digraph or a silent letter for the same job — aisle, height, my — and picks a different trick each time. Here the slide has a shape of its own, and it looks nothing like the ఏ you met a chapter ago even though the romanizations share a letter.

Follow the numbered strip: start where movement 1 begins, and let each panel show you the next movement before your pen makes it. Then write ఐ (ai) yourself, slowly and larger than it is printed, with ఏ beside it, and look at the two.
The strip shows where to start the character and which way to travel, in one attested order. That is taught with real variation from school to school, so the strip names its source beneath it: treat it as a sound way in, not the only one.

Script you'll notice: ◌ూ.
◌ూ — ū.
It is a vowel sign. It is not a letter and never stands alone: it attaches to a consonant and replaces the a built into it. Replaces, not adds — the a is gone.
You already know its short partner. ◌ు puts a quick u on a consonant; ◌ూ (ū) puts a held one on it — the same vowel, kept twice as long. Telugu counts that length as the difference between two different words, so the two signs are two different characters and not two ways of writing one.
Say mu, then say mū and let the vowel run on. That extra length is all the second mark is asking for.
You already say this one, and the held vowel is sitting in the middle of it:
మూడు mūḍu — three, from the numbers you counted out.

Lesson 4 of 8.
ఆరు, ఏడు, ఎనిమిది, తొమ్మిది, పది — six to ten.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
The top of the count is where Telugu is least like its cousins — two of these you simply learn on their own.

You'll want to know: The five.
[a table of 5 rows, read aloud]
numeral: ౬ (6). word: ఆరు. said: āru.
numeral: ౭ (7). word: ఏడు. said: ēḍu.
numeral: ౮ (8). word: ఎనిమిది. said: enimidi.
numeral: ౯ (9). word: తొమ్మిది. said: tommidi.
numeral: ౧౦ (10). word: పది. said: padi.

The word, taken apart - Seven, and a lost sound.
Six is an easy cousin (Tamil āṟu becomes Telugu āru). Seven is more interesting: Tamil ēḻu carries the rare ḻ (the zh of Tamiḻ); Kannada hardened it to ḷ (ēḷu); Telugu went further still and turned it into a plain ḍ — ēḍu. One inherited sound, three fates across the three scripts.

The word, taken apart - Eight and nine strike out alone.
Here Telugu strays furthest from its cousins —.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
number: 8. Tamil / Malayalam: eṭṭu / eṭṭu. Telugu: enimidi.
number: 9. Tamil / Malayalam: oṉpatu / onpatu. Telugu: tommidi.
You can't reach enimidi and tommidi from Tamil by regular sound-change the way you can reṇḍu from iraṇṭu: tommidi is a different word entirely, and enimidi — though it shares the ancient eṇ- root behind eṭṭu — has drifted too far to derive. So unlike 2–7, learn these two fresh.
Ten comes partly back into the fold — Telugu padi is a worn-down relative of the pat‑ in Tamil pattu (and the ‑patu hiding inside tommidi and Tamil oṉpatu, "one-to-ten").

You'll want to know: four pieces, and everything they build.
The question you learned and its answer are made of the same four pieces:
[a table of 4 rows, read aloud]
piece: పేరు pēru, name.
piece: నా nā, మీ mī, my, your.
piece: ఏమిటి ēmiṭi, what?
piece: నువ్వు nuvvu, మీరు mīru, you, close, you, respectful.
Two side by side give the answer, నా పేరు … The respectful one in front and the question word behind give the question, మీ పేరు ఏమిటి?

Grammar Lens: the two things the name exchange was really teaching.
Two points sit under those four pieces:
No word for is. Nā pēru Arun is "my name Arun," and the link stays unsaid in every sentence of this shape.
Respect rides on the plural. మీరు is a plural doing the work of politeness — the move Tamil nīṅgaḷ and French vous also make.
The familiar your goes with the familiar you: నీ పేరు ఏమిటి?
