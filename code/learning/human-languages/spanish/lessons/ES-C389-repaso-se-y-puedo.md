# Review — the verbs that will not sit behind *sé*

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-SABER, ES-GRAMMAR-SABER-INFINITIVO] -->

[PAUSE 2s] "I know how to throw." (*Sé lanzar*.)

## What you've built
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-SABER, ES-GRAMMAR-SABER-INFINITIVO, ES-LEX-PODER] -->

You have *saber* plus a plain verb, and you have the four verbs from the chapter
before. Put them together and something useful goes wrong.

| | behind *sé* | behind *puedo* |
|---|---|---|
| *lanzar* | **sé lanzar** — fine | **puedo lanzar** — fine |
| *borrar* | **sé borrar** — fine | **puedo borrar** — fine |
| *empujar* | *sé empujar* — **odd** | **puedo empujar** — fine |
| *gritar* | *sé gritar* — **odd** | **puedo gritar** — fine |

[PAUSE 2s] All four go behind *puedo* without a blink. Only two go behind *sé*.

## Why it's said this way
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-GRAMMAR-SABER-INFINITIVO, ES-LEX-GRITAR, ES-LEX-EMPUJAR] -->

*Saber* before a plain verb does not mean "can." It means **know how to** — and
knowing how is something you were once taught.

Throwing well is learned. Erasing properly is learned. But nobody was ever taught
to **shout**, and nobody took lessons in **pushing**. So *sé gritar* lands
strangely. It is not ungrammatical; it claims a skill where there is none.

[PAUSE 2s] That is the cleanest test there is for the difference you met with
*poder*. *Puedo gritar* is ordinary: I am able to, right now, or I am allowed to.
Swap in *sé* and the sentence quietly claims you studied it.

*Sé nadar pero hoy no puedo.* The skill is permanent; the chance is not.

And the root says the same thing from underneath. *Saber* is Latin *sapere*, "to
taste" — and the one who can taste is the one who can **tell things apart**.
Discrimination first, knowledge second. It is why the verb for having a flavour
and the verb for having a skill are one verb in Spanish.
