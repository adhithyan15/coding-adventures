8 lessons. You can do the first 6 of them in the car; after that you will want to have stopped.


Lesson 1 of 8.
antipático — the opposite made by fastening four letters on the front.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say simpático. Now say what you would expect its opposite to look like, before reading on. This is one of the times the obvious guess is right.

You'll want to know first.
simpático — likeable, and a false friend: it does not mean sympathetic.

Sounds you'll need.
accent-acute — an-ti-PÁ-ti-co: the written accent sits where the stress does, and adding anti- does not move it. see reference.
t-dental — both t sounds are made with the tongue on the teeth, with no puff of air after them.

The word, taken apart.
antipático — "unpleasant, disagreeable." Take simpático and put Greek anti-, "against, opposite," on the front.
The two Greek prefixes underneath are a pair, and seeing them together makes both words transparent:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
prefix: sym-. Greek meaning: together with. with -pático: feeling with you.
prefix: anti-. Greek meaning: against. with -pático: feeling against you.
The -patí- in the middle is páthos, "feeling, suffering" — the same piece inside English sympathy, empathy and pathetic.
So simpático and antipático are literally feeling-with and feeling-against. Spanish uses them for people who are pleasant and unpleasant to be around, which is a fair everyday translation of the same idea.
Anti- is doing exactly the job you already know it does in English: antibiotic, antiseptic, Antiarctic — against life, against rot, opposite the Bear.

What you've built.
Both words end in -o and both change ending to match the person:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
pleasant. masculine: simpático. feminine: simpática.
unpleasant. masculine: antipático. feminine: antipática.
The prefix never moves and never agrees. Only the last letter does the work.

[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: both in the feminine (simpática, antipática)]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: which Greek word for feeling is buried in the middle of both (páthos)]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
What does anti- mean? (Against.) What does the -patí- in the middle mean? (Feeling.) Does the written accent move when the prefix is added? (No — it stays on PÁ.)


Lesson 2 of 8.
inteligente — picking between things, which is all intelligence ever was.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say alegre. Notice it ends in -e and never changes for a man or a woman. The word in this lesson does the same thing.

You'll want to know first.
alegre — cheerful, and the first -e adjective you met that refuses to agree.

Sounds you'll need.
g-soft — the g in -gente comes before e, so it is the throaty sound. In-te-li-JEN-te, not gen as in English. see reference.
stress-penultimate — stress on JEN, the second syllable from the end, and the word carries no written accent because that is already the default.

The word, taken apart.
inteligente — "intelligent." Latin intellegere, which is two pieces:
inter — between.
legere — to gather, to pick out, and later to read.
To be intelligent is to pick between. Not to know a great deal; to choose well among the things in front of you. The Romans named the ability after the sorting, not after the store of facts.
Legere is one of the most productive verbs Latin ever handed over, and every one of these is the same act of picking:
[a table of 5 rows, read aloud]
word: select. what is picked: chosen out (se- apart + legere).
word: collect. what is picked: picked together.
word: elect. what is picked: picked out of a group.
word: legible. what is picked: able to be picked out by eye.
word: lecture. what is picked: a reading aloud.
Spanish has them too: seleccionar, colección, elegir, legible, lectura.

What you've built.
Two shapes of adjective, side by side:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
ending: -o. masculine: antipático. feminine: antipática.
ending: -e. masculine: inteligente. feminine: inteligente.
An -o adjective has two forms. An -e adjective has one. That is the whole distinction, and it saves you from ever inventing inteligenta.

[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: "antipático e inteligente" — and hear only the first one able to change]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: which English word for choosing a leader comes from legere (elect)]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
What two Latin pieces are inside inteligente? (Inter, between, and legere, to pick out.) Does it change for a woman? (No — it ends in -e.)


Lesson 3 of 8.
trabajador — the same ending as actor, four hundred years further along.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say trabajar. Say el actor. One is a verb and one is a person, and this lesson turns the first into the second using the ending the second already showed you.

You'll want to know first.
trabajar — to work, and a word with a violent past you have already been told about.
el actor — the doer, built with Latin -tor.

Sounds you'll need.
stress-final — tra-ba-ja-DOR, stress on the last syllable, exactly as in actor. see reference.
b-soft — the b between vowels is soft, with the lips nearly but not quite touching.

The word, taken apart.
trabajador — "hard-working." Take trabajar, drop the -ar, add -dor.
That ending is the Latin -tor from actor, worn down by centuries of Spanish mouths. Latin -tor became Spanish -dor between vowels, and Spanish then used it on its own verbs to build new words the Romans never had:
[a table of 4 rows, read aloud]
verb: trabajar. meaning: to work. the one who does it: trabajador.
verb: jugar. meaning: to play. the one who does it: jugador.
verb: hablar. meaning: to speak. the one who does it: hablador.
verb: ganar. meaning: to win. the one who does it: ganador.
Actor was borrowed from Latin whole and kept the old -tor. Trabajador was built inside Spanish and got the modern -dor. Same ending, two different ages, and the pair tells you which words came in through books and which grew at home.
Here is the piece that surprises people: trabajador is both a noun and an adjective. Un trabajador is a worker. Es trabajador means he is hard-working. The word does not change; only the sentence around it does.

What you've built.
A third shape of adjective, and it is not the -o one:
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
adjective: antipático. masculine: antipático. feminine: antipática.
adjective: inteligente. masculine: inteligente. feminine: inteligente.
adjective: trabajador. masculine: trabajador. feminine: trabajadora.
An adjective ending in a consonant normally refuses to agree. -dor is one of the exceptions that does: it adds -a and nothing else. Una mujer trabajadora.

[your turn — say: "el actor es trabajador" — the borrowed ending and the home-grown one in a single sentence]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]

Lesson 4 of 8.
serio — the opposite of cheerful, and a word about weight.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say alegre. This lesson gives you the other end of that same scale.

You'll want to know first.
alegre — cheerful, the word printed on sheet music as allegro.

Sounds you'll need.
stress-penultimate — SE-rio: -io is a single syllable here, so the stress lands on SE. see reference.
r-tap — one flick, between two vowels, which is the easiest place in Spanish to get it right.

The word, taken apart.
serio — "serious, earnest." Latin serius, whose first meaning was weighty, heavy.
Every language seems to reach for weight when it wants to talk about gravity of manner, and English does it three separate times without noticing:
a grave matter — Latin gravis, heavy.
a weighty decision.
a matter of some gravity.
Spanish does the same with serio and grave. The metaphor is so worn that nobody hears it any more: a serious person is a person who is hard to lift.
One warning worth having. English serious has drifted toward meaning bad — a serious accident. Spanish serio stayed with the manner: a persona seria is earnest and reliable, not damaged. For the bad kind Spanish uses grave.

What you've built.
Two words for mood and manner, at opposite ends and in different shapes:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
ending: -e (cheerful). masculine: alegre. feminine: alegre.
ending: -o (serious). masculine: serio. feminine: seria.
You now have both ends of the scale, and each one demonstrates a different agreement rule.

Lesson 5 of 8.
tímido — fear, and the verb for putting it into somebody.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say serio. A serious person and a shy person look similar from the outside and are nothing alike inside. This lesson names the second one.

You'll want to know first.
serio — earnest, from Latin for weighty.

Sounds you'll need.
accent-acute — TÍ-mi-do: the accent marks the stress on the third syllable from the end, which Spanish always writes out. see reference.
t-dental — tongue against the teeth, no puff of air. An English t here is the single clearest giveaway of an English speaker.

The word, taken apart.
tímido — "shy, timid." Latin timidus, from the verb timere, "to fear."
Timere built a small family, and English took all of it:
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
word: timid. literally: inclined to fear.
word: timorous. literally: full of fear.
word: intimidate. literally: to put fear into someone.
That last one is the useful one. In- means into. To intimidate somebody is to place the fear inside them — the exact fear that tímido names. The shy person and the bully are described with the same Latin root, from opposite ends.
Spanish keeps the whole family too: tímido, temer (to fear), intimidar.
A note on what the word does not say. Tímido describes how a person meets strangers, not whether they are frightened at this moment. For being afraid right now, Spanish says tener miedo. This is settled character, which is the whole point of the chapter.

What you've built.
Three -o adjectives now, all agreeing the same way:
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
masculine: antipático. feminine: antipática.
masculine: serio. feminine: seria.
masculine: tímido. feminine: tímida.
Two of the three carry a written accent, and in both cases the accent stays exactly where it was when the ending changes: tímido becomes tímida.

[your turn — say: "tímida", and notice the accent has not moved]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: "serio y tímido"]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: which English verb means to put fear into someone (intimidate)]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
What does Latin timere mean? (To fear.) What does the in- of intimidate add? (Into — putting the fear into someone.) Does tímido mean frightened right now? (No — that is tener miedo.)


Lesson 6 of 8.
sociable — a companion, an ending, and the strangest word in English sport.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say tímido. Now say the opposite kind of person, and expect the word to look almost exactly like its English twin.

You'll want to know first.
tímido — shy, from Latin for fear.

Sounds you'll need.
stress-penultimate — so-CIA-ble, stress on the second syllable from the end and no written accent, because that is the default. see reference.
b-soft — the b is soft, between a vowel and an l.

The word, taken apart.
sociable — "sociable, outgoing." Latin socius, "a companion, an ally, a sharer," plus -able, "able to be, inclined to."
Socius is one of those roots that turns out to be everywhere:
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
word: society. the companion in it: the whole body of companions.
word: associate. the companion in it: to make someone a companion.
word: social. the companion in it: to do with companions.
And then there is the one nobody expects. English association football was shortened by Oxford students, who liked adding -er to clipped words, into soccer — from assoc. The most ordinary word in world sport has socius sitting inside it, meaning nothing more than companion.
Spanish has the family too: la sociedad, social, el socio — which is a business partner or a club member, and is socius almost unchanged after two thousand years.

What you've built.
The six character words of this chapter sort into three agreement shapes, and now you have seen all three:
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
shape: -o. example: tímido. feminine: tímida.
shape: -e. example: inteligente. feminine: inteligente.
shape: -able. example: sociable. feminine: sociable.
-able behaves like -e: one form for everyone. A hombre sociable and a mujer sociable, spelled identically.

Wrap-up Recall.

What did Latin socius mean? (A companion or ally.) Does sociable change for a woman? (No — -able has one form.) Which everyday Spanish word for a club member is socius barely changed? (El socio.)


Lesson 7 of 8.
ocho palabras de carácter — eight words, three endings, two pairs.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, because the lesson points at something written down. You can listen to all of it now and come back to the parts that need looking at once you have stopped.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Name as many of the eight as you can before reading the table. Aim for six.

Grammar Lens: the ending decides whether the word can move.
All eight, sorted by what their last letters allow:
[a table of 8 rows, read aloud]
word: simpático. meaning: likeable. feminine: simpática.
word: antipático. meaning: unpleasant. feminine: antipática.
word: serio. meaning: earnest. feminine: seria.
word: tímido. meaning: shy. feminine: tímida.
word: trabajador. meaning: hard-working. feminine: trabajadora.
word: alegre. meaning: cheerful. feminine: alegre.
word: inteligente. meaning: clever. feminine: inteligente.
word: sociable. meaning: outgoing. feminine: sociable.
Read the third column against the first. Where the two differ, the word moves for a woman; where they are identical, it does not.
Five move and three do not, and you can predict which from the spelling alone: a word ending in -o changes, a word ending in -e does not, and -dor is the consonant ending that changes anyway.

Grammar Lens: two of them are matched opposites.
Four of the eight come in pairs, which halves what there is to remember:
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
one end: simpático. other end: antipático. what the pair measures: how you are to be around.
one end: tímido. other end: sociable. what the pair measures: how you meet strangers.
one end: alegre. other end: serio. what the pair measures: how you carry yourself.
Three scales, six words. Inteligente and trabajador stand alone.

Lesson 8 of 8.
¿Cómo es? — the question these eight words exist to answer.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say inteligente and trabajador. Two words a reference letter cannot do without, and you have had neither until now.

Grammar Lens: es for character, está for the moment.
Spanish has two verbs where English has one, and character is the clean half of the split:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
verb: es. what it reports: settled character. example: Es tímido. — He is a shy person.
verb: está. what it reports: how someone is right now. example: Está alegre. — He is cheerful today.
All eight of these adjectives are about settled character, so all eight take es. That is what makes them exam adjectives rather than mood adjectives: a question asking ¿Cómo es tu hermano? wants the lasting answer.
Alegre is the one that can honestly go either way — es alegre is a cheerful person, está alegre is a person having a good day.

Reading.
Read aloud. Every word has been taught:
— ¿Cómo es tu hermana? — Es simpática e inteligente. Es un poco tímida, pero es muy trabajadora. — ¿Y tu hermano? — No es antipático, pero es muy serio.
Two things to catch. Y becomes e before a word starting with the i sound — simpática e inteligente — because y inteligente would blur into one vowel. And every adjective describing hermana has taken its -a, while the ones that cannot change have stayed put.

[your turn — say: "es simpática e inteligente" and hear why it is not y]

[your turn — say: one sentence about somebody you know, using es and two adjectives]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Which of the two verbs does settled character take? (Es.) Why does y become e before inteligente? (The following word begins with the i sound, and the two vowels would run together.) Which of the eight can honestly take either verb? (Alegre.)
