\chapter{Family}
\label{ch:family}

Four words --- father, mother, brother, sister --- and every one of them is
among the oldest words we can reconstruct, older than Latin, older than Rome.
Each is also a key to a whole shelf of English vocabulary.
\emph{Practice lessons: \texttt{lessons/FR-C10-*.md}.}

\section{\emph{le p\`{e}re}, \emph{la m\`{e}re} --- the oldest words in the family}
\label{lesson:parents}

The words for ``father'' and ``mother'' are among the \textbf{oldest words we
can reconstruct}. French kept them almost bare: \textbf{p\`{e}re} and
\textbf{m\`{e}re}. And they are secretly the \emph{same words} as English
\textbf{father} and \textbf{mother}.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}
\toprule
French & English \\
\midrule
\emph{le p\`{e}re} & the father \\
\emph{la m\`{e}re} & the mother \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Note the articles: \emph{le} p\`{e}re is masculine, \emph{la} m\`{e}re is
feminine --- the gender you have been tracking since the first chapter.

\begin{cousinweb}
\textbf{le p\`{e}re} (``father'') $\leftarrow$ Latin \emph{pater} $\leftarrow$
Proto-Indo-European \emph{*ph$_2$t\'{e}r}.\\[3pt]
\textbf{la m\`{e}re} (``mother'') $\leftarrow$ Latin \emph{m\=ater}
$\leftarrow$ Proto-Indo-European \emph{*m\'{e}h$_2$t\=er}.\\[3pt]
The Latin root also gives English its ``of a parent'' adjectives:
\textbf{pat}ernal, \textbf{pat}ernity, \textbf{pat}ron; and
\textbf{mat}ernal, \textbf{mat}ernity, \textbf{mat}ron, and even
\textbf{matter} (\emph{m\=ater} $\to$ \emph{materia}, ``the stuff a
tree-mother makes'').
\end{cousinweb}

\begin{grammarlens}
\textbf{One sound-law, two languages.} French took \emph{pater} and
\emph{m\=ater} straight from Latin. English took the \emph{same}
Proto-Indo-European words down a different road, through \textbf{Grimm's law},
which turned \emph{p} $\to$ \emph{f} and \emph{t} $\to$ \emph{th}:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
Proto-Indo-European & Latin / French & English \\
\midrule
\emph{*ph$_2$t\'{e}r} & \emph{pater} $\to$ \emph{p\`{e}re} & \textbf{f}a\textbf{th}er \\
\emph{*m\'{e}h$_2$t\=er} & \emph{m\=ater} $\to$ \emph{m\`{e}re} & \textbf{m}o\textbf{th}er \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

So \emph{p\`{e}re} and \emph{father} are not lookalikes borrowed late --- they
are the \textbf{same inherited word}, one keeping the \emph{p}, the other
shifting it to \emph{f}.
\end{grammarlens}

\section{\emph{le fr\`{e}re}, \emph{la s\oe{}ur} --- brother and sister}
\label{lesson:freres-soeurs}

With parents in hand, the next pair: \textbf{fr\`{e}re} (``brother'') and
\textbf{s\oe{}ur} (``sister''). Both come straight from Latin, and both hand
English a whole family of words.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}
\toprule
French & English \\
\midrule
\emph{le fr\`{e}re} & the brother \\
\emph{la s\oe{}ur} & the sister \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{sounds}
\textbf{s\oe{}ur} is spelled with \oe{} --- an \emph{o} and an \emph{e} fused
into a single letter. It spells the vowel that \emph{soror} wore down to:
\emph{soror} $\to$ \emph{seror} $\to$ \emph{s\oe{}ur}. Spell it aloud as
``s-\oe{}-u-r,'' remembering that the \oe{} counts as \emph{one} letter.
\end{sounds}

\begin{cousinweb}
\textbf{le fr\`{e}re} (``brother'') $\leftarrow$ Latin \emph{fr\=ater}
$\leftarrow$ Proto-Indo-European \emph{*b$^h$r\'{e}h$_2$t\=er} --- the same
root that, through Grimm's law (\emph{b$^h$} $\to$ \emph{b}), gave English
\textbf{brother}. It also gives \textbf{frat}ernal, \textbf{frat}ernity,
\textbf{frat}ernize, and --- softened in old French --- \textbf{friar}, a
``brother'' in a religious order.\\[3pt]
\textbf{la s\oe{}ur} (``sister'') $\leftarrow$ Latin \emph{soror}, behind
English \textbf{soror}ity and \textbf{soror}al; and a sister in a convent is
still called a \emph{s\oe{}ur}. English \textbf{sister} comes from the
\emph{same} Proto-Indo-European root \emph{*sw\'{e}s\=or}, by the Germanic
road --- Latin \emph{soror} and English \emph{sister} are cousins.
\end{cousinweb}

The four family words now form a set --- \emph{p\`{e}re}, \emph{m\`{e}re},
\emph{fr\`{e}re}, \emph{s\oe{}ur} --- and each one is a key to a
Latin-through-English word: \textbf{pat}ernal, \textbf{mat}ernal,
\textbf{frat}ernal, \textbf{soror}ity.
