\chapter{Farewells}
\label{ch:farewells}

Chapter 1 gave you the tender \bn{আসি} (\emph{\=ashi}, ``I'll come [again]''). Its
fuller, everyday form:

\begin{center}
\bn{আবার দেখা হবে!} \quad(\emph{\=ab\=ar d\ae{}kh\=a h\^{o}be} --- ``We'll meet again!'')
\end{center}

\emph{Practice lessons: \texttt{lessons/BN-C04-*.md}.}

\section{\bn{আবার} \emph{(\=ab\=ar)} --- ``again''}
\label{lesson:abar}

\begin{cousinweb}
\bn{আবার} (\emph{\=ab\=ar}, ``again'') is a native Indo-Aryan adverb with no tidy
English cognate, but a familiar job: it turns a parting into a promise. Add it to
Chapter 1's \emph{\=ashi} and you can say ``we'll meet \emph{again}.''
\end{cousinweb}

\section{\bn{দেখা হবে} \emph{(d\ae{}kh\=a h\^{o}be)} --- ``(we) will meet''}
\label{lesson:dekha-hobe}

\begin{cousinweb}
\bn{দেখা হবে} literally ``\textbf{[a] seeing will happen}'' --- \bn{দেখা}
(\emph{d\ae{}kh\=a}, ``seeing,'' from \emph{d\ae{}kh\=a} ``to see'') $+$ \bn{হবে}
(\emph{h\^{o}be}, ``will be/happen,'' future of \emph{h\^{o}w\=a} ``to
be/become''). Bengali says ``a meeting will occur'' rather than ``we will meet''
--- an impersonal, gentle promise of reunion.
\end{cousinweb}

\begin{grammarlens}
\textbf{The impersonal future.} Many ``we'll\ldots'' expressions use \textbf{noun
$+$ \emph{h\^{o}be}} (``\ldots will happen''): \emph{d\ae{}kh\=a h\^{o}be} (``a
seeing will happen''), \emph{k\^{o}th\=a h\^{o}be} (``a talk will happen''). The
event itself is the subject --- no ``we.''
\end{grammarlens}

\section{\bn{আবার দেখা হবে} \emph{(\=ab\=ar d\ae{}kh\=a h\^{o}be)} --- ``we'll meet again''}
\label{lesson:abar-dekha-hobe}

\bn{আবার} (again) $+$ \bn{দেখা হবে} (a seeing will happen) $=$ ``\textbf{we'll meet
again}.'' It is the warm, explicit form of Chapter 1's \emph{\=ashi} --- the
subcontinent's ``promise of return'' (Hindi \emph{phir milenge}, Tamil \emph{p\=oy
varugi\d{r}\=e\d{n}}), never a blunt ``goodbye.''

\section{\bn{কাল দেখা হবে} \emph{(k\=al d\ae{}kh\=a h\^{o}be)} --- ``see you tomorrow''}
\label{lesson:kal-dekha-hobe}

\begin{cousinweb}
\bn{কাল} (\emph{k\=al}) means \textbf{both ``tomorrow'' and ``yesterday''} --- the
day on either side of today --- from Sanskrit \emph{k\=ala} (``time''), the very
root behind Hindi's \emph{kal} and Punjabi's \emph{ak\=al} (``timeless''). The
verb's tense tells which: \emph{k\=al d\ae{}kh\=a h\^{o}be} (future $\to$
``tomorrow''). So the phrase is ``tomorrow, a seeing will happen.''
\end{cousinweb}

\section{The farewells}
\label{lesson:practice}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}
\toprule
Bengali & English \\
\midrule
\bn{আসি।} & I'll come [again]. (Ch.1 goodbye) \\
\bn{আবার দেখা হবে।} & We'll meet again. \\
\bn{কাল দেখা হবে।} & See you tomorrow. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Roots banked: \emph{d\ae{}kh\=a h\^{o}be} (the impersonal ``a seeing will
happen''), \emph{k\=al} (\emph{k\=ala} ``time,'' cousin of Hindi \emph{kal},
Punjabi \emph{ak\=al}). Next chapter: the first real verbs --- \emph{\=ami
b\=angl\=a b\^{o}li} --- and Bengali's total lack of gender.
