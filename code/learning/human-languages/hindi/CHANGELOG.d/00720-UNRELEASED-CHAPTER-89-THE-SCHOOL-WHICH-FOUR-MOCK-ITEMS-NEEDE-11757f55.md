### Both registers, because the two papers want different ones

**स्कूल** (*skūl*) is borrowed whole from English, and carries a joke it did not
ask for: English *school* comes from Greek **skholḗ**, which meant **leisure**.
The Greeks thought study was what you did with your free time, and every
language that borrowed the word borrowed the joke — *escuela*, *Schule*,
*shkola*, स्कूल.

**विद्यालय** (*vidyālay*) is built rather than borrowed: **विद्या** knowledge +
**आलय** abode. आलय is productive — *himālaya* is हिम (snow) + आलय, the abode of
snow — and विद्या rests on the root **विद्** "to know", which reaches Latin
*vidēre* (video, vision, evidence) and English **wit** and **wisdom**.

The chapter teaches both because the papers split: a reading paper is built of
signs, forms and notices, where the formal word appears, and a listening paper
of people talking, where the casual one does. A candidate with only one of them
stalls on half the exam.

This is the third appearance of the borrowed/built pair, and the review lesson
names the pattern: नाश्ता against दोपहर का खाना, स्कूल against विद्यालय.
