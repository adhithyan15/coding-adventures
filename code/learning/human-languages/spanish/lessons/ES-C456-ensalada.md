# la ensalada — the salted thing

## You'll want to know: la ensalada
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-C456-CUENTA-01]; assesses=[ES-LEX-VERDURA] -->

**la ensalada** — the salad.

*Una ensalada mixta* — a mixed salad, the standard one on every menu: lettuce,
tomato, onion, tuna, olives. *De primero, ensalada* — salad to start.

**Ensaladilla** is not a small salad. *La ensaladilla rusa* is a cold dish of
potato, carrot, tuna and mayonnaise, and it is a fixture in bars.

## Grammar Lens: ordering a course
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C456-CUENTA-01, ES-LEX-VERDURA] -->

A set menu comes in courses, and Spanish names them with **de**:

| Spanish | what it covers |
|---|---|
| *de primero* | for a first course |
| *de segundo* | for a main |
| *de postre* | for pudding |

*De primero, una ensalada* is a complete order. No verb is needed, and adding
*quiero* makes it heavier rather than politer — the waiter has asked what you
are having for each course, and the phrase answers exactly that.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C456-CUENTA-01] -->

**Ensalada** is *en-* plus *sal*: a thing that has been **salted**.

It came from Catalan or Occitan and reached English as **salad** through
French, wearing the salt down to nothing on the way. Spanish kept it visible,
which is the whole point of stopping here — the dish is named after the
dressing, not after the leaves.

The same **sal** is in **la salsa**, a sauce, and in **el salario** — Latin
*salārium* is built on *sal*, and that much is solid. The famous story
attached to it is not: no Roman source says soldiers were paid in salt, and
the salt lesson has already warned you that the tale is an eighteenth-century
invention. A salad, a sauce and a salary share a root; they do not share that
anecdote.
