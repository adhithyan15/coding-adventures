Spanish, chapter 203: Fueron --- They Were, and They Went.
1 lesson. All 1 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 1.
fueron — they were, and they went — one form for two verbs, refusing the ending everything else takes.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Fui — how many verbs does it belong to? (Two: ser and ir.) So fui is both I was and I went.
[pause 1 second]
The plural keeps that bargain, and breaks a different one.

Grammar Lens: the form that refuses the ending.
fueron — they were and they went.
Every other past plural you have takes -aron or -ieron. This one takes neither:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
tener, tuv- + -ieron becomes tuvieron.
ser / ir, — becomes fueron.
Not fuieron. The stem simply becomes fue- and the -ron is stuck on.
[pause 2 seconds]
And fuimos is we were and we went, with the same double duty.

The word, taken apart.
Spanish borrowed its whole past for to be from a different verb.
Latin fuī and fuērunt are the past of a root meaning to grow, to become — not of esse, to be. When a verb is used constantly its past tends to wear away, and languages patch the hole from whatever is nearby.
[pause 2 seconds]
English did exactly the same thing. Was and were do not come from be either; they come from a root meaning to dwell, to remain. Two languages, the same hole, the same repair.

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 2 seconds]
Which two verbs does fueron serve? (Ser and ir.)
[pause 2 seconds]
And why is the past of to be built from another verb entirely? (Because a constantly used past wears away, and the hole gets patched — in Spanish and in English both.)
