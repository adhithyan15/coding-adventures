Latin, chapter 303: Fūrtum.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
fūrtum, fūrtī — a theft.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to betray, then the Latin for to open up.

You'll want to know: fūrtum, fūrtī.
fūrtum, fūrtī — "a theft".
Fūrtum in balneō factum est. A theft was committed at the baths.
Neuter, built on fūr, 'thief', which is taught. The ablative fūrtō means 'by stealth'.

The word, taken apart.
fūrtum is built on fūr, "thief" (EtymDB), which is inherited from Proto-Italic fōr.

Lesson 2 of 5.
latrōcinium, latrōciniī — highway robbery, banditry.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]

You'll want to know: latrōcinium, latrōciniī.
latrōcinium, latrōciniī — "highway robbery, banditry".
In illā silvā latrōcinia crēbra erant. In that forest robberies were frequent.
Neuter, from latrō (taught), 'bandit'. Cicero calls a lawless war a latrōcinium.

The word, taken apart.
latrōcinium is built on latrō, "bandit", first "a hired soldier" (EtymDB, Lewis & Short). EtymDB traces latrō to Greek latron, "pay".

Lesson 3 of 5.
grassātor, grassātōris — a mugger, a street thug.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a theft, then the Latin for highway robbery.

You'll want to know: grassātor, grassātōris.
grassātor, grassātōris — "a mugger, a street thug".
Noctū grassātōrēs per viās errābant. At night muggers roamed the streets.
Masculine, 3rd declension, from grassor, 'to prowl'. Satirists warn of them in dark city streets.

The word, taken apart.
grassātor is built on grassor, "to go about, to prowl" (EtymDB), with the ending -tor. Lewis & Short note an older sense, "an idler".

Praedōnēs mercātōrem cēpērunt et aurum eius retinuērunt. Robbers captured a merchant and kept his gold.
Masculine, 3rd declension. At sea a praedō is a pirate.

praedō is built on praeda, "loot, booty" (EtymDB): one who takes booty.

Lesson 5 of 5.
praeda, praedae — loot, booty; prey.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a mugger, then the Latin for a robber.

You'll want to know: praeda, praedae.
praeda, praedae — "loot, booty; prey".
Latrōnēs praedam in spēluncā posuērunt. The bandits put the loot in a cave.
Feminine. Also the prey of an animal. English prey comes from it through French, and predator from its verb.

The word, taken apart.
praeda is explained by Lewis & Short as a short form of an older praehenda, "a thing seized", from prehendō, "to seize". English prey comes from it through French.
