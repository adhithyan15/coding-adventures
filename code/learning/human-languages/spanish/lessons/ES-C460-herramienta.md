## You'll want to know: la herramienta
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-C460-PEDIR-01]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C449-TALLER-04] -->

**la herramienta** — the tool.

*Una caja de herramientas* — a toolbox. *No tengo las herramientas* — I haven't
got the tools. *La herramienta adecuada* — the right tool for the job.

It covers the abstract sense as well: *una herramienta muy útil* about an app
or a method, exactly as English uses "tool".

## Grammar Lens: the h that hides a diphthong
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C460-PEDIR-01, ES-LEX-C449-TALLER-01] -->

Say it: **e-rra-MIEN-ta**. The **h** is silent, as it is anywhere in Spanish
that it stands alone, and underneath it is the **ie** you have met before:

| Latin | Spanish |
|---|---|
| ferramenta | la h**e**rramienta |
| ferrum | el h**ie**rro |

One rule governs both vowels, and it is the one you already have: a stressed
**e** breaks, an unstressed one stays flat. In *ferrum* the stress is on the
**e**, so it breaks — *h**ie**rro*. In *ferramenta* the stress has moved onto
the suffix, so the first **e** stays flat — *h**e**rra-* — and the break
happens where the stress went instead: *-m**ie**nta*, the very same *-miento*
you have in *el seguimiento*.

The **h** is written because it was once spoken. Latin *f-* weakened to a
breathy *h* in Castilian mouths, the breath faded, and the spelling stayed
behind. That *f*-to-*h* shift is one of the loudest things Spanish did to
Latin.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C460-PEDIR-01] -->

Latin **ferramenta**, "things made of iron" — the plural of *ferramentum*,
from **ferrum**, iron.

So a *herramienta* is an **iron thing**, and the name is older than any of the
tools you own. The same *ferrum* is the **Fe** on the periodic table, and it
gives Spanish *el hierro*, *el herrero* — the blacksmith — and *la herradura*,
a horseshoe.

That **f** to **h** is the signature of Spanish among the Romance languages.
Latin *facere* gave *hacer*, *filius* gave *hijo*, *ferrum* gave *hierro* —
while Italian kept *ferro* and French kept *fer*. The letter is silent now, but
it marks where an *f* used to be.
