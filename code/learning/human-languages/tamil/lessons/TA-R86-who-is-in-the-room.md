# (இவர், விடை, and the two we-words) — one idea in four places

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[TA-LEX-IVAR-EN-NANBAR-01] -->

[PAUSE 3s] Introduce the person standing beside you as your friend.

## Grammar Lens: the two words for we
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[TA-LEX-C85-NAAM-NAANGAL-01] -->

| word | who it covers |
|---|---|
| **நாம்** | me, and **you too** |
| **நாங்கள்** | me and my people, **but not you** |

English has one *we* and leaves the listener guessing. Tamil makes you choose
before you open your mouth, and the choice is audible to everyone in the room.

Getting it wrong is not a grammar slip — it either includes somebody you meant
to exclude or shuts out somebody standing right there.

## You'll want to know: இவர், and the ending that judges
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[TA-LEX-IVAR-EN-NANBAR-01, TA-PRAGMATICS-IVAR-EN-NANBAR-02] -->

**இவர் என் நண்பர்** — *this is my friend* — is three words with no verb in the
middle, the same silence the name sentence had.

What it adds is a **choice of ending**. **நண்பர்** carries respect; a plainer
ending would not. Introducing someone in Tamil therefore states your regard for
them out loud, in front of them, whether or not you meant to say anything about
it.

## You'll want to know: விடை
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[TA-LEX-VIDAI-01] -->

**விடை** is *leave to depart* — a noun for the act of taking your leave, sharing
a root with **வீடு**, the house.

Set it beside the farewells: the everyday one promises a return, and this word
names the departure itself. Tamil has both, and it reserves the naming one for
the heavier moment.
