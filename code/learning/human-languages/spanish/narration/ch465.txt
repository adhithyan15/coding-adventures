Spanish, chapter 465: El cuaderno de campo.
6 lessons. You can do the first 3 of them in the car; after that you will want to have stopped.


Lesson 1 of 6.
el cuaderno — the notebook.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say el libro and el papel. One is bound and finished; the other is loose and blank. The word coming next sits between them.

You'll want to know: el cuaderno.
el cuaderno — the notebook, the exercise book.
Traiga un cuaderno y un bolígrafo.
Un cuaderno de espiral — a spiral notebook. El cuaderno de campo — the field notebook, the one a person carries to write down what they see outside.
A libro is written for you. A cuaderno is waiting for you to write in it. That is the whole difference, and it is why a school gives out cuadernos and a library lends libros.

The word, taken apart.
There is a number hiding in this word, and it is four.
Latin quaterni meant four at a time, off quattuor, four — the same four you can already count to. A quaternus was a sheet of parchment folded once to make four pages, and a stack of those was the cheapest book anybody could make. The object was named after how many pages one fold produced.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
Latin: quattuor. Spanish: cuatro.
Latin: quaternus. Spanish: el cuaderno.
English took the same object and kept the count plainer: a quire is still a gathering of folded sheets, and it is quaternus worn down through French.
So the notebook in a bag and the number four are the same word, separated by the one thing nobody thinks about when buying one — how it was folded.

Grammar Lens: the ua that does not move.
Spanish has taught you to watch stressed vowels, because those are the ones that break. This family shows the other half of that rule.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
word: cua-tro. stress falls on: the ua.
word: cua-der-no. stress falls on: the -der-.
The ua sits still in both. It is not a broken vowel waiting to happen — it came straight from the Latin qua- and stayed. The lesson is that the stress test tells you where to look, and looking sometimes tells you nothing is happening. A vowel is only a candidate for breaking if it is stressed and came from a short Latin e or o; this one is neither.

Lesson 2 of 6.
anotar — to write it down.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say apuntar and la nota. You already have a verb for writing something down. A second one is about to arrive, and the two are not interchangeable.

You'll want to know: anotar.
anotar — to note down, to record in writing.
Anote la temperatura cada mañana en el cuaderno.
Anotar los datos — to record the figures. Anotar un gol — to score a goal, because the scoreboard is a record too.

Grammar Lens: anotar is not apuntar.
Both put words on paper, and the difference is what the writing is for.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
what you are doing: jotting so you do not forget. the verb: apuntar.
what you are doing: entering it into a record. the verb: anotar.
Apunta mi número is a scrap of paper in a pocket. Anote su número en el registro is a line in a book that will be read later by somebody else.
The etymologies say the same thing, which is rare enough to be worth noticing. Apuntar is punctum, a point — the tip of a pen touching down, one dot, gone. Anotar is nota, a mark that was made to be read. One is the act of writing; the other is the thing written.
So a doctor apunta while you talk and anota in your file afterwards. Where Spanish gives you two verbs, it is usually because Spanish thinks the two acts are different.

The word, taken apart.
a- plus nota, the mark you already have from la nota.
Latin nota was a sign that stood for something — a letter, a brand, a mark in the margin. From there it went two ways in English at once: a note you write, and to notice, which is to take a mark of something without writing it at all.
You were given notar alongside la nota — to notice, to catch the mark. That is Spanish having taken the second road. Anotar is the first road, with a- on the front to put it back on paper.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
Spanish: notar. what it does: to notice it.
Spanish: anotar. what it does: to write it down.
One root, one prefix, and the prefix is the whole difference between seeing something and keeping it.
Anotado is the participle, and las anotaciones are the notes themselves — the -ción ending doing the job it has done since you first met it.

Lesson 3 of 6.
la temperatura — the temperature.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say el calor and el frío. You have the two ends. Here is the word for the scale that runs between them.

You'll want to know: la temperatura.
la temperatura — the temperature.
Anote la temperatura a las ocho y a las ocho de la tarde.
Tomar la temperatura — to take somebody's temperature. Tener temperatura is to be running a fever, with no number said out loud.
Note what calor and frío cannot do: they tell you which way, never how much. Hace calor is a judgement. La temperatura is a measurement, and the whole reason a notebook wants one is that a measurement can be compared with yesterday's.

The word, taken apart.
Latin temperare did not mean heat at all. It meant to mix in the right proportion — to hold two things in balance so that neither wins.
Follow that one idea and a very odd family falls into line:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
English: to temper steel. what is being balanced: heat against hardness.
English: a temperate climate. what is being balanced: summer against winter.
A person's temper is the mix of their humours, and losing it is the mix coming apart. Tempera paint is pigment held in the right proportion of egg. And a temperature is the point a mixture has reached on the scale between hot and cold.
Spanish keeps the verb as templar, to temper or to tune an instrument, and templado is the word for weather that is neither one thing nor the other.

Grammar Lens: -ura on an adjective.
La factura gave you -ura as the thing an action produces, with a verb hiding inside the noun. Temperatura is built the same way and on the same instruction: the verb inside it is temperare, and what the action produces is a reading.
The ending has a second family, though, and it does not take verbs at all:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
base: alto. noun: la altura.
base: ancho. noun: la anchura.
Those sit on adjectives, and they name how much of the quality there is — altura is how tall, anchura is how wide. So when you meet a new -ura noun, look for a verb first, as factura told you to, and look for an adjective second.

Lesson 4 of 6.
la lluvia — the rain.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, because the lesson points at something written down. You can listen to all of it now and come back to the parts that need looking at once you have stopped.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say llueve and el llanto. Both begin with the sound this word begins with, and both have already told you why.

You'll want to know: la lluvia.
la lluvia — the rain.
Lluvia en los últimos tres días: 12 mm.
Lluvia fuerte — heavy rain. Un día de lluvia — a rainy day. La lluvia ácida — acid rain.

Grammar Lens: the verb and the noun do different work.
You already have llueve, and it refuses a subject: there is nobody raining. La lluvia is what you reach for the moment rain has to be counted, described or put in a column.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
what you are saying: it is raining now. the word: llueve.
what you are saying: how much rain there was. the word: la lluvia.
Llueve mucho is a report from the window. Hubo mucha lluvia is a line in a notebook. A verb with no subject cannot be measured; a noun can, which is why the rainfall column is headed lluvia and never llueve.

The word, taken apart.
This one you have been handed twice already, and it is worth saying so.
When you learned llueve, you were told it comes from Latin pluere, and that Latin's cl-, fl- and pl- all became Spanish ll-. Then, under el llanto, the rule was put to work on a list — and la lluvia was on that list, sitting there as the example beside lleno and llorar.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
Latin: pluvia. Spanish: la lluvia.
Latin: plenus. Spanish: lleno.
So nothing here is new except the use. Latin pluvia is the rain itself, pluere is the raining, and Spanish kept both with the same opening consonant shifted the same way. English borrowed the Latin form untouched and so kept the pl-: pluvial, and the pluviometer that measures exactly what this chapter's notebook is for.
That is what a corpus is for. A rule you were given at the start turns up later holding a word you now need, and the word costs you nothing because the rule already paid for it.

Lesson 5 of 6.
(el cuaderno, anotar, la temperatura, la lluvia) — from cold.

Warm-up.
[pause 3 seconds]
Four words, no prompts. The book you write in, the verb for writing into it, the number you write, and the weather you write it about.

You'll want to know: the four, cold.
[a table of 4 rows, read aloud]
English: the notebook. Spanish: el cuaderno.
English: to note down. Spanish: anotar.
English: the temperature. Spanish: la temperatura.
English: the rain. Spanish: la lluvia.

Grammar Lens: an impression is not a record.
This chapter is about the moment a thing stops being an impression and becomes a record.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
impression: hace calor. record: la temperatura: 24.
impression: llueve. record: lluvia: 12 mm.
Hace calor and llueve are both true and neither can be compared with yesterday. The nouns can. That is why the chapter's verb is anotar rather than apuntar: a note you jot is for you, and a note you enter is for whoever reads the book next.

The words, taken apart.
Three of the four hide something that is not about writing or weather at all.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
word: el cuaderno. what is underneath: four — quattuor, four pages from one fold.
word: la temperatura. what is underneath: mixing — temperare, to hold in proportion.
Anotar is the plain one: nota, a mark, with a- in front. And la lluvia you were given twice before you needed it — once as pluere under llueve, once as the worked example under el llanto.

Lesson 6 of 6.
(el cuaderno de campo) — read it, then fill in a day.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say el cuaderno and anotar. Somebody else will read what you write in it, which is the whole reason the instructions exist.

Reading: the notebook.
Taped inside the front cover of a shared notebook in a community garden:
CUADERNO DE CAMPO — CÓMO USARLO Anote la temperatura dos veces al día: a las ocho de la mañana y a las ocho de la tarde. Si ha llovido, anote también la lluvia en milímetros. Si no ha llovido, escriba un cero. No deje la casilla en blanco. Una línea por día. Si olvida un día, déjelo vacío y siga: es peor anotar de memoria que no anotar nada.

Grammar Lens: a zero is not a blank.
Four instructions, and the last one is the interesting one.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
the instruction: anote la temperatura. what it wants: twice a day, at fixed hours.
the instruction: escriba un cero. what it wants: a zero, not an empty box.
The notice draws a line between un cero and la casilla en blanco. A zero says it did not rain. A blank says nobody looked. Those are different facts, and a record that cannot tell them apart is worth less than one that can — which is why the last line would rather you left a gap than filled it in from memory.

Grammar Lens: anote, not anota.
Every instruction here is usted: anote, escriba, deje, siga. A notice does not know who will read it, so it takes the polite form by default.
[a table of 1 row, read aloud]
to one person you know: anota. on a notice: anote.
The notices in this book have been doing it all along. What is worth adding is that anote is the same form as the subjunctive — the polite command borrows it — which is why reduzca on a road sign looked the way it did.

Guided Practice.
[pause 3 seconds]
Yesterday it rained in the morning and the afternoon was warm. Fill in the line out loud: say the date, the two temperatures, and the rain.
[pause 3 seconds]
Now say what you would write if it had not rained at all — and say why that is not the same as leaving it blank.

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
The field notebook? (El cuaderno de campo.) The polite command to note something? (Anote.) What goes in the box on a dry day? (Un cero — never a blank.)
