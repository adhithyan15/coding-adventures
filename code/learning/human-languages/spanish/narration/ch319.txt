Spanish, chapter 319: Review --- Second Looks.
3 lessons. All 3 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 3.
Review — what the surface does not tell you.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
One word to start. What does pronto mean on its own? (Soon — and "ready", the sense English kept in prompt.)

What you've built.
Four things you met separately, gathered because they share a shape: in each one, the part you can see is not the part you should trust.
[pause 2 seconds]
First. Pronto looks like a word about right now. It is about readiness, from promptus, "brought forth" — and "soon" is where readiness ended up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Second. Lo siento and perdón both come out as English "sorry". The English does not divide them; the Latin does. One names your feeling, the other asks for their pardon.
[pause 2 seconds]
Third. El agua looks masculine and is not. The article moved for sound; the object pronoun did not, and says la.
[pause 2 seconds]
Fourth. The h on hermano looks like the h on hijo. It is not the same letter's history: one stood where a Latin f was breathed aloud for centuries, the other was put on the page long after the sound had gone.

Grammar Lens: the mark that was designed, not inherited.
The ¿ belongs on that list too, from the other side.
[pause 2 seconds]
It is not a survival. It was decided — the Spanish Royal Academy made it official in 1754, because Spanish leaves the words of a question where they were and something had to warn the reader in advance.
[pause 2 seconds]
So the page is carrying a job that English hands to word order. Four inherited surfaces that mislead, and one deliberate surface that helps: worth holding together, because reading a language well is mostly knowing which surfaces to believe.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: pronto, then English prompt — readiness in both]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: el agua, then la bebo]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: hermano, and remember the letter you are not pronouncing]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[repeat that twice]
[question — say your answer, then pause 10 seconds]
Which of the two sorries asks the other person for something?
[question — say your answer, then pause 14 seconds]
El agua takes el. Which pronoun replaces it, and what does that prove?
[question — say your answer, then pause 14 seconds]
Hijo and hermano both start with a silent h. Which one was once spoken aloud?
[question — say your answer, then pause 12 seconds]
Was the opening mark inherited from Latin, or decided by somebody?

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Which is the honest witness to a noun's gender — the article or the object pronoun? (The pronoun.) Which of Spanish's two sorries is about fault and getting past people? (Perdón.) When was ¿ made official? (1754.) Was hermano's h ever a sound? (No.) And what does pronto mean on its own? (Soon, from "ready".)


Lesson 2 of 3.
Review — the five words of a leaving.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Two to start. Say "the street." (La calle.) Say "the corner." (La esquina.)

What you've built.
Say the five in order and hear the distance grow at every step: la calle, la esquina, la huella, el puerto, el faro — the street, its corner, the mark left in the dirt, the water's edge, and the light still visible when the town is not.
[pause 2 seconds]
Now the part worth a second visit. Not one of the five was built for this list. Calle was a cattle track, callis. Esquina is a shinbone, Germanic skina. Huella comes from a day spent treading wool, fullāre. Puerto is the Roman harbour, portus, twin to the puerta on land. And faro was an island, Pharos, with a fire on top of a tower.
[pause 2 seconds]
Five ordinary nouns out of five unrelated quarries. That is the ratio you should expect from here on.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: all five in order — la calle, la esquina, la huella, el puerto, el faro]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: la puerta, then el puerto — the gate on land, the gate off the sea]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[repeat that twice]
[question — say your answer, then pause 18 seconds]
Name the five words of the leaving, in order from the street outwards.
[question — say your answer, then pause 10 seconds]
Which body part is hiding inside esquina?
[question — say your answer, then pause 12 seconds]
El faro was a place before it was a thing. Which place?

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
What was fullāre? (Treading cloth in a vat — the trampling that gave huella its footprint.) What does ob portum describe? (A wind standing toward harbour — an opportunity.) And what is a faro in a car park today? (A headlight.)


Lesson 3 of 3.
Review — the harbour, the light, and see you soon.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
The last two nouns of the run. Say "the harbour." (El puerto.) Say "the lighthouse." (El faro.)

What you've built.
A leaving needs a place and a time, and you have both.
[pause 2 seconds]
The place: el puerto, the gate off the sea, and el faro, the light standing at its mouth. The time: hasta pronto — hasta, "until", and pronto, "soon".
[pause 2 seconds]
Say the whole thing once, slowly, as one leaving:
El puerto. El faro. Hasta pronto.
[pause 2 seconds]
Three words out of three different centuries and two different languages — a Roman harbour, a Greek island, and an Arabic preposition holding a Latin adjective. You can hear all of that now, which you could not at the start.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: el puerto, el faro, hasta pronto]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[repeat that twice]
[question — say your answer, then pause 10 seconds]
Name the harbour and the lighthouse in Spanish, in that order.
[question — say your answer, then pause 14 seconds]
Say see you soon, and give the two words it is made of.

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Which of the two is the light, and which is the water? (El faro; el puerto.) And what does pronto promise? (Soon — you will be back shortly.)
