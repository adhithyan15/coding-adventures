# seit and vor — since when, and how long ago

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GE-LEX-LERNEN-01, GE-LEX-DEUTSCH-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say "I am learning German". (*Ich lerne Deutsch.*) Now we add
how long.

## Grammar Lens: seit, with the present
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[GE-GRAMMAR-SEIT-PRAESENS-01]; assesses=[GE-LEX-JAHR-01, GE-LEX-MONAT-01, GE-LEX-STUNDE-01, GE-LEX-WANN-01] -->

English says "I **have been** learning German for two years". German keeps
the present, because the learning is still going on:

> *Ich lerne* **seit** *zwei Jahren Deutsch.*

**seit** means "since, for", and it takes the dative: *seit einem Jahr*,
*seit einer Stunde*, and in the plural an extra *-n*: *seit zwei Jahren*,
*seit drei Monaten*.

The question is *seit wann?*, since when: *Seit wann lernst du Deutsch?*

The trap for English speakers is the tense. *Ich habe seit zwei Jahren
Deutsch gelernt* forces the English shape onto German, and for something
still going on it sounds wrong. If it is still true, keep the present.

## Why it's said this way: vor for ago
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[GE-GRAMMAR-VOR-AGO-02]; assesses=[GE-GRAMMAR-ERZAEHL-PRAETERITUM-01, GE-GRAMMAR-SEIN-ICH-WAR-01, GE-GRAMMAR-DANN-VERB-ZWEI-02] -->

For something over and done, German says **vor**, also with the dative,
and it stands **before** the time, where English "ago" comes after:

> *Vor zwei Jahren war ich in Berlin.* — Two years ago I was in Berlin.

The time phrase takes the first place, so the verb comes next: *vor zwei
Jahren* **war** *ich*.

And for a stretch of time that is finished, no little word is needed at
all: *Ich war drei Monate in Berlin*, I was in Berlin for three months.

So: still going on, *seit* and the present. Over, *vor* for when, or the
bare time for how long.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GE-GRAMMAR-SEIT-PRAESENS-01] -->

**seit** ← Middle High German *sīt* (EtymDB 2.1). Further back it is
usually traced to an Old High German word meaning "later", and linked with
the first part of English *since*; EtymDB does not record those steps.
**vor** is usually traced to Old High German *fora*, a cousin of English
*fore* in *before*; EtymDB's entry for *vor* does not settle it, so that
too is given as the usual account.
