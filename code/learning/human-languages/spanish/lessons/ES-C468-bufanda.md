## You'll want to know: la bufanda
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-C468-ABRIGO-04]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C468-ABRIGO-03] -->

**la bufanda** — the scarf, the winter one you wind round your neck.

> Ponte la **bufanda**, que estamos a dos grados.

That **que** is not the joining *que* you have. It is the spoken shorthand for
*porque* — *put your scarf on, because it's two degrees* — and Spanish leans
on it constantly for reasons.

A *bufanda* is the one you put on because it is cold. **Un fular** is the
light draped one worn for how it looks; **un pañuelo** is usually a
handkerchief and only sometimes a scarf. A *bufanda* can be silk, so the
material is not the test — the reason is.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C468-ABRIGO-04, ES-LEX-C450-SUELO-01] -->

Every other word in this chapter came with a story. This one comes with a
shrug, and you have met that shrug before.

*La bufanda* has no settled origin. The usual suggestion is French
**bouffante**, puffed out, on *bouffer*, to puff — which fits a scarf's bulk
around the throat. Another line points at Italian *buffa*, a visor or a
covering for the face. The dictionaries hedge, and so does this lesson.

*El patio* is where this book set the rule out: an origin left open is a
claim, not an oversight, and saying so is more useful than picking the
prettier story. *El andén*, *tirar* and *el gorro* carry the same mark, and
*el gorro* is the nearest neighbour — winter headwear whose origin the Academy
also marks uncertain.

*La bufanda* is the fifth. Read the prose rather than the front matter for
this: plenty of lessons leave the root list empty and still tell you the whole
story. What is unusual here is that there is no story to tell.
