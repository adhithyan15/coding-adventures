# కొడుకు (koḍuku) — "son," and the unexpected word it shares a root with

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[TE-LEX-C35-KUTUMBAM-01, TE-LEX-C35-SNEHITUDU-01, TE-ETYMON-C19-VAYASU-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] *Kuṭumbaṁ*, family; *snēhituḍu*, friend. Now the two words for
your own children — and Telugu does not build them the tidy way you might
expect.

## You'll want to know: కొడుకు
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[TE-LEX-C36-KODUKU-01]; assesses=[TE-GRAMMAR-C19-VAYASU-02] -->

> **కొడుకు** — *koḍuku* — **son**

> **వాడి కొడుకు వయసెంత?** — *vāḍi koḍuku vayasenta?* — "How old is his
> son?" — the verbless age question you already know, asked about someone
> else's child.

## The word, taken apart - the root that points sideways, not across
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[TE-ETYMON-C36-KODUKU-02]; assesses=[] -->

**కొడుకు** is inherited from Proto-Dravidian **\*kōẓ-**, "young, tender." You
might expect "daughter," Telugu's other child-word, to share that same root
with a different ending — that is exactly the shape Kannada's *magu/maga/
magalu* takes. Telugu does not do that. The very same **\*kōẓ-** root instead
gives Telugu **కోడలు** (*kōḍalu*), "**daughter-in-law**" — the woman a son
marries, not the daughter a parent has. So *koḍuku* and *kōḍalu* are true
cognates, one root apart; *koḍuku* and *kūturu* — son and daughter — are not
related to each other at all. The next lesson takes up *kūturu*'s own,
separate root.
