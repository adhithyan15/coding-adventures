French, chapter 258: The Scene — c'était, il y avait, il faisait froid.
6 lessons. All 6 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 6.
je parlais — the past that is still running.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say "I spoke", with avoir and the participle. (J'ai parlé.)
[pause 1 second]
That past has an edge: it happened, and it finished. French has a second past with no edge at all, and every story you tell from here on needs both of them.

Grammar Lens: one stem, one set of endings.
Start from the nous form of the present and take off its -ons. What is left is the stem. Then add -ais for "I":
nous parl-ons, je parlais — I was speaking, I used to speak nous finiss-ons, je finissais — I was finishing nous pren-ons, je prenais — I was taking.
The other persons keep that stem and change only the ending: je parlais, tu parlais, il parlait, nous parlions, vous parliez, ils parlaient.
Two things to hear. Four of those six are one sound: parlais, parlais, parlait and parlaient all end in the same open è, so the pronoun in front is what tells a listener who was speaking. And nous parlions is one small i away from the present nous parlons. Listen for that i.
This past is the imparfait, and almost every verb follows this recipe. The one that refuses it comes next.
What it means is "was ‑ing", or "used to": j'habitais à Paris, I lived in Paris, back then, with no start and no finish in view.

The word, taken apart: where -ais came from.
Latin already had this past, and it marked it with a b: habēbat, "he was having", from habēre, the Latin verb that gave avoir (EtymDB 2.1). In French the b wore away entirely. You met one of its forms in the chapter on être: j'étais goes back to stābam, "I was standing".
Older French spelled the ending -oit, il parloit, and the spelling -ais settled only in the nineteenth century, long after the sound had changed. Neither EtymDB nor Lewis & Short records endings this late, so that history is the usual account in histories of French, given here as one.

Lesson 2 of 6.
c'était, il y avait — the words a scene is made of.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Build "I was speaking". (Je parlais.) Say "it's good". (C'est bon.) Then "there is bread". (Il y a du pain.)
[pause 1 second]
Hold on to all three. This lesson moves the last two into the past.

You'll want to know: c'était.
One verb refuses last lesson's recipe: être. Its nous form, nous sommes, gives nothing to build on, so it has a stem of its own, ét-, with the same endings: j'étais, tu étais, il était, nous étions, vous étiez, ils étaient.
The form you will use most is the one inside c'est:
C'était bon. — It was good. C'était samedi. — It was Saturday.
C'est is now; c'était is how things were, all through some stretch of the past.
avoir does follow the recipe: nous avons gives j'avais. Age is the clearest case. J'ai vingt ans is now; j'avais vingt ans is the age you were, back then.

You'll want to know: il y avait.
You have il y a, "there is" and "there are". Its past is il y avait:
Il y avait beaucoup de monde. — There were a lot of people. Il n'y avait pas de pain. — There was no bread.
Like il y a, it does not change for a plural: one avait for one thing or for a hundred. And after the negative, pas de stays exactly as it was in the present.

The phrase, taken apart.
était goes back to Latin stābat, "he was standing", the standing verb that lent être every form beginning ét-. avait goes back to habēbat, "he was having", the past of habēre, the Latin verb that gave avoir (EtymDB 2.1). In both, the Latin b wore away.
The little y of il y a is inherited from Latin ibī, "there" (EtymDB 2.1; Lewis & Short, under ibi). So il y avait is, word for word, "it had there".

Lesson 3 of 6.
il faisait froid — the weather, made in the past.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say "there were a lot of people", the way last lesson built it. (Il y avait beaucoup de monde.) Then "it's cold", today. (Il fait froid.)

You'll want to know: il faisait froid, il faisait chaud.
English says the weather was cold. French says it made cold, and you have that already: il fait froid. Move it into the past with the recipe. Nous faisons gives the stem fais-:
Il faisait froid. — It was cold. Il faisait chaud. — It was hot.
Listen to the first syllable. The ai of faisait is said like the short e of le: "fe-zè". It is the one surprise in the whole recipe.
Rain has a verb of its own. You have il pleut, "it's raining", and its past is il pleuvait, "it was raining".
Keep c'était and était for things. Le café était froid — the coffee was cold — is about the coffee. Il faisait froid is about the whole day. Put three of these together and you have a morning before anything has happened in it:
Il faisait froid et il pleuvait. Il n'y avait pas de soleil.
In Québec, relaxed speech has its own word for a biting cold: il faisait frette. In a letter, everyone writes froid.

The phrase, taken apart.
faire is inherited from Latin facere, "to make, to do" (EtymDB 2.1), and faisait goes back to its past faciēbat, "he was making" (Lewis & Short, under facio). English fact and factory share it.
froid is inherited, through Old French froit, from Latin frīgidus, "cold" (EtymDB 2.1), which English borrowed whole as frigid. chaud goes back to Old French chaut (EtymDB 2.1), usually traced to Latin calidus, "warm, hot" (Lewis & Short).

Lesson 4 of 6.
je dormais / j'ai dormi — the same sleep, told two ways.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Paint a cold morning in two short sentences. (Il faisait froid. Il n'y avait pas de soleil.)
[pause 1 second]
Nothing has happened in it yet. This lesson is about what happens.

Grammar Lens: the scene and the events.
Take one fact, that you slept, and let the tense decide what kind of fact it is:
Je dormais. — I was sleeping. (no edge: what was going on) J'ai dormi. — I slept. (an edge: a thing that happened and ended).
Neither is more correct. A told story needs both, in a division of labour:
The imparfait paints the scene: what was going on, what things were like. The passé composé drops the events into it: what happened.
Il faisait froid. Je dormais. Ma mère a téléphoné. — It was cold. I was asleep. My mother phoned.
English has no separate ending for this. It leans on was ‑ing and used to and lets the context carry the rest. French settles it in the verb.

Grammar Lens: what used to happen.
A habit is scene too. It went on, again and again, with no single edge:
Autrefois, je dormais beaucoup. — In the old days, I used to sleep a lot.
So the imparfait answers two questions: what was going on at that moment, and what used to happen. The passé composé answers a third: what happened, once, and was done.

The word, taken apart: why "imparfait".
imparfait is inherited from Latin imperfectus, "unfinished, incomplete" (EtymDB 2.1; Lewis & Short): in-, "not", plus perfectus, "carried all the way through", which is itself per-, "through", plus facere, "to make". That facere is the one inside last lesson's faisait.
So the name describes the job. The imparfait is the not-carried-through past: unfinished in time, with no edge on it.

[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Lesson 5 of 6.
quand, pendant que — the scene, and what cut into it.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say "I was sleeping", then "I slept". (Je dormais, then j'ai dormi.)
[pause 1 second]
This lesson joins a scene and an event into one sentence.

Grammar Lens: quand, where the event comes in.
You have quand, "when". It puts a scene and an event into one sentence:
Je lisais quand ma mère a téléphoné. — I was reading when my mother phoned. J'attendais le train quand il a commencé à pleuvoir. — I was waiting for the train when it started to rain.
The first half is the scene, still running, in the imparfait. The quand half is the event that cut into it, in the passé composé. Turn the sentence round and the tenses keep their jobs: Quand ma mère a téléphoné, je lisais.
A quand can hold a scene too: Quand j'avais vingt ans, j'habitais à Paris. A stretch of life is not an event, so both halves are scene.

Grammar Lens: pendant que, two things at once.
pendant que means "while". It holds a scene open so that something else can happen inside it:
Pendant que je lisais, mon frère dormait. — While I was reading, my brother was asleep. Pendant que j'attendais le train, j'ai téléphoné à ma mère. — While I was waiting for the train, I phoned my mother.
Two scenes side by side, then one event inside a scene.
French has one more way to hold a moment still: être en train de, "to be in the middle of". J'étais en train de lire quand ma mère a téléphoné.

The word, taken apart.
pendant comes from pendre, "to hang", which is inherited from Latin pendēre, "to hang, to be suspended" (EtymDB 2.1; Lewis & Short, under pendeo). Pendant que je lisais is, at root, "while my reading was still hanging": open, not yet settled. English pending has the same picture.
quand is inherited from Latin quandō, "when" (EtymDB 2.1), and the d it kept in spelling is the Latin one.

Lesson 6 of 6.
Review — the scene, and what cut into it.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Build "I was reading" from lire. (Je lisais.) Then say "I had a dog". (J'avais un chien.)

What you've built.
Five lessons, and every piece of a scene is in your hands. The recipe, the nous stem plus -ais, on almost any verb: je parlais, je finissais, je prenais. The one verb that refuses it, être, with c'était. The scene-setter il y avait. The weather, made rather than being: il faisait froid. And the division of labour: the imparfait paints, the passé composé drops the events in.
[pause 2 seconds]
The joints between the two are quand and pendant que.
Je lisais quand ma mère a téléphoné. J'attendais le train quand il a commencé à pleuvoir.
The second one is worth hearing twice. The rain is an event here, because it began: il a commencé. Once it is falling, it is scene again: il pleuvait.
