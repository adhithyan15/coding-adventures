Lesson 1 of 5.
fūr, fūris — a thief.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for the public baths, then the Latin for an amphitheatre.

You'll want to know: fūr, fūris.
fūr, fūris — "a thief".

The word, taken apart.
fūr is inherited, usually traced to Proto-Indo-European bhōr, "thief" (EtymDB 2.1), from the root of ferō, "to carry"; Greek phōr, "thief", is a cousin (Lewis & Short).

Lesson 2 of 5.
latrō, latrōnis — a robber.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for an amphitheatre, then the Latin for a thief.

You'll want to know: latrō, latrōnis.
latrō, latrōnis — "a robber".

The word, taken apart.
latrō was probably borrowed from Greek: latron, "pay, hire" (EtymDB 2.1), and latris, "a hired servant" (Lewis & Short). A latrō was first a hired soldier, then a robber.

Lesson 3 of 5.

cōnsul, cōnsulis — "a consul".

The word, taken apart.
cōnsul belongs with cōnsulō, "to consult, to deliberate" (EtymDB 2.1); Old Latin wrote it consol. Where the root comes from is uncertain.

Lesson 4 of 5.
imperātor, imperātōris — a general, an emperor.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a robber, then the Latin for a consul.

You'll want to know: imperātor, imperātōris.
imperātor, imperātōris — "a general, an emperor".

The word, taken apart.
imperātor is built on imperō, "to command" (EtymDB 2.1), itself in, "in, upon", with parō, "to prepare, to order", with -tor, "one who does it". English emperor comes from it through French.
