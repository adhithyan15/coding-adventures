# por favor — "please," or literally "for a favor"

## Warm-up

[PAUSE 2s] The natural partner to *gracias*. English "please" is a strange,
opaque little word; Spanish "please" is transparent — it says exactly what
it's asking.

## You'll want to know first

- [ES-C01-L05 (gracias)](./ES-C01-L05-gracias.md) — the courtesy pair;
  *gracias* thanks, *por favor* requests.

## Sounds you'll need

- `stress-default-consonant` — *favor* ends in *r* (a consonant that isn't
  *n/s*), so the stress lands on the **last** syllable: *fa-VOR*.

## The word, taken apart

*por favor* is two words: "**for** favor."

**por** — for, by, through. Root: Latin **pro** ("for, on behalf of"). You
own *pro* directly in English: **pro**-something (in favor of it), a **pro**
(professional), **pro**ponent, and buried inside *purpose*, *portray*. It's
one of the great workhorse prepositions — hold onto it, it comes back
constantly.

**favor** — favor. Root: Latin **favor**, from **favere** ("to be kindly
disposed, to protect"). English took it almost unchanged:

- **favor**able, **favor**ite, **favor**itism.
- and *favor* itself — a kind act done for someone.

So *por favor* = "for a favor" — you're framing your request as asking a
small kindness. Literally: "if you'd do me the favor."

## Why it's said this way

English "please" is a shortened "if you please" / "may it please you" — the
politeness is there but hidden, worn smooth until nobody hears it anymore.
Spanish left its politeness **visible**: *por favor* still transparently says
"as a favor to me." Same instinct as the greeting-blessings — Spanish
courtesy tends to keep the human gesture on the surface (a favor, a blessing,
a grace) rather than compressing it into an opaque particle.

You can stack it with what you learned: *un café, por favor* ("a coffee,
please"), *gracias* when it arrives.
