## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-C423-CHAR-06]; assesses=[] -->

**sociable** — "sociable, outgoing." Latin **socius**, "a companion, an ally, a
sharer," plus **-able**, "able to be, inclined to."

*Socius* is one of those roots that turns out to be everywhere:

| word | the companion in it |
|---|---|
| **society** | the whole body of companions |
| **associate** | to make someone a companion |
| **social** | to do with companions |

And then there is the one nobody expects. English *association football* was
shortened by Oxford students, who liked adding **-er** to clipped words, into
**soccer** — from *assoc*. The most ordinary word in world sport has *socius*
sitting inside it, meaning nothing more than *companion*.

Spanish has the family too: *la sociedad*, *social*, *el socio* — which is a
business partner or a club member, and is *socius* almost unchanged after two
thousand years.

## What you've built
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C423-CHAR-06, ES-LEX-C423-CHAR-02, ES-LEX-C423-CHAR-05] -->

The six character words of this chapter sort into three agreement shapes, and
now you have seen all three:

| shape | example | feminine |
|---|---|---|
| **-o** | tímido | tímida |
| **-e** | inteligente | inteligente |
| **-able** | sociable | sociable |

**-able** behaves like **-e**: one form for everyone. A *hombre sociable* and a
*mujer sociable*, spelled identically.
