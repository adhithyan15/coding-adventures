Lesson 1 of 5.
اس کا (is kā) — his, her.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, because the lesson points at something written down. You can listen to all of it now and come back to the parts that need looking at once you have stopped.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
What is the oblique of یہ? Then say our room.
[your turn — recall: two chapters back, the shape a noun takes before a postposition — kamre]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

You'll want to know: اس کا.
اس کا کمرہ — is kā kamrā — his room, or her room.
ان کا کمرہ — un kā kamrā — their room, or his room, respectfully.
Urdu has no word for his and none for her. It takes the demonstrative in the oblique shape you learned two chapters ago and puts کا after it — which is why this could not be taught earlier. The third-person possessive is built on the oblique, and until the oblique existed there was nothing to build it on.

Grammar Lens: the column, finally full.
[a table of 5 rows, read aloud]
person: I. possessive: میرا. written: fused.
person: we. possessive: ہمارا. written: fused.
person: you, respectfully. possessive: آپ کا. written: apart.
person: he, she, it. possessive: اس کا (is kā). written: apart.
person: they, or he respectfully. possessive: ان کا. written: apart.
Five of the six an A1 candidate needs, and the shape of the sixth — your, to somebody close — follows the same rule.
Every one of them ends in -ا and agrees like an adjective, so اس کی ماں is his mother and اس کے کمرے میں is in his room.

Lesson 2 of 5.
اپنا (apnā) — one's own.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say his room, then our room. Then say my name.

You'll want to know: اپنا.
میں اپنے کمرے میں ہوں — maiṅ apne kamre meṅ hūṅ — I am in my (own) room.
Here is a rule English does not have and Urdu will not let you skip. When the owner is the same person as the subject of the sentence, Urdu replaces the ordinary possessive with اپنا (apnā).
So it is میرا کمرہ when somebody else is speaking about my room, and اپنا (apnā) کمرہ the moment I am the one doing something in it.

Grammar Lens: getting it wrong changes the meaning.
This is not a politeness or a nicety. The two sentences mean different things:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
وہ اپنے کمرے میں ہے means he is in his own room.
وہ اس کے کمرے میں ہے means he is in somebody else's room.
An English speaker hears he is in his room for both and has no way to tell them apart. Urdu marks the difference and requires you to.
اپنا (apnā) agrees like every other possessive: اپنا (apnā) کمرہ, اپنی ماں, اپنے کمرے میں.

Lesson 3 of 5.
ہے نا؟ (hai nā?) — isn't it?

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Ask a yes-or-no question with کیا. Then say the negator that stands in front of a refused noun.

You'll want to know: ہے نا؟.
آپ یہاں ہیں، ہے نا؟ — āp yahāṅ haiṅ, hai nā? — you are here, aren't you?
یہ آپ کا کمرہ ہے، ہے نا؟ (hai nā?) — this is your room, isn't it?
Both halves have been in this book for twenty-five chapters. ہے arrived in chapter two and نہ in chapter nineteen, and nobody has ever put them together. It is not a new word; it is an assembly.

Grammar Lens: two ways to ask, and they mean different things.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
how you ask: کیا … āp ṭhīk haiṅ? what it says: I do not know. Tell me.
how you ask: āp ṭhīk haiṅ, ہے نا؟ (hai nā?). what it says: I think so. Agree with me.
Chapter nineteen's کیا opens a genuine question. The tag makes a statement and asks the listener to confirm it, and a conversation runs on the second far more than on the first.
The reply is the jī hāṅ of chapter one. (Ṭhīk and jī stay in romanization: between them they need three letters the script ladder has not reached.)

Lesson 4 of 5.
ماں! (māṅ!) — calling somebody.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say mother. Then say the greeting from chapter one, and ask yourself who you said it to.

You'll want to know: the kin term as an address.
ماں! سلام — māṅ! salām — Mother! Hello.
This book has taught ماں since chapter seventeen as a noun — a person you can talk about. Not once has anybody been called.
That matters more in Urdu than in English. An Urdu speaker addresses almost nobody by name. A mother is ماں, an older brother is bhāī, a shopkeeper is bhāī, a stranger of your parents' age is uncle or auntie. Using a first name where a kin term is expected is the mistake, not the other way round.

Grammar Lens: what the vocative does to the noun.
Urdu kept a fragment of the old vocative case, and it is one letter's worth:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
the noun: ends in -ā. talking about: کمرہ. calling: کمرے.
the noun: plural. talking about: کمروں (before a postposition). calling: کمرو.
The call form of a plural is -و, not the -وں you learned in chapter twenty-five. The nasal drops. That is the whole difference between shouting to a group and saying something about them.
ماں does not end in -ا, so it does not change — which is why calling your mother is the easiest vocative in the language.
