# je pourrais, j'aimerais — the would of French

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[FR-GRAMMAR-MODAL-CHAIN-04, FR-LEX-JE-DEVRAIS-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Ask a stranger for a coffee. (*Je voudrais un café.*) Then say
"I should sleep". (*Je devrais dormir.*)

[PAUSE 1s] Two forms of one tense. This lesson gives it its name and its
recipe.

## Grammar Lens: the infinitive, plus the imparfait's endings
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[FR-GRAM-CONDITIONNEL-01]; assesses=[FR-GRAM-IMPARFAIT-01, FR-LEX-POUVOIR-02, FR-LEX-AIMER-09, FR-LEX-FINIR-01] -->

This is the **conditionnel**, French's "would". Keep the **whole**
infinitive and add the endings of the imparfait, **-ais** for "I":

> parler, **je parlerais** — I would speak
> finir, **je finirais** — I would finish
> prendre, **je prendrais** — I would take

A verb in *-re* lets its final *e* go first: *prendr-*, *prendrais*. The
other persons change only the ending: **je parlerais**, **tu parlerais**,
**il parlerait**, **nous parlerions**, **vous parleriez**, **ils
parleraient**. Beside the imparfait: *je parlais*, I was speaking; *je parlerais*, I
would speak. The infinitive inside it is the whole difference.

A few common verbs squeeze their stem first. You have met two of them,
*vouloir* in **je voudrais** and *devoir* in **je devrais**; the third is
*pouvoir*, **je pourrais**. *Aimer* follows the plain recipe: **j'aimerais**.

Three jobs, all softening. A **polite request**: *Vous
pourriez m'aider ?* — Could you help me? A **wish**: *J'aimerais partir en
vacances.* — I'd like to go on holiday. And **advice**: *À ta place, je
dormirais.* — In your place, I'd sleep.

## The word, taken apart: "I had to speak"
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[FR-ETYMON-CONDITIONNEL-02]; assesses=[FR-LEX-IL-Y-AVAIT-02, FR-ETYMON-IMPARFAIT-AIS-02] -->

Latin had no would-tense. Spoken Latin made one by gluing a past of
*habēre*, "to have", onto the infinitive: roughly "I had to speak", which
slid into "I would speak". French chose the **imperfect** of that verb, the
same *habēbat* that became *avait*; worn down on the end of a verb, it is
the **-ait** of *parlerait*. Italian glued on a different past, which is why
its endings do not match. Neither EtymDB nor Lewis & Short covers a change
this late; this is the usual explanation in histories of Romance, given as
that. The name **conditionnel** comes from Medieval Latin *conditiōnālis*,
built on *conditiō*, "a condition" (EtymDB 2.1).
