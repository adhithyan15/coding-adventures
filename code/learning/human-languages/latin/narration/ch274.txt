Lesson 2 of 5.
negōtium, negōtiī — business.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a granddaughter, then the Latin for leisure.

You'll want to know: negōtium, negōtiī.
negōtium, negōtiī — "business".

The word, taken apart.
negōtium is nec, "not", with ōtium, "leisure" (Lewis & Short): no leisure, so business. Festus already took it apart this way.

Lesson 3 of 5.
lucrum, lucrī — profit.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for leisure, then the Latin for business.

You'll want to know: lucrum, lucrī.
lucrum, lucrī — "profit".

The word, taken apart.
lucrum is inherited, usually traced to Proto-Indo-European leh2w-, "to take as booty, to profit" (EtymDB 2.1), with an ending for a means; German Lohn, "wages", is a cousin (Lewis & Short).

Lesson 4 of 5.
damnum, damnī — loss, damage.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for business, then the Latin for profit.

You'll want to know: damnum, damnī.
damnum, damnī — "loss, damage".

The word, taken apart.
damnum is usually traced to Proto-Indo-European dh2pnom, "an outlay, an expense" (EtymDB 2.1), the source also of Greek dapanē, "expense"; Lewis & Short still preferred a link with dare, "to give".

Lesson 5 of 5.
dēbitum, dēbitī — a debt.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for profit, then the Latin for loss.

You'll want to know: dēbitum, dēbitī.
dēbitum, dēbitī — "a debt".

The word, taken apart.
dēbitum is the past participle of dēbeō, "to owe" (EtymDB 2.1), used as a noun: what is owed. English debt comes from it through French.
