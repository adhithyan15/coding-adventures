# falava — the past that is still running

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[PT-LEX-PRETERITO-PERFEITO-02, PT-GRAMMAR-FALAR-04] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say "I spoke" the way chapter fifteen built it. (*Falei.*)

[PAUSE 1s] That past has an edge: it happened, and it finished. Portuguese has
a second past with no edge at all, and every story you tell from here on needs
both of them.

## Grammar Lens: two endings, three families
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[PT-GRAMMAR-IMPERFEITO-01]; assesses=[PT-LEX-C43-VERBOS4-03, PT-LEX-C172-VERBOS172-04, PT-GRAMMAR-MORAR-04] -->

Take the infinitive and drop its last two letters. An *-ar* verb adds **-ava**;
an *-er* or an *-ir* verb adds **-ia**:

> fal-ar, **falava** — I was speaking, I used to speak
> com-er, **comia** — I was eating, I used to eat
> part-ir, **partia** — I was leaving

The other persons keep that ending and change only what comes after it:
**falava**, **falavas**, **falava**, **falávamos**, **falavam** — I, you, he
or she, we, they. And for the other two families: **comia**, **comias**,
**comia**, **comíamos**, **comiam**.

Two things to hear. The *nós* form carries a written accent, *falávamos*,
*comíamos*, because the stress sits further back than usual. And the forms
for "I" and for "he or she" are the same word: *eu falava*, *ele falava*. When
it matters, keep the *eu*.

This is the **pretérito imperfeito**, and almost every verb follows this one
recipe. The few that refuse begin in the next lesson.

What it means is "was ‑ing", or "used to": *morava em Lisboa*, I lived in
Lisbon, back then, with no start and no finish in view. That is exactly what a
story needs for its background.

## The word, taken apart: where the v came from
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[PT-ETYMON-IMPERFEITO-BA-02]; assesses=[] -->

Latin already had this past, and it marked it with a **b**: Latin *amābat*,
"he was loving", and *habēbat*, "he was having". In an *-ar* verb that *b*
softened to a **v**, so *falava* goes back to a spoken Latin past of
*fābulārī*, "to talk", the verb that gave **falar** (EtymDB 2.1). After *e* and
*i* the *b* wore away entirely, which is why *comia* and *partia* end in a bare
*-ia*.

Neither EtymDB nor Lewis & Short records endings this late, so that last step is
the usual account in histories of Portuguese, given here as one.
