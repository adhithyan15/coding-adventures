# हम — the word that was already inside hamārā

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[HI-LEX-C84-POSS-02] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say *hamārā*. You learned it as "our" and were told what it was
built on. Say that word on its own — you have been carrying it for a chapter
without being given it.

## You'll want to know: हम
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[HI-LEX-C86-PRON-01]; assesses=[] -->

**हम** (*ham*) — "we."

The possessive chapter said *hamārā* was built on **हम**, and there the matter
rested. Here is the word itself.

## Grammar Lens: where Hindi says "we" and English says "I"
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[HI-LEX-C86-PRON-01, HI-LEX-C84-POSS-02] -->

**हम** does a second job that has no English equivalent. In much ordinary
speech it stands in for **मैं**, "I," when the speaker is being modest or
speaking as part of a household:

| said | usually means |
|---|---|
| ham jāte haiṅ | we go — or, often, *I go* |
| hamārā ghar | our house — or *my house* |

This is the same instinct that made *hamārā parivār* the natural way to say "my
family." A Hindi speaker places themselves inside a group by default where an
English speaker steps out of it.

A listening paper will play you *ham* where you expect *maiṅ*, and nothing marks
the difference but context.
