Latin, chapter 510: Folly and madness.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
ineptus, inepta, ineptum — silly, foolish; tactless.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for steadfast, then the Latin for good sense.

You'll want to know: ineptus, inepta, ineptum.
ineptus, inepta, ineptum — "silly, foolish; tactless".
Fābula inepta est, sed puerī rīdent. The story is silly, but the children laugh.
in- 'not' + aptus 'fitting': someone who says the wrong thing at the wrong time. English inept.

The word, taken apart.
ineptus is built on in- "not" and aptus "fitting" (Lewis & Short). English inept comes from it.

Lesson 2 of 5.

Vir īnsānus in viā clāmat. A madman is shouting in the street.
in- 'not' + sānus 'healthy'. Also of wild, crazy plans. English insane.

īnsānus is built on in- "not" and sānus "sound, healthy" (Lewis & Short; EtymDB). English insane comes from it.

Lesson 3 of 5.

Warm-up.

īnsānia, īnsāniae — "madness, folly".
Haec īnsānia est, nōn cōnsilium. This is madness, not a plan.
Feminine, first declension, from īnsānus.

The word, taken apart.
īnsānia is built on īnsānus "mad" (Lewis & Short). English insanity comes from the related īnsānitās.

Lesson 4 of 5.
dēmēns, dēmentis — out of one's mind, crazy.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for mad, then the Latin for madness.

You'll want to know: dēmēns, dēmentis.
dēmēns, dēmentis — "out of one's mind, crazy".
Dēmēns es, sī in tempestāte nāvigās. You are out of your mind if you sail in a storm.
One ending. dē- 'away from' + mēns 'mind'. English demented.

The word, taken apart.
dēmēns is built on dē- "away from" and mēns "mind" (Lewis & Short; EtymDB). English demented comes from the related Late Latin verb dēmentō.

Lesson 5 of 5.
dēlīrō, dēlīrāre — to be delirious, talk nonsense.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for madness, then the Latin for out of one's mind.

You'll want to know: dēlīrō, dēlīrāre.
dēlīrō, dēlīrāre — "to be delirious, talk nonsense".
Aeger febrī dēlīrat. The patient is delirious with fever.
dēlīrō, dēlīrāre, dēlīrāvī: dē 'out of' + līra 'furrow' — to plough crooked, then to wander in mind. English delirium.

The word, taken apart.
dēlīrō is built on dē- "away from" and līra "a furrow" (Lewis & Short; EtymDB): first to plough out of the line, then to wander in mind. English delirious and delirium come from it.
