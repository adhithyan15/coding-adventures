Lesson 1 of 5.
strigilis, strigilis — a strigil, scraper.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for the cold room, then the Latin for a pool.

You'll want to know: strigilis, strigilis.
strigilis, strigilis — "a strigil, scraper".
Servus dorsum dominī strigile pūrgat. The slave cleans his master's back with a scraper.
Feminine, third declension. After oil and sweat, bathers scraped their skin clean with this curved tool.

The word, taken apart.
strigilis is built, Lewis & Short says, on stringō "to strip, graze"; EtymDB compares Greek stlengis, so the exact history is not settled.

Lesson 2 of 5.
unguō, unguere — to anoint, rub with oil.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a pool, then the Latin for a strigil.

You'll want to know: unguō, unguere.
unguō, unguere — "to anoint, rub with oil".
Post balneum corpus oleō unguimus. After the bath we rub the body with oil.
unguō, unguere, ūnxī, ūnctum; also spelt ungō. Unguentum, already known, comes from it. English unction.

The word, taken apart.
unguō is inherited from a Proto-Indo-European root meaning "to anoint, salve" (EtymDB). unguentum "ointment" comes from it, and English unction and ointment from its family.

Lesson 3 of 5.
ambulātiō, ambulātiōnis — a walk, stroll.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a strigil, then the Latin for to anoint.

You'll want to know: ambulātiō, ambulātiōnis.
ambulātiō, ambulātiōnis — "a walk, stroll".
Post cēnam ambulātiōnem in hortō facimus. After dinner we take a walk in the garden.
Feminine, third declension, from ambulō. Also a covered walk in a villa.

The word, taken apart.
ambulātiō is the action noun of ambulō "to walk" (Lewis & Short; EtymDB). English ambulance and perambulate come from the same verb.

Lesson 4 of 5.
rūsticor, rūsticārī — to stay in the country.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to anoint, then the Latin for a walk.

You'll want to know: rūsticor, rūsticārī.
rūsticor, rūsticārī — "to stay in the country".
Aestāte pater in vīllā rūsticātur. In summer father stays in the country at the villa.
Deponent: rūsticor, rūsticārī, rūsticātus sum, from rūsticus. English rusticate.

The word, taken apart.
rūsticor is built on rūsticus "of the country", from rūs "countryside" (Lewis & Short). English rusticate comes from it.

Lesson 5 of 5.
amoenus, amoena, amoenum — lovely, pleasant (of places).

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a walk, then the Latin for to stay in the country.

You'll want to know: amoenus, amoena, amoenum.
amoenus, amoena, amoenum — "lovely, pleasant (of places)".
Prope rīvum locus amoenus est. There is a lovely spot near the stream.
Of gardens, groves and views. Locus amoenus — a pleasant place.

The word, taken apart.
amoenus is a native Latin adjective; Lewis & Short reports links with amō "to love" and with a Greek word, but the origin is not settled.
