# nicht am Ende — where the "not" goes

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GE-GRAMMAR-KEIN-VS-NICHT-01, GE-LEX-NICHT-01, GE-GRAMMAR-VERB-SECOND-02] -->

[PAUSE 2s] You can pick the right negative. Now the last piece of this chapter:
German is fussy about *where* **nicht** stands, and the fussiness has one cause
you can see through.

## Grammar Lens: it stands in front of what it cancels
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[GE-GRAMMAR-NICHT-STELLUNG-01]; assesses=[GE-LEX-NICHT-01, GE-LEX-GUT-02, GE-LEX-WOHNEN-01, GE-LEX-ICH-WOHNE-IN-02] -->

> **nicht** stands **immediately in front of the word it cancels** — and when
> what it cancels is the verb itself, there is nothing in front of it to stand
> before, so it goes to the **end**.

Both halves of that are one habit, seen from two sides:

> **Das ist nicht gut.** — the quality is cancelled, so *nicht* leads it.

> **Ich wohne nicht in Berlin.** — the place is cancelled, so *nicht* leads it.

> **Ich verstehe nicht.** — the verbing is cancelled. There is no word left to
> lead, and *nicht* falls to the far end.

English parks its *not* beside the helper verb and leaves it — *I do not live in
Berlin* — so German's *nicht* sounds to an English ear as though it drifts. It is
being aimed.

## Why it's said this way
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GE-GRAMMAR-NICHT-STELLUNG-01, GE-ETYMON-NICHT-NAUGHT-02, GE-GRAMMAR-SEPARABLE-09] -->

The end-of-sentence habit has a history. **Nicht** began as a **noun** —
*niwiht*, "not a whit", a scrap of a thing — and a German object goes late. So
the old sentence was something like *"I understand not-a-scrap"*, with the
scrap sitting where any object sits.

The meaning drained out of the noun and left the position behind. A separable
prefix goes to that same far end, for a related reason: German finishes a
sentence with the piece that completes its sense.
