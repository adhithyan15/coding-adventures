# a word in a hurry — the short form and the long one

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-MUY, ES-LEX-MAL] -->

[PAUSE 2s] *Muy* — very. *Mal* — badly.

[PAUSE 1s] Each of those arrived beside a longer word, and the pairing was not
a coincidence either time.

## Why it's said this way
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-GRAMMAR-APOCOPE-PATTERN]; assesses=[ES-ETYMON-MUY-MULTUM, ES-LEX-MALO] -->

Set the two pairs beside each other.

| long form | short form | short one appears |
|---|---|---|
| *mucho* | ***muy*** | before an adjective or adverb |
| *malo* | ***mal*** | before a masculine noun, and as the adverb |

[PAUSE 2s] This is not two exceptions. It is one habit of the language: Spanish
**bites the ending off a word that is leaning on another word**, and keeps it
whole when that word stands on its own.

[PAUSE 2s] Once you have heard it twice you start noticing it everywhere, and it
stops being something to memorise. A short form is a word in a hurry to get to
the next one.
