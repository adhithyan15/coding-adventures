Latin, chapter 339: Proelium.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
proelium, proeliī — a battle.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a standard-bearer, then the Latin for a tribune.

You'll want to know: proelium, proeliī.
proelium, proeliī — "a battle".
Proelium ab hōrā prīmā ad vesperum dūrābat. The battle lasted from the first hour until evening.
Neuter. A single battle; bellum (taught) is the whole war. Proelium committere: to join battle.

The word, taken apart.
proelium is a word whose origin Lewis & Short call doubtful; they suggest, with hesitation, a link with duellum, the old form of bellum.

Lesson 2 of 5.
aciēs, aciēī — a battle line; a sharp edge.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a tribune, then the Latin for a battle.

You'll want to know: aciēs, aciēī.
aciēs, aciēī — "a battle line; a sharp edge".
Dux aciem ante castra posuit. The leader drew up the battle line in front of the camp.
Feminine, 5th declension, like diēs. First a sharp edge, then the sharp front of an army.

The word, taken apart.
aciēs is inherited from a Proto-Indo-European root meaning "sharp" (EtymDB): the edge of a blade, then the front edge of an army. English acid comes from the same root.

Lesson 3 of 5.
agmen, agminis — an army on the march; a crowd.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a battle, then the Latin for a battle line.

You'll want to know: agmen, agminis.
agmen, agminis — "an army on the march; a crowd".
Agmen longum per vallem prōcēdēbat. A long line of soldiers was moving forward through the valley.
Neuter, 3rd declension: a body in motion. Novissimum agmen: the rearguard.

The word, taken apart.
agmen is built on agō, "to drive, to lead" (EtymDB), with the ending -men: a train in motion.

Lesson 4 of 5.
incursiō, incursiōnis — a raid, an attack.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a battle line, then the Latin for an army on the march.

You'll want to know: incursiō, incursiōnis.
incursiō, incursiōnis — "a raid, an attack".
Hostēs incursiōnem in agrōs nostrōs fēcērunt. The enemy made a raid on our fields.
Feminine, 3rd declension, from incurrō, 'to run against'. English incursion.

The word, taken apart.
incursiō is built on incurrō, "to run against", from in and currō, "to run" (Lewis & Short). English incursion comes from it.

Lesson 5 of 5.
dīmicō, dīmicāre — to fight it out, to struggle.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for an army on the march, then the Latin for a raid.

You'll want to know: dīmicō, dīmicāre.
dīmicō, dīmicāre — "to fight it out, to struggle".
Ambō exercitūs ācriter dīmicāvērunt. Both armies fought it out fiercely.
dīmicō, dīmicāre, dīmicāvī, dīmicātum. A hard, deciding fight.

The word, taken apart.
dīmicō is built on micō, "to flash, to quiver", with dis- (EtymDB): perhaps first the flashing of swords to and fro.
