## Unreleased — chapter 97: the present continuous, and two old sentences that were never lumps

### The first chapter in three that the alphabet did not block

Chapters 95 and 96 were both held up by untaught letters. This one needed
**none**: *rahā*, *rahī* and *rahe* are all writable with glyphs the track has
had for a long time. What was missing was an **atom**, and nothing else.

That is worth saying plainly, because the last two chapters could have made the
alphabet look like the universal explanation. It is not. Some points are blocked
by the script and some are blocked by nobody having written the lesson, and the
glyph check is what tells you which — in about thirty seconds.

### The note was right, and both frames get collected

`HI-A1-V-07`'s note said the pattern *"appears twice as a fixed frame — `ek baj
raha hai` in the hour lesson, `barish ho rahi hai` in the weather lesson — but no
atom presents it as a pattern applying to any stem."* That is exactly true, and
it needed no correction.

So the chapter does not merely teach the pattern; it goes back for the two
frames:

| sentence | what it actually is |
|---|---|
| एक बज रहा है | *baj-* bare, **रहा**, **है** |
| बारिश हो रही है | *ho-* bare, **रही**, **है** |

Neither was ever a lump. The only difference between them is that the clock is
masculine and the rain is feminine, and the middle word was reporting that all
along.

### Three words, one job each

| piece | what it carries |
|---|---|
| कर | the action, bare |
| रहा | that it is in progress |
| हूँ | who, and that it is now |

**रहा** is the past participle of **रहना**, "to stay, to remain," so the sentence
says almost literally *doing — having stayed — I am*. The action is pictured as
one you are still inside, which is the same metaphor English spells out in *I
remain at work*.

The shape is unusually tidy for a tense, and the tidiness is the teachable part:
**the stem stays bare**. The habitual puts an ending on it; the continuous takes
the ending off and puts a separate word behind it. That is why the pattern is
productive — it works on any stem you know without touching the verb.

The grid is two independent dials:

| subject | middle and last |
|---|---|
| मैं (m) | रहा हूँ |
| मैं (f) | रही हूँ |
| वह (m) | रहा है |
| वह (f) | रही है |
| हम, वे, आप | रहे हैं |
| तुम | रहे हो |

The middle word answers *what is the subject like*, the last answers *which
person*, and turning one does nothing to the other. **रहा / रही / रहे** is the
fourth **-आ / -ई / -ए** set in the track, after the colours, the habitual and the
past copula.

### The thing an English speaker gets for free

Hindi and English both keep a two-way split between *I do* and *I am doing*.
French, German and Spanish translate both lines with a single present tense. So
the chapter says outright that **the instinct transfers**: translate the shape,
not the words.

The one caution it adds is the direction the transfer fails — Hindi leans on the
habitual for an arranged near future where English reaches for *I am*.

### A self-inflicted forward-reference spike, caught before it shipped

First draft of the contrast lesson used the headword **करता हूँ / कर रहा हूँ**.
`forward-language` went **13 → 18**, and all five new entries had the same
`taughtBy`: that lesson, claiming to introduce **करता हूँ** — a form taught since
chapter 5. Five earlier lessons that use it read as early uses.

This is the self-inflicted case of HL-C378's family, and the second time a
headword of mine has produced one. **A contrast lesson is exactly where it
happens**, because the natural headword prints both sides of the contrast and one
side is always already taught — that is what makes it a contrast.

Fixed by renaming the headword to **दो वर्तमान** (*do vartamān*, "two
presents"), the metalinguistic form the *repaso* lessons already use. Count back
to **13**, and the lesson reads better for it: its subject is the distinction,
not either form.
