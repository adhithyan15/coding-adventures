# el vendedor — the ending you already have, doing its real job

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-VENDER, ES-LEX-C423-CHAR-03] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say *vender*. Say *trabajador*. You have the verb and you have the
ending. Put them together before reading on.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-C427-AGENT-01]; assesses=[] -->

**el vendedor** — "the seller, the shop assistant."

Take *vender*, drop the **-er**, add **-dor**. That is the whole operation, and
you met the ending as an adjective in the character chapter. Here it does what
it was built for: **making a person out of an action.**

| verb | the one who does it |
|---|---|
| trabajar | el trabaja**dor** |
| vender | el vende**dor** |
| jugar | el juga**dor** |
| ganar | el gana**dor** |

The ending is **productive**, which is the property worth having. You are not
learning a word; you are learning a machine that turns any verb you know into
the person who does it.

## Grammar Lens: where the ending puts the stress
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C427-AGENT-01, ES-LEX-C423-CHAR-03] -->

Every **-dor** noun stresses its last syllable: *vende-DOR*, *trabaja-DOR*,
*juga-DOR*. None of them carries a written accent, because a word ending in
**-r** is stressed on the last syllable by default and the accent marks only
departures from the default.

The feminine adds **-a** and nothing else: *la vendedora*, *la trabajadora*.
