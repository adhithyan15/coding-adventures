# آمدن — “to come,” and an alef that wears a hat

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[FA-LEX-RAFTAN, FA-STEM-RAV, FA-GRAMMAR-PRESENT-STEM] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say the pair for “to go” as one item. Now expect the same two-part
shape from every verb that follows, including this one, which travels in the
opposite direction.

## You'll want to know first — one verb and one new shape
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[FA-LEX-AMADAN, FA-SCRIPT-ALEF-MADDE]; assesses=[] -->

> **آمدن** — *âmadan* — **to come**

The last three letters are old friends: **م** *m*, **د** *d*, **ن** *n*, and the
*-dan* ending tells you at once that this is an infinitive. The first shape is
new. **آ** is plain **ا** with a small wavy stroke over it, called *madde*, and
it is what Persian writes when a word opens on long **â**. Plain **ا** at the
start of a word carries a short vowel — that is the **ا** in **اسم** *esm*.
The hat is the difference between *esm* and *âmadan*.

## The word, taken apart — the root English kept as *come*
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[FA-STEM-A]; assesses=[] -->

**âmadan** was built from a prefix **â-**, “toward,” and the very old verb root
\**gwem-*, “to go, to come.” In Old Iranian that gave a form like
\**â-gmata-*, “come toward”; the *g* wore away, the *m* survived, and Middle
Persian **âmad** is what was left.

That root is one of the family's great travellers. English kept it almost bare
as **come**. Latin turned it into *venīre*, which English then borrowed back
dozens of times over: **advent**, **adventure**, **convene**, **convenient**,
**event**, **invent**, **avenue**, **revenue**, **prevent**, **souvenir**.
Sanskrit has *gacchati*, “goes.” So *âmadan* and *come* are not lookalikes; they
are the same word, four thousand years apart.

The present stem is **آ** *â* — a single letter — heard as *ây-* before an
ending: *mi-âyam* “I come.” Store the pair as **âmadan, â-**. It comes from a
different ancient root than the infinitive does, which is exactly why the two
look nothing alike.
