Lesson 1 of 6.
ਸਤਿ ਸ੍ਰੀ ਅਕਾਲ (sat srī akāl) — the Sikh greeting.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, for letter shapes on the page. You can listen to everything else now — leave the section called The letters in this word until you have stopped, and I will say so again when we reach it.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
The first Punjabi words — and a greeting that is a small statement of faith.

The letters in this word.
(If you already read Gurmukhi, skim this.) Gurmukhi runs left to right under a top line; every consonant carries a built-in "a." ਸ sa, ਤ ta, ਰ ra, ਕ ka, ਲ la. A vowel sign changes the vowel (ਾ ā, ਿ i, ੀ ī); a word-initial vowel rides a carrier — ਅ is the "a"-carrier. So ਅਕਾਲ = a, kā, l becomes akāl.

The word, taken apart.
A three-word creed:
ਸਤਿ (sat, "truth") — from Sanskrit satya, the same "being/truth" root behind English sooth and soothsayer ("truth-teller").
ਸ੍ਰੀ (srī) — an honorific "revered/glorious," from Sanskrit śrī ("glory, splendor").
ਅਕਾਲ (akāl, "timeless") = a- ("not") + kāl ("time, death," Sanskrit kāla). In Sikhism Akāl is a name of God: the Timeless One.
So the whole greeting declares: God is the eternal Truth.

Why it's said this way.
The greeting of Sikhs — said with folded hands, serving as both hello and goodbye. It is a profession of faith as much as a greeting, which is why it carries weight. A Sikh will often answer it by saying it right back.

Lesson 2 of 6.
ਨਮਸਤੇ (namaste) — the shared greeting.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, for letter shapes on the page. You can listen to everything else now — leave the section called The letters in this word until you have stopped, and I will say so again when we reach it.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Not every greeting in Punjab is Sikh. Here is the one used across communities.

The letters in this word.
New: ਨ na, ਮ ma, and ਤੇ te (ਤ ta + the e-sign ੇ). With ਸ from before, read ਨ, ਮ, ਸ, ਤੇ becomes namaste.

The word, taken apart.
ਨਮਸਤੇ (namaste) = Sanskrit namas ("a bow") + te ("to you") — literally "I bow to you." It is the very same word as Hindi namaste — one of the Sanskrit-inherited words Punjabi and Hindi share exactly.

Why it's said this way.
Punjab is home to many communities. Namaste is the more general, pan-Indian greeting — used across faiths — where sat srī akāl is specifically Sikh. Reaching for the right one is itself a small act of courtesy.

Lesson 3 of 6.
ਧੰਨਵਾਦ (dhannavād) — "thank you," the Sanskrit way.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, for letter shapes on the page. You can listen to everything else now — leave the section called The letters in this word until you have stopped, and I will say so again when we reach it.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Punjabi has two ways to say thank you. Here is the first — the one inherited from Sanskrit.

The letters in this word.
New: ਧ dha (a breathy d), ਵ va, ਦ da, and the nasal dot ੰ (ṭippi) sitting on ਧੰ dhaṁ. Read ਧੰ, ਨ, ਵਾ, ਦ becomes dhannavād.

The word, taken apart.
ਧੰਨਵਾਦ (dhannavād) = Sanskrit dhanya ("worthy, deserving") + vāda ("a saying, a speaking") — "a saying of "you are worthy."" It is the same Sanskrit "thank you" behind Hindi dhanyavād and Marathi dhanyavād: one word, shared across the Indo-Aryan north.

Why it's said this way.
This is the formal, Sanskritic thanks — what you would write, or say in a respectful setting. Hold it against the next word, shukrīā, which arrived from an entirely different direction.

Lesson 4 of 6.
ਸ਼ੁਕਰੀਆ (shukrīā) — "thank you," the Persian way.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, for letter shapes on the page. You can listen to everything else now — leave the section called The letters in this word until you have stopped, and I will say so again when we reach it.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
The same meaning as dhannavād — but a word that came to Punjab from the opposite direction.

The letters in this word.
New: ਸ਼ sha — the letter ਸ (sa) with a dot beneath, a pair bindi that marks a Perso-Arabic sound. With ੁ (the u-sign) and ੀ (ī), read ਸ਼ੁ, ਕ, ਰੀ, ਆ becomes shukrīā.

The word, taken apart.
ਸ਼ੁਕਰੀਆ (shukrīā) is not Sanskrit. It came through Persian from Arabic shukr ("gratitude") — the same Semitic root behind Arabic shukran. Punjab sat for centuries on the road between Persia and India, and (like Urdu and Hindi) it carries two vocabularies: Sanskrit (dhannavād) and Perso-Arabic (shukrīā), side by side for the same idea. The dot under ਸ਼ is the script's own record of the borrowing — Gurmukhi literally annotates its loanwords.

Why it's said this way.
Both words are alive. Shukrīā feels a touch more colloquial and Urdu-flavored; dhannavād more formal and Sanskritic. Choosing between them is choosing which of Punjab's two heritages to lean on — the everyday drama of a border language.

Lesson 5 of 6.
ਹਾਂ / ਨਹੀਂ (hāṇ / nahīṇ) — "yes" and "no".

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, for letter shapes on the page. You can listen to everything else now — leave the section called The letters in this word until you have stopped, and I will say so again when we reach it.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Two tiny words you cannot hold a conversation without — and one of them is a cousin of English.

The letters in this word.
New: ਹ ha, and the nasal sign ਂ (bindi) that hangs above the line. ਹਾਂ (hāṇ) and ਨਹੀਂ (nahīṇ) both end in a nasalised vowel — the breath released through the nose, marked by that dot.

The word, taken apart.
ਹਾਂ (hāṇ, "yes") and ਨਹੀਂ (nahīṇ, "no"). The "no" is built on the ancient negative na- — the very same Proto-Indo-European ne that surfaces in English no, not, and none, in Latin nōn, and in Hindi nahīṇ. Say "no" in Punjabi and in English and you are reaching for the same five-thousand-year-old syllable.

Why it's said this way.
The nasalised vowel (that bindi) is everywhere in Punjabi — it is part of what gives the language its distinctive music, and it often carries grammatical weight (marking plurals, oblique forms). For now, just hear the "n"-colored release at the end of both words.
