Italian, chapter 300: Feriti e illesi.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
travolgere — to run over, to sweep away.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Italian for to dent, then the Italian for to damage.

You'll want to know: travolgere.
travolgere — "to run over, to sweep away".
Un'auto ha travolto un ciclista. A car ran over a cyclist.
Past participle travolto. Also a wave or a flood: travolto dall'acqua.

The word, taken apart.
travolgere joins tra- "across", from Latin trāns, and volgere "to turn", from Latin volvere "to roll" (Lewis & Short).

Lesson 2 of 5.
il ferito — the injured person, the wounded.

[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Italian for to damage, then the Italian for to run over.

You'll want to know: il ferito.
il ferito — "the injured person, the wounded".
I feriti sono in ospedale. The injured are in hospital.
Plural i feriti; as an adjective, ferito means injured.

The word, taken apart.
ferito is the past participle of ferire "to wound", which is inherited from Latin ferīre "to strike" (EtymDB).

Lesson 4 of 5.
illeso — unhurt, unharmed.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Italian for the injured person, then the Italian for to injure.

You'll want to know: illeso.
illeso — "unhurt, unharmed".
Il bambino è uscito illeso dall'incidente. The child came out of the accident unhurt.
Uscire illeso — to come out unharmed.

The word, taken apart.
illeso is a learned borrowing from Latin illaesus "unhurt", from in- "not" and laesus, the past participle of laedere "to hurt" (Lewis & Short).

Lesson 5 of 5.
cavarsela — to come through all right, to get by.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Italian for to injure, then the Italian for unhurt.

You'll want to know: cavarsela.
cavarsela — "to come through all right, to get by".
È stato un brutto incidente, ma ce la siamo cavata. It was a bad accident, but we came through it.
An idiom: me la cavo, se l'è cavata. Also ‘to manage’: me la cavo con l'inglese.

The word, taken apart.
cavarsela is built on cavare "to dig out, to pull out", which is inherited from Latin cavāre "to hollow out" (Lewis & Short): to pull oneself out of trouble.
