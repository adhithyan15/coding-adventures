# sie sind / Sie sind — "they are," and "you are," politely

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GE-LEX-DU-SIE-02, GE-GRAMMAR-SEIN-IHR-SEID-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] The polite **Sie** is literally the word for "they," worn as a
courtesy. Its verb form follows from that, and costs you nothing new.

## You'll want to know: sie sind and Sie sind
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[GE-GRAMMAR-SEIN-SIE-SIND-01]; assesses=[GE-GRAMMAR-SEIN-WIR-SIND-01] -->

> **sie sind** — "they are."
> **Sie sind** — "you are," to someone you would address formally.

The form is **sind**, the very same word you learned for *wir*. Nothing changes
in the mouth at all: *wir sind*, *sie sind*, *Sie sind* are three subjects and
one verb form.

## Grammar Lens: the capital carries the politeness
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GE-GRAMMAR-GROSSSCHREIBUNG-01, GE-GRAMMAR-SEIN-SIE-SIND-01] -->

On the page, *sie* and *Sie* differ by exactly one capital letter — and German
already spends capitals on grammar rather than decoration. In speech there is
no difference at all; the context tells you which one is meant.

| written | means |
|---|---|
| *sie sind* | they are |
| *Sie sind* | you are, formally |

That is the whole of it. Six subjects, and you have now met every present form
of the verb.

<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GE-GRAMMAR-SEIN-SIE-SIND-01, GE-GRAMMAR-GROSSSCHREIBUNG-01] -->

[PAUSE 3s] Say "they are." (**Sie sind**.) Say "you are" to a stranger.
(**Sie sind** — same sound.) What separates the two on paper? (**The capital
S**.) How many different present forms of this verb have you now met? (**Five**
— *bin, bist, ist, sind, seid*.) Next: one adjective, so you can finally say
something about yourself.
