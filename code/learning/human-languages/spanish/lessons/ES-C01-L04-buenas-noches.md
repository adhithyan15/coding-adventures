# buenas noches — good evening *and* good night

## Warm-up

[PAUSE 2s] The third and last of the blessing-greetings. One new word,
*noche* — and it lights up a constellation of English words about the night.

## You'll want to know first

- [ES-C01-L03 (buenas tardes)](./ES-C01-L03-buenas-tardes.md) — same *buenas*
  + blessing pattern; this is the night version.

## Sounds you'll need

- `soft-c` — the **ch** in *noche* is the same *ch* as English *church*:
  *NO-cheh*. (Spanish *ch* is its own single sound.)
- Regular stress: *NO-ches*.

## The word, taken apart

**noche** — night / evening.
Root: Latin **nox**, stem **noct-** ("night"). This *noct-* is everywhere in
English:

- **noct**urnal — active at night.
- **noct**urne — a piece of night-music.
- equi**nox** — *equi* ("equal") + *nox*, the day when night and day are
  equal length.

Same root, worn a little smoother, gives Spanish *noche* — and, one step
further along the same path, French *nuit* and Italian *notte*.

## Why it's said this way

Same amputated blessing as its two siblings (*"Buenas noches os dé Dios"*).
But *buenas noches* has a twist the others don't: it works both as a
**greeting** *and* as a **farewell**. Arriving at a dinner at 9pm, you say
*buenas noches* ("good evening"). Leaving that same dinner at midnight, you
say *buenas noches* again ("goodnight"). English splits these into two
different phrases — "good evening" to arrive, "goodnight" to leave — where
Spanish uses one for both. Context, not the words, tells you which.

There's no separate "good evening" vs. "good night" in Spanish because the
blessing doesn't care about direction — you're wishing good hours either
way, coming or going.
