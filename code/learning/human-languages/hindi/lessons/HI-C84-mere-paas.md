# मेरे पास — Hindi has no word for "have"

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[HI-CONCEPT-C02-MERAA-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say *merā*, *merī*, *mere*. You have had all three since the second
chapter. This lesson shows you that the third one has a second job nobody
mentioned.

## You'll want to know: मेरे पास
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[HI-LEX-C84-POSS-05]; assesses=[] -->

**मेरे पास** (*mere pās*) — "I have." Literally: **near me**.

**पास** (*pās*) means "near, at hand." Hindi has no verb meaning *to have*, so
possession is built out of location:

> मेरे पास एक किताब है।
> *mere pās ek kitāb hai* — "Near me there is a book" = **I have a book.**

The thing owned is the **subject** of that sentence, which is why *hai* agrees
with *kitāb* and not with the speaker.

## Grammar Lens: mere is not the plural here
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[HI-LEX-C84-POSS-05, HI-CONCEPT-C02-MERAA-01, HI-LEX-C84-POSS-01] -->

This is the part that repays attention. You learned *mere* as the **plural** of
*merā*. It is also the **oblique** — the form a word takes in front of a
postposition — and the two happen to look identical:

| phrase | why *mere* |
|---|---|
| *mere dost* | plural — my friends |
| *mere pās* | oblique — *pās* is a postposition |

So *mere pās* is not "my nears." It is *merā* bending because something stands
after it. Every **-आ** possessive does this: *uskā* → *uske pās*, *hamārā* →
*hamāre pās*, *apnā* → *apne pās*.

That single habit — **-आ** becomes **-ए** in front of a postposition — is worth
more than the phrase itself, because it governs every postposition in the
language.
