# ചായയും കാപ്പിയും ഇല്ല — neither of them is here

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ML-CONCEPT-C70-UM-01, ML-LEX-ILLA-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Join two things with the ending you know: *tea and coffee*.

[PAUSE 2s] Then say the word for *there isn't any*.

## You'll want to know
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ML-GRAMMAR-C109-NEITHER-01]; assesses=[ML-CONCEPT-C39-CHAAYA-01, ML-CONCEPT-C39-KAAPI-01] -->

> **ചായയും കാപ്പിയും ഇല്ല.**
>
> *cāyayum kāppiyum illa* — "**There is neither tea nor coffee.**"

**Nothing in that sentence is new.** You built **ചായയും കാപ്പിയും** when you
learned to join two things, and **ഇല്ല** has been yours since your first
conversation. Put the one in front of the other and Malayalam has said *neither*.

## Grammar Lens: English buys a special word for this and Malayalam does not
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ML-GRAMMAR-C109-NEITHER-01, ML-CONCEPT-C70-UM-01] -->

English reaches for a pair of words it keeps only for this job — *neither … nor*
— and they behave unlike anything else in the sentence.

Malayalam spends nothing:

| | |
|---|---|
| **ചായയും കാപ്പിയും** | the joining you already do |
| **ഇല്ല** | the negative you already have |

The list is built the ordinary way, with the ending on **every** item, and then
the whole list is denied at once. What English treats as a special construction,
Malayalam treats as a list that happens to end in a negative.

**Both items are inside the denial**, which is the thing to hear. You are not
saying *there is no tea, and coffee*. The ending has tied the two together
before **ഇല്ല** arrives, so the negative lands on the pair.

## Wrap-up Recall
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ML-GRAMMAR-C109-NEITHER-01, ML-LEX-ILLA-01] -->

ഇല്ല**.) How many new words did that need? (**None** — the joining ending and

pair? (**The pair.**)
