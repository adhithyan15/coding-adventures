Spanish, chapter 468: Dos grados.
6 lessons. The first lesson already needs your eyes or your hands, so save this one for when you have stopped.


Lesson 1 of 6.
la chaqueta — the jacket.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, because the lesson points at something written down. You can listen to all of it now and come back to the parts that need looking at once you have stopped.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say el jersey and la piel. Both lessons put the next word in front of you without stopping to teach it.

You'll want to know: la chaqueta.
la chaqueta — the jacket.
Hace frío. Llévate una chaqueta.
Una chaqueta de piel — a leather jacket, the phrase la piel already gave you. La chaqueta del traje — the suit jacket.
Among knitwear the line is clean: a jersey goes over your head, a chaqueta de punto opens down the front — which is what a cardigan is. Beyond knitwear a chaqueta is any short tailored jacket.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
where: Spain. the everyday word: la chaqueta.
where: much of Latin America. the everyday word: el saco, la campera, la chamarra.
Take the regional column seriously here. In Mexico and much of Central America chaqueta carries a vulgar second sense, and the neutral word is saco or chamarra.

The word, taken apart.
You were shown this one in passing when you learned el jersey, in a table of garments named after somewhere. It sat there as the odd row, because it is not named after a place at all.
French jaquette is a small jaque, a short tunic — and that much is firm. Where jaque came from is not. One account takes it to Jacques, the stock name for a French peasant, the way English once said Jack for any ordinary man. Another takes it through Spanish jaco to an Arabic word for a mail jerkin. Both are live; neither is proved.
You already know Spanish will name a garment after its wearer — los vaqueros are the cowherd's trousers. If the Jacques account is right, la chaqueta is the same habit, which is why it sat in the wrong column of the jersey chapter's table:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
garment: el jersey. named after: an island.
garment: la chaqueta. named after: a man's name, probably.
English took both ends of the pair: jack, a medieval padded tunic, straight from jaque; and jacket, from the little one, jaquette.

Lesson 2 of 6.
el grado — the degree.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say la temperatura, then the scale: poco, bastante, muy. The word coming next is what that scale is a scale of, and you have never been given it.

You'll want to know: el grado.
el grado — the degree.
Estamos a tres grados.
Doce grados — twelve degrees. Bajo cero — below zero. The everyday frame with a number is estar a: estamos a tres grados, we are at three degrees. You will also hear hace tres grados and hay tres grados; estar a is the one to produce.
When you learned la temperatura you got the quantity without its unit. This is the unit.

Grammar Lens: the word you already had under another job.
Poco, bastante, muy is a scale of degree, and degree is grado. The review that taught that scale carries this word in its own filename and never once put it in front of you. Here it is.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
use: tres grados. what it counts: steps up a thermometer.
use: the poco / bastante / muy scale. what it counts: steps up a quality.
Latin gradus is a step — the one you take walking. From there it went to any step in a series: a step on a staircase, a step of rank, a step of temperature. English keeps the walking sense in gradual, one step at a time, and in grade, degree and graduate, who has climbed the steps.
So a grado is not only a number. It is a position on a set of stairs, and a thermometer is a staircase somebody agreed the height of.

The word, taken apart.
Gradus also gives Spanish el grado for a university degree and for a school year, and la graduación, which is both the ceremony and the strength of an alcohol — another scale with steps on it.
De buen grado — willingly, gladly — is a different word, from gratum, pleasing. Two grados, and only this one has stairs in it.

Lesson 3 of 6.
el abrigo — the coat.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say el frío and la chaqueta. One is the reason for the next word; the other is not warm enough for it.

You'll want to know: el abrigo.
el abrigo — the coat, the overcoat.
A tres grados no salgas sin abrigo.
Abrigar is the verb: abrígate, wrap up warm. And una manta que abriga mucho is a blanket that keeps you very warm — the verb is about the warmth, not the wrapping.
A chaqueta is a jacket. An abrigo is the long one you put on over everything else when it is properly cold.

The word, taken apart.
This word is not named for its shape. It is named for what it does.
Behind abrigar is Latin apricari, to bask in the sun. Apricus was the adjective: open to the sun, warm.
Then Spanish did something quietly odd with it. A word for a place the sun gets into became a word for a place the weather cannot, and the coat is named for the second one. El abrigo is not "a long coat", it is a shelter — un abrigo rocoso is a hollow in the rock face out of the weather, and al abrigo de is in the shelter of.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
Spanish: el abrigo. the shelter: the one you wear.
Spanish: al abrigo de. the shelter: the one you stand behind.
Watch that second one: what follows de is what shelters you, not what you are hiding from.
One warning while apricus is in front of you: English apricot looks like a relative and is not one. That word came through Arabic and Portuguese from Latin praecoquum, the early-ripening fruit, and the resemblance to apricus is a coincidence people have been reading meaning into for centuries.
So the garment is named for the sunny corner it replaces.

Lesson 4 of 6.
la bufanda — the scarf.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say el abrigo and la chaqueta. One more goes on top of either, and it is the last piece this chapter needs.

You'll want to know: la bufanda.
la bufanda — the scarf, the winter one you wind round your neck.
Ponte la bufanda, que estamos a dos grados.
That que is not the joining que you have. It is the spoken shorthand for porque — put your scarf on, because it's two degrees — and Spanish leans on it constantly for reasons.
A bufanda is the one you put on because it is cold. Un fular is the light draped one worn for how it looks; un pañuelo is usually a handkerchief and only sometimes a scarf. A bufanda can be silk, so the material is not the test — the reason is.

The word, taken apart.
Every other word in this chapter came with a story. This one comes with a shrug, and you have met that shrug before.
La bufanda has no settled origin. The usual suggestion is French bouffante, puffed out, on bouffer, to puff — which fits a scarf's bulk around the throat. Another line points at Italian buffa, a visor or a covering for the face. The dictionaries hedge, and so does this lesson.
El patio is where this book set the rule out: an origin left open is a claim, not an oversight, and saying so is more useful than picking the prettier story. El andén, tirar and el gorro carry the same mark, and el gorro is the nearest neighbour — winter headwear whose origin the Academy also marks uncertain.
La bufanda is the fifth. Read the prose rather than the front matter for this: plenty of lessons leave the root list empty and still tell you the whole story. What is unusual here is that there is no story to tell.

Lesson 5 of 6.
(la chaqueta, el grado, el abrigo, la bufanda) — from cold.

Warm-up.
[pause 3 seconds]
Four words, no prompts. What you put on, what you put on over it, what goes round your neck, and the number that decides.

You'll want to know: the four, cold.
[a table of 4 rows, read aloud]
English: the jacket. Spanish: la chaqueta.
English: the degree. Spanish: el grado.
English: the coat. Spanish: el abrigo.
English: the scarf. Spanish: la bufanda.

Grammar Lens: a list that was closed, reopened on purpose.
The wardrobe chapter called its list covered end to end. It was — for what that exam asked. Pantalón, falda, camisa, zapato, vaqueros, jersey, bolso: enough to say what you are wearing.
What it could not do is answer a question about weather.
[a table of 1 row, read aloud]
the old list: what you are wearing. this chapter: what the cold makes you wear.
That is the difference between the two exams. The first asks you to describe; the second asks you to decide, and deciding needs a number. Hace frío was never going to be enough once somebody writes estamos a dos grados on a screen at a bus stop.

The words, taken apart.
Two of the four have a settled origin, a third has a guess, and the fourth refuses.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
word: el grado. underneath: gradus, a step — a thermometer is a staircase.
word: el abrigo. underneath: apricari, to bask in the sun — a shelter you wear.
word: la chaqueta. underneath: Jacques, a peasant's name — probably.
La bufanda has no agreed origin at all, and its lesson says so rather than covering over, the way el patio and el gorro did before it.

Lesson 6 of 6.
(el aviso de la parada) — read it, then decide.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say el grado and el abrigo. The first decides the second, which is the whole task here.

Reading: the screen, and the message.
On the screen at the bus stop:
07:40 — LÍNEA 12 — 6 min Ahora: 2 grados. Máxima hoy: 7 grados. (La máxima is the day's high.)
And a message on your phone:
Hace mucho frío. Ponte el abrigo, no la chaqueta. Y llévate la bufanda, que estuviste enfermo la semana pasada.

Grammar Lens: what the message is doing that the screen cannot.
The screen gives a number. The message gives a decision, and the two are not the same kind of statement.
[a table of 1 row, read aloud]
the screen: 2 grados. the message: ponte el abrigo, no la chaqueta.
Note no la chaqueta. With the chosen thing said first, Spanish needs only no and the thing ruled out. Turn it round and you are back in the shape sino gave you — no la chaqueta, sino el abrigo. Same correction, two orders.
And que estuviste enfermo gives the reason, with the spoken que that stands in for porque, which la bufanda introduced two lessons ago. A screen rarely explains itself; a person usually does.

Guided Practice.
[pause 3 seconds]
Say what the temperature is now and what it will reach.
[pause 3 seconds]
Now answer the message: say what you are putting on and what you are leaving behind, using no the way the message does.

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
How cold is it? (Estamos a dos grados.) What goes on? (El abrigo and la bufanda.) What stays behind? (La chaqueta.)
