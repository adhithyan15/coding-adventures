# sehr — "very," and it used to mean "sorely"

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GE-LEX-GUT-02] -->

[PAUSE 2s] *Gut* answers "how are you?" perfectly well. One small word in front
of it turns a polite answer into a warm one.

## You'll want to know: sehr
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[GE-LEX-SEHR-01]; assesses=[GE-LEX-GUT-02] -->

> **sehr** — "very."

Said *zair*: German **s** before a vowel buzzes like English **z**, the **h** is
silent and stretches the vowel, and the **r** softens almost to *ah*.

> **Sehr gut!** — "Very well!"

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[GE-ETYMON-SEHR-SORE-02]; assesses=[GE-LEX-SEHR-01] -->

**sehr** is English **sore**. In Middle High German it meant *painfully,
grievously* — and intensifiers very often start life as words for pain, then
fade into pure degree. English did exactly the same thing and then half
forgot: "**sorely** missed," "**sore** afraid" both mean *very*, not *painful*.

So *sehr gut* is, at the root, "sorely good" — and English *very* went the same
road from a different start, having once meant *truly* (French *vrai*).
