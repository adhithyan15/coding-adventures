Persian, chapter 292: What I Think, And So.
3 lessons. All 3 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 3.
به نظر من (be nazar-e man) — to my view, which is to say, in my opinion.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say "to seem", then "to think". (Be nazar residan, fekr kardan.)
[pause 1 second]
A story says what happened. Giving reasons starts one step earlier, with what you think about it.

You'll want to know: به نظر من.
به نظر من — be nazar-e man — "in my opinion", "if you ask me".
به نظر من این فیلم خیلی خوب است. — be nazar-e man in film kheyli khub ast — In my opinion this film is very good.
Word for word it is "to the view of me": be, "to", nazar, "view", and the ezafe joining man as its owner. Change the owner to hand the opinion back: به نظر شما؟, be nazar-e shomâ?, "what do you think?".
It is the nazar of be nazar residan, "to seem", "to reach the view". The present of that verb opens a careful opinion: به نظر میرسد که..., be nazar miresad ke..., "it seems that...".

Grammar Lens: fekr mikonam ke.
The verb you have held since chapter seven, in the present, with که, ke, "that":
فکر میکنم که قطار دیر میرسد. — fekr mikonam ke qatâr dir miresad — I think the train will be late.
Ke opens the thought, and the thought keeps its ordinary verb, miresad. In speech ke is often dropped: fekr mikonam qatâr dir miresad.
"I don't think so" is the negative with nothing after it: فکر نمیکنم, fekr nemikonam.

The phrase, taken apart.
nazar is borrowed from Arabic naẓar, "a look, a view" (EtymDB 2.1); be and man are Persian. So the phrase sets an Arabic noun inside a Persian frame, the way fekr kardan sets Arabic fekr, "thought", in front of a Persian verb.

Guided Practice.

[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: hand it back — be nazar-e shomâ?]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: fekr mikonam ke qatâr dir miresad, then without ke]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — answer: qatâr dir miresad? with "I don't think so" — fekr nemikonam]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
What does be nazar-e man say, word for word? (To the view of me.) "What do you think?" (Be nazar-e shomâ?) What does ke do after fekr mikonam? (It opens the thought, and the verb after it stays ordinary.)


Lesson 2 of 3.
برای همین (barâ-ye hamin) — so: the arrow that points forward.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say "because", two ways. (Chon, zirâ.) Then "in my opinion". (Be nazar-e man.)
[pause 1 second]
An opinion needs its reasons, and a reason needs a result. Persian can join the two from either end.

Grammar Lens: two arrows.
One fact, three ways to join it:
خسته بودم، برای همین زود خوابیدم. — khaste budam, barâ-ye hamin zud khâbidam — I was tired, so I went to sleep early.
چون خسته بودم، زود خوابیدم. — chon khaste budam, zud khâbidam — Because I was tired, I went to sleep early.
زود خوابیدم، زیرا خسته بودم. — zud khâbidam, zirâ khaste budam — I went to sleep early, because I was tired.
برای همین, barâ-ye hamin, comes after the cause and points forward to the result. Barâ-ye is "for", and hamin is "this very": "for this very reason". Chon and zirâ sit on the cause itself and point back to it. Chon can open the sentence; zirâ mostly follows the result, and sounds written.

Grammar Lens: pas, the short one.
پس, pas, is "so" when you draw a conclusion, often at the front of what you conclude:
پس قطار دیر میرسد. — pas qatâr dir miresad — So the train will be late.
Banâbarin, "therefore", does the same job in writing. Pas is also the pas of pas az, "after": a result comes after its cause.

The word, taken apart.
hamin is ham, "same", with in, "this". EtymDB 2.1 takes ham through Middle and Old Persian back to an Indo-European word for "same", the one English kept as same. Pas goes back, in EtymDB, through Middle Persian ps, read pas, to an Indo-European word meaning "behind, afterwards".

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: khaste budam, barâ-ye hamin zud khâbidam]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: the same, with the cause first and chon in front]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: a conclusion — pas qatâr dir miresad]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: an opinion and its result — be nazar-e man film khub ast, barâ-ye hamin miravam]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Which joiner points forward to the result? (Barâ-ye hamin, or pas.) Which point back to the cause? (Chon and zirâ.) What does hamin say, taken apart? (This very: ham and in.)


Lesson 3 of 3.
Review — what I think, and what follows.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say "in my opinion", then "I think that". (Be nazar-e man, fekr mikonam ke.)

What you've built.
Two lessons, and an opinion now has both ends. It opens with be nazar-e man or fekr mikonam ke, or carefully with be nazar miresad ke. Its cause comes with chon. Its result comes with barâ-ye hamin, or with pas when you are drawing a conclusion.
[pause 2 seconds]
Think back to the winter trip in chapter two hundred and ninety-one, and give your view of it:
به نظر من سفر خوب بود، چون هتل گرم بود. — be nazar-e man safar khub bud, chon hotel garm bud — In my opinion the trip was good, because the hotel was warm.

[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: vaqti bâ qatâr bargashtim, khaste budim, barâ-ye hamin zud khâbidim]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Which joiner goes on the cause, and which on the result? (Chon on the cause; barâ-ye hamin on the result.) "I don't think so"? (Fekr nemikonam.) Which one draws a conclusion in two letters? (Pas.)
