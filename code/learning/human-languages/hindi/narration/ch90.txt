Hindi, chapter 90: Four Adjectives That Always Agree.
6 lessons. The first lesson already needs your eyes or your hands, so save this one for when you have stopped.


Lesson 1 of 6.
लंबा (lambā) — tall and long are the same word, and this one bends.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, because the lesson points at something written down. You can listen to all of it now and come back to the parts that need looking at once you have stopped.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
The last chapter gave you four adjectives and not one of them changed its ending. Say आसान and remember why it stayed put. This chapter gives you three that do the opposite, and you can see it coming from the spelling.

You'll want to know: लंबा.
लंबा (lambā) — "tall," and also "long."
Hindi does not split those two. A person is lambā, a road is lambā, a film is lambā. English keeps three words for one measurement and chooses between them by what the thing is doing: tall if it stands up, long if it lies down, lengthy if it is made of time. Hindi measures the distance and stops there.

The word, taken apart.
लंबा (lambā) is Sanskrit लम्ब (lamba), "hanging down, drooping." The idea began as what dangles — and a thing that dangles is measured by how far down it reaches.
That root travelled. Latin limbus is a hanging border or hem, and English took it twice:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
word: limb. the hanging thing: what hangs off the trunk.
word: limbo. the hanging thing: the edge, the hem of things.
So लंबा (lambā) and English limb are the same old idea about hanging, arrived at from opposite ends of a continent.

Grammar Lens: this one ends in -आ, so it bends.
Nothing new here — the shape you already learned, meeting a new word:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
shape: ends in -आ. masculine singular: लंबा (lambā). feminine: लंबी.
shape: ends in a consonant. masculine singular: आसान. feminine: आसान.
लंबा ends in -आ, so it behaves like काला: lambā for a man, lambī for a woman, lambe for more than one man.
This is the whole reason the easy-and-difficult lesson and this one belong next to each other. That one gave you four adjectives that never move, and every one of them ended in a consonant. These three all end in -आ, and every one of them moves. Nothing has to be memorised — look at the last letter.

Lesson 2 of 6.
पतला (patlā) — thin, and a root that fell over.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say लंबा (lambā) and its feminine form. The next word has the same ending, so you already know what it will do.

You'll want to know: पतला.
पतला (patlā) — "thin."
It works for a person, for a sheet of paper, and for a soup that has too much water in it. English needs thin, slim, slender and watery for that range; Hindi uses one word and lets the noun decide which English word you would have reached for.

The word, taken apart.
पतला (patlā) goes back to Sanskrit पत्र and the root पत् (pat), "to fall." A thin thing is one that has fallen flat — flattened out until there is hardly anything left of it.
The same root is sitting in two Hindi words you may already recognise:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
word: पत्ता (pattā). the falling in it: a leaf — the thing that falls.
word: patjhaṛ. the falling in it: autumn — literally the leaf-fall.
Autumn in Hindi is named exactly the way American English names it: fall. Two languages that never discussed it reached for the same picture.

Grammar Lens: the second of the three.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
adjective: लंबा (lambā). masculine: लंबा (lambā). feminine: लंबी.
adjective: पतला (patlā). masculine: पतला (patlā). feminine: पतली.
Same ending, same behaviour, no exception to learn. This is what a rule that actually works looks like: you were told the shape, and every new word that has the shape does the thing.

Wrap-up Recall.

Lesson 3 of 6.
मोटा (moṭā) — thick, and the word that refuses to be only about people.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say पतला (patlā) and its feminine. The word in this lesson is its opposite, and it ends the same way.

You'll want to know: मोटा.
मोटा (moṭā) — "thick, stout, heavy."
Set it against पतला (patlā) and the pair covers the whole range at once:
[a table of 4 rows, read aloud]
thing: a person. पतला (patlā): slim. मोटा (moṭā): stout.
thing: a rope. पतला (patlā): thin. मोटा (moṭā): thick.
thing: a book. पतला (patlā): slim. मोटा (moṭā): fat.
thing: a voice. पतला (patlā): reedy. मोटा (moṭā): deep.
That last row is the one to keep. A मोटी आवाज़ is a deep voice, not a fat one — the metaphor runs from the thickness of the string to the lowness of the note, exactly as it does on any instrument.

The word, taken apart.
मोटा (moṭā) is from Sanskrit मूर्त by way of Prakrit, carrying the sense of something solid, with body to it — a shape that has taken form rather than staying an outline.
Hindi extends it to mean rough or approximate, which follows from the same picture: a मोटा (moṭā) estimate is one drawn with a thick line rather than a thin one, so it has no fine detail. English does this with broadly, and the metaphor is identical.

Grammar Lens: all three of them, and the rule holds.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
adjective: लंबा (lambā). masculine: लंबा (lambā). feminine: लंबी.
adjective: पतला (patlā). masculine: पतला (patlā). feminine: पतली.
adjective: मोटा (moṭā). masculine: मोटा (moṭā). feminine: मोटी.
Three words, one ending, one behaviour. Against the four from the last chapter — none of which ended in -आ and none of which moved — that is the cleanest demonstration this book can give you that the ending is the rule.

Lesson 4 of 6.
नाटा (nāṭā) — the gap that a word you already knew could not fill.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
You have लंबा (lambā) for tall. Reach for its opposite using a word you already know, and then read on to find out why that reach does not work.

You'll want to know: नाटा.
नाटा (nāṭā) — "short, of a person's height."
The obvious guess is choṭā, and the obvious guess is wrong — not because it is rude, but because it means something else.

The word, taken apart.
This is the clearest case in the chapter of a word existing because another word is busy:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
word: choṭā. about a thing: small. about a person: younger.
word: नाटा (nāṭā). about a thing: —. about a person: short in height.
Choṭā bhāī is a younger brother regardless of how tall he is. Hindi took the size word for a person's age and left the height meaning without an owner, and नाटा (nāṭā) is what filled the hole.
Sanskrit नत (nata) is behind it, meaning bent, bowed, stooping — the same root as namaste, where the bowing is a greeting. A nāṭā person is pictured as one closer to the ground.
A note on using it. नाटा (nāṭā) is plain rather than polite, and about a person it can land as blunt. When you want neutral, Hindi says qad meṅ choṭā, "short in height" — which spells out the height part that choṭā on its own leaves ambiguous. Recognise नाटा (nāṭā); reach for the longer phrase.

Grammar Lens: the fourth of four, and the rule has not moved.
[a table of 4 rows, read aloud]
adjective: लंबा (lambā). masculine: लंबा (lambā). feminine: लंबी.
adjective: पतला (patlā). masculine: पतला (patlā). feminine: पतली.
adjective: मोटा (moṭā). masculine: मोटा (moṭā). feminine: मोटी.
adjective: नाटा (nāṭā). masculine: नाटा (nāṭā). feminine: नाटी.
Four words for the shape of a person, all four ending in -आ, all four bending. You can now answer a question about anyone's build without once needing to remember which words agree.

Lesson 5 of 6.
chhah ākār — six shapes, and the two that were only ever about things.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Name the four new adjectives with their feminine forms before reading on.

Grammar Lens: which ones a person can be.
You had two size words already, and they were taught about objects. The four new ones are the ones a person gets described with:
[a table of 6 rows, read aloud]
adjective: बड़ा. feminine: बड़ी. used of a person?: as elder, not as large.
adjective: choṭā. feminine: choṭī. used of a person?: as younger, not as small.
adjective: लंबा (lambā). feminine: लंबी. used of a person?: yes — tall.
adjective: नाटा (nāṭā). feminine: नाटी. used of a person?: yes — short in height.
adjective: पतला (patlā). feminine: पतली. used of a person?: yes — thin.
adjective: मोटा (moṭā). feminine: मोटी. used of a person?: yes — stout.
The first two rows are the ones worth pausing on. बड़ा and choṭā applied to a person almost always mean older and younger, not big and small — baṛā bhāī is an elder brother, whatever his height. So the track could size a box long before it could describe a body, which is why these four were needed — and why nāṭā had to be a separate word rather than a reuse of choṭā.
All six end in -आ. All six bend. That is not a coincidence: size and shape words are old inherited vocabulary, and inherited Hindi adjectives are the ones that kept the ending.

Lesson 6 of 6.
वह लंबी है (vah lambī hai) — the sentence that makes you choose the ending.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say लंबा (lambā) and पतला (patlā). In a list they sit still. In a sentence about a real person they will not.

Grammar Lens: the ending is chosen by who you are talking about.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
about: a man. sentence: वह लंबा (lambā) है. the ending: -आ.
about: a woman. sentence: वह लंबी है (vah lambī hai). the ending: -ई.
वह does not change. है does not change. The adjective carries the whole load, which is the opposite of English, where he and she do the work and tall never moves.
That is worth saying plainly: in English the pronoun tells you the gender and the adjective is inert. In Hindi the pronoun is silent about it and the adjective tells you. A listener who misses the ending has missed the information.

Reading.
Read aloud. Every word has been taught:
वह लंबी है (vah lambī hai)। वह पतली है। वह लंबा (lambā) है। वह मोटा (moṭā) है। वह नाटा (nāṭā) है।.
Five sentences, two people, and the only thing telling you which is which is the last letter of the adjective.
