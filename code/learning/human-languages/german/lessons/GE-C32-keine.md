# keine — the same word, in front of a *die*

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GE-LEX-KEIN-01, GE-LEX-EIN-EINE-01, GE-GRAMMAR-PLURAL-DIE-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] *Kein Hund.* Now do the same to **eine Katze** before you read on.
You already know the answer; you learned it on *ein* and *eine* long ago.

## You'll want to know: keine
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[GE-LEX-KEINE-01]; assesses=[GE-LEX-KEIN-01, GE-LEX-EIN-EINE-01, GE-LEX-KATZE-04, GE-LEX-MILCH-02] -->

> **keine** — the shape *kein* takes in front of a *die*-word.

> **eine Katze** → **keine Katze** — "a cat" → "no cat"

> **eine Nacht** → **keine Nacht** — "a night" → "no night"

And it is the shape in front of a **plural**, whatever the noun's own gender,
because a German plural takes *die*:

> **keine Hände** — "no hands"

The extra **-e** is not a new ending. It is *eine*'s **-e**, unmoved. Put the
*k* on the front of the word you already say and stop there.

## Grammar Lens: one word cancels the whole family
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GE-LEX-KEINE-01, GE-LEX-KEIN-01, GE-LEX-DER-DIE-DAS-02, GE-LEX-MILCH-02, GE-ETYMON-MILCH-04] -->

Something worth noticing before the next lesson complicates it: German gets
**two** jobs out of these two shapes. *Keine Katze* is "no cat" — one cat, and
there is not one. *Keine Hände* is "no hands" — however many there might have
been, there are none.

English needs *no* for the first and *no* for the second and *not any* for a
third case, and never had to decide which; German lets the noun's own article
decide it, so there is nothing extra to remember.

Mass words take *kein* and *keine* too, on the same reasoning — **die Milch**
is a *die*-word, so:

> **Ich habe keine Milch.** — "I have no milk."
