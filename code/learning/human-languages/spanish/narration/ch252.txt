Spanish, chapter 252: Ahora, Hoy --- Now and Today.
2 lessons. All 2 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 2.
ahora — now.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Mañana — tomorrow. La hora — the hour.
[pause 1 second]
One more time word, and it is hiding something you already know.

The word, taken apart: ahora.
Ahora — now. Look at what is sitting in the middle of it: hora, the word for hour you learned long ago.
[pause 2 seconds]
Latin said hac hora — at this hour. Two words, worn together into one, and the h in the middle went silent along the way. Now is literally this hour. Spanish is full of these — phrases that stopped being phrases.

Why it's said this way.
[pause 2 seconds]
And now you can put a time on the tense you learned for the moment:
Ahora estoy comiendo. — Right now, I'm eating.
[pause 2 seconds]
Notice which tense it takes. Ahora goes with the progressive, because both mean at this instant — the word and the tense are saying the same thing about the clock, which is why they sit together so comfortably.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: ahora, then mañana]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: Ahora estoy comiendo]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[repeat that twice]
[question — say your answer, then pause 13 seconds]
Say: right now I am eating.
[question — say your answer, then pause 12 seconds]
Which word you already knew is hiding inside ahora?
[question — say your answer, then pause 11 seconds]
Which of ahora and manana is the one you are inside of?

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 2 seconds]
Ahora was which two Latin words? (Hac hora — this hour.)
[pause 2 seconds]
And how do you say "right now I am eating"? (Ahora estoy comiendo.) Next: the same trick, worn down even further, in a word of three letters.


Lesson 2 of 2.
hoy — today.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Ahora was hac hora — at this hour, two Latin words worn into one.
[pause 1 second]
Here is the same wearing-down, gone further.

The word, taken apart.
Hoy — today. Latin said hodie, which was itself hoc die, this day.
[pause 2 seconds]
So the pair is symmetrical, and neither has parts any more: this hour wore down to ahora, this day wore down to three letters. A phrase became a word, and then the word became short enough that nobody hears the phrase in it.
[pause 2 seconds]
Set the three against each other and your day has a shape: hoy, the day you are in; ahora, the instant inside it; mañana, the one that has not come.

Why it's said this way.
[pause 2 seconds]
Ahora pulls the progressive, because both mean at this instant. Hoy covers a whole day, so it takes the ordinary forms:
Hoy voy a comer con ella. — Today I'm going to eat with her.
[pause 2 seconds]
That is the going-to future, which is how Spanish says the thing English says with I'm eating. A day is wide enough to hold something that has not happened yet, and an instant is not.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: ahora, hoy, mañana]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: Hoy voy a comer con ella]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[repeat that twice]
[question — say your answer, then pause 10 seconds]
The word for today?
[question — say your answer, then pause 13 seconds]
Which Latin word wore down into hoy, and what did it mean?
[question — say your answer, then pause 15 seconds]
Say: today I am going to eat with her.

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 2 seconds]
Hoy was which Latin word? (Hodie, which was this day.)
[pause 2 seconds]
And which of your three time words is the widest? (Hoy — a whole day, with ahora somewhere inside it.)
