Latin, chapter 494: Hatred and disgust.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
ōdī, ōdisse — to hate.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for unworthy, then the Latin for to be indignant.

You'll want to know: ōdī, ōdisse.
ōdī, ōdisse — "to hate".
Fēlēs aquam ōdit. The cat hates water.
Perfect in form, present in meaning: ōdī — I hate, ōderam — I hated, ōderō — I shall hate. Future participle ōsūrus.

The word, taken apart.
ōdī is inherited, through Proto-Italic ōdai, from a Proto-Indo-European root meaning "to hate" (EtymDB). The noun odium "hatred", source of English odium and odious, is built on it.

Lesson 2 of 5.
odiōsus, odiōsa, odiōsum — hateful, tiresome.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to be indignant, then the Latin for to hate.

You'll want to know: odiōsus, odiōsa, odiōsum.
odiōsus, odiōsa, odiōsum — "hateful, tiresome".
Iter longum et odiōsum fuit. The journey was long and tiresome.
Built on odium — hatred. In everyday Latin it often means no more than annoying. English odious.

The word, taken apart.
odiōsus is built on odium "hatred" with the ending -ōsus "full of" (Lewis & Short; EtymDB). English odious comes from it.

Lesson 4 of 5.
fastīdium, fastīdiī — disgust, squeamishness; disdain.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for hateful, then the Latin for to turn up one's nose at.

You'll want to know: fastīdium, fastīdiī.
fastīdium, fastīdiī — "disgust, squeamishness; disdain".
Aeger cibum cum fastīdiō spectat. The sick man looks at the food with disgust.
Neuter, second declension. Disgust of the stomach, and the scorn of a snob.

The word, taken apart.
fastīdium is a native Latin word; Lewis & Short compares fastus "pride", but the origin is not settled. English fastidious comes from fastīdiōsus, built on it.

Lesson 5 of 5.
taedet, taedēre — it wearies, it disgusts.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to turn up one's nose at, then the Latin for disgust.

You'll want to know: taedet, taedēre.
taedet, taedēre — "it wearies, it disgusts".
Mē taedet longārum ōrātiōnum. I am weary of long speeches.
Impersonal: taedet, taedēre, taeduit or taesum est. The person in the accusative, the thing in the genitive: mē taedet labōris — I am sick of the work. Taedium, already known, is built on it.

The word, taken apart.
taedet is a native Latin impersonal verb, and the noun taedium "weariness" is built on it (Lewis & Short); its deeper origin is not settled. English tedium and tedious come from taedium.
