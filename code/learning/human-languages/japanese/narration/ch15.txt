This chapter does not start at six. Six is one of only two numbers left that costs a new sign, and both of those wait until the end.

You'll Want to Know — hear and say.
なな — nana — seven.
しち — shichi — seven, the same number said the other way.
All three signs are yours: な, し, ち.

What you've built — the same seam, a second time.
Wiktionary's readings box for the kanji seven says exactly what the box for four said:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
number: four. borrowed reading: し shi. native word: よん yon.
number: seven. borrowed reading: しち shichi. native word: なな nana.
Twice now, and both times at a number where the borrowed reading is easy to mishear — shi against ichi, shichi against ichi. The native word is the one that keeps the count clear, and it is the one you will hear.
One instance was a fact about four. Two is a pattern, and this is where the book says so.

Guided Practice — read, copy, recall.
Write なな. Say what you have: ichi, ni, san, yon, go … nana. Leave the hole where six is; you will hear it.

One mora, one sign, and you have had that sign since chapter nine, where it closed よく. Say hachi, ku.
Nine has a second everyday reading as well, kyū, and it is the commoner of the two. This book cannot spell it yet — it needs two signs you have not met — so it is written here in romanization only and will be spelled when those signs arrive.

What you've built — a second reading that is not a native word.
Four and seven each had two names, and in both the second was the native word. Nine has two names too, and Wiktionary's readings box puts both of them on the borrowed side:
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
number: four. readings the box calls borrowed: し. the native word: よん — used.
number: seven. readings the box calls borrowed: しち. the native word: なな (nana) — used.
number: nine. readings the box calls borrowed: く (ku), kyū. the native word: kokono — not used in the plain count.
So the pattern is narrower than it looked two lessons ago: some numbers have two everyday names, and only some of those get their second name from the native side. Nine has two names for a different reason.
You met the plain case at eight. This is the other edge.

Script — the shape.
ろ — ro.
Write ら once. ろ starts the same way and ends differently: a short stroke across the top, then down and round to the left and back up, closing the loop instead of leaving a hook.
The sound is the one ら already taught you — a single tap of the tongue, neither the English r nor the English l.
This is the first new sign in two chapters, and it is here for one word.

Before we start: this one needs your hands, so it is not a driving lesson. You will want a pen and something to write on and your eyes, for letter shapes on the page. [your turn — recall: write ゆ, full size — R2, five lessons back]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — recall: say my husband — R3, twenty lessons back]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — recall: write く (ku) — R4, eighty lessons back]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Script — the shape.
ゅ — small yu.
Same shape as ゆ, written small and sitting low — the way っ is a small つ.
The two small signs do opposite jobs, and it is worth putting them side by side:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
sign: っ. what it does: holds a silent beat before the next consonant.
sign: ゅ (small yu). what it does: joins to the sign before it, and the two become one beat.
So し plus ゅ (small yu) is not shi-yu. It is one beat, shu. Voice the し with the dakuten first and じ plus ゅ (small yu) is one beat, ju.
That is the sign the last number needs.

Guided Practice.
Write ゆ, then ゅ (small yu) beneath it, half the height.
Write っ and ゅ (small yu) side by side; say which one holds and which one joins.
Read じゅ as a single beat. Do not let a yu escape between the two.

Wrap-up Recall.
Stop after one accurate small form.
Source: Unicode Hiragana chart.

You'll Want to Know — hear and say.
じゅう — jū — ten.
Three signs, two beats. し voiced with the dakuten is じ; じ with the small ゅ (small yu) is one beat, ju; the う after it holds that beat long. Say it as two beats, not three: ju–u, not ji-yu-u.

Grammar Lens — ten words, ninety-nine numbers.
じゅう (jū) is not just the tenth word in a list. It is the one that makes the list go further than itself, and it does so with no joining word at all:
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
what you say: じゅういち. what it is: ten, one becomes eleven.
what you say: にじゅう. what it is: two, ten becomes twenty.
what you say: にじゅういち. what it is: two, ten, one becomes twenty-one.
A number before じゅう (jū) multiplies it. A number after じゅう (jū) is added to it. Nothing is inserted between them.
Ten words, and every number up to ninety-nine is one of their arrangements. That is what the last two chapters were for.

Guided Practice — read, copy, recall.
Write じゅう. Say the whole run, one to ten, without stopping. Then say jūichi, nijū, sanjū, and build one number of your own between twenty and ninety-nine.

Wrap-up Recall.
