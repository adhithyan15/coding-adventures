## You'll want to know: copiar
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-C472-ENCUESTA-05]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C472-ENCUESTA-01] -->

**copiar** — to copy.

> Tres bibliotecas han **copiado** el sistema.

*Copiar un documento*, *copiar a alguien*. The noun is **la copia**, a copy —
and note that it is feminine, where English has no gender to keep track of.

*Copiar en un examen* is to cheat, so the verb is not always neutral; context
does the work.

## Grammar Lens: copying a thing and copying a person
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C472-ENCUESTA-05, ES-LEX-C472-ENCUESTA-01] -->

The preposition changes what you are claiming.

| what you say | what it means |
|---|---|
| copiar **el sistema** | reproduce the thing |
| copiar **a** otra biblioteca | imitate the one who did it |

*Han copiado el sistema* is a compliment about a method. *Han copiado a otra
biblioteca* puts another institution in the sentence and can sound like a
charge of unoriginality.

Three libraries copying a system is a success story. The same three copying
*a* somebody is a different report altogether, and a reading question will
sometimes turn on which one the text said.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C472-ENCUESTA-05] -->

Latin **copia** — **abundance**, plenty. Not *a duplicate*: the word for
plenty.

The sense travelled because to copy a manuscript was to make there be **more
of it**. A medieval scribe producing a second manuscript was making *copia*,
abundance, and the name of the result eventually became the name of the act.

The old meaning is still in English **copious**, meaning plentiful, and in
**cornucopia**, the horn of plenty. Set those beside *una copia* and the
journey is visible: from having a lot, to having another one.
