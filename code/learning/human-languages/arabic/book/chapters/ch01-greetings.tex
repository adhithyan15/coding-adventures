\chapter{Greetings}
\label{ch:greetings}
\hlchaptermodality{1}

The Arabic greetings --- and, taught through them, how to read and write the
Arabic script and how the three-consonant \textbf{root} builds nearly every
word. The sound and meaning always come first. A written form appears only
after tiny observe-and-trace steps have introduced at most three new shapes.

\section*{Three facts about the script, and one about the roots}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Right to left.} The first letter of a word is on the
    \emph{right}.
  \item \textbf{Letters connect} (cursive-style), so a letter's shape shifts
    a little by position; a few are ``one-way'' --- they join the letter before
    but not the one after.
  \item \textbf{Short vowels are usually invisible.} Long vowels (\emph{\=a
    \=\i\ \=u}) are full letters; the short \emph{a/i/u} between consonants are
    simply known, not written.
\end{enumerate}

And the engine of the whole language: most Arabic words are a \textbf{root} of
three consonants carrying a core meaning, poured into fixed vowel
\textbf{patterns}. The classic is \textbf{k--t--b} (writing): \emph{kataba} (he
wrote), \emph{kit\=ab} (book), \emph{k\=atib} (writer), \emph{maktab} (office
$=$ ``a place of writing''), \emph{maktaba} (library) --- six words, one root.
Every greeting below is a root plus a pattern; watch the roots surface.

% canonical-insertion: AR-C01-salam
\section{\emph{sal\=am} --- say ``peace'' before meeting the page}
\label{lesson:salam}
\label{lesson:AR-C01-salam}

\begin{sounds}
Keep your eyes off the script for one lesson. Say \emph{sa--l\=am}, holding the
second vowel a little longer. The final sound is a clean \emph{m}. The next two
micro-lessons will reveal the spelling two shapes at a time.
\end{sounds}

\begin{cousinweb}
\emph{sal\=am}, ``peace.'' Root \textbf{s--l--m}
(\emph{wholeness, safety, peace}) --- an enormous family: \emph{sal\=am}
(peace), \emph{isl\=am} (submission $\to$ peace with God), \emph{muslim} (``one
who submits,'' the \emph{mu--\,-i-} pattern), \emph{sallama} (to greet). It
reaches past Arabic: Hebrew \textbf{shalom} is the \emph{same} Semitic root.
English meets it as the borrowing \emph{salaam} and the name \emph{Sal\=im}.
\end{cousinweb}

\begin{culture}
On its own, \emph{sal\=am} is a warm ``peace'' --- and the seed of the full
greeting \emph{as-sal\=amu `alaykum}, coming shortly. To say it is to wish the
other person peace, not merely to signal ``hello.''
\end{culture}

Only after saying and understanding the greeting, take one writing microstep:
\ar{س} is \emph{s\=\i n}, the opening \emph{s}. Notice its three small teeth
and trailing bowl. Trace it once, hide it, then air-write the three teeth. Do
not decode or copy the complete word yet.

% canonical-insertion: AR-W00-sin-lam
\section{\ar{س ل} --- two shapes after the sound}
\label{lesson:ar-w00-sin-lam}
\label{lesson:AR-W00-sin-lam}

Arabic runs \textbf{right to left}. Recall one shape and add only one:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \ar{س} is \emph{s\=\i n}, the \emph{s} retrieved from lesson one.
  \item \ar{ل} is \emph{l\=am}, the \emph{l}: a tall upright and low curve.
\end{itemize}
Trace each once with a finger. Hide the models, air-write the three teeth of
\emph{s\=\i n}, then the tall \emph{l\=am}. Finally write \ar{س ل} once, still
separate. Nothing else is due yet.

% canonical-insertion: AR-W00-alif-mim-salam
\section{\ar{ا م} --- finish \ar{سلام} without a cliff}
\label{lesson:ar-w00-alif-mim-salam}
\label{lesson:AR-W00-alif-mim-salam}

Add just two shapes: \ar{ا}, \emph{alif}, is one upright and carries long
\emph{\=a} here; \ar{م}, \emph{m\=\i m}, is a round head with a tail. Now point
right to left through \ar{س · ل · ا · م}. Trace \ar{سلام} once, copy it once
while looking, then cover the model and write it once from memory. Compare and
correct one feature. The pen lifts after \emph{alif}, which does not join
forward.

% canonical-insertion: AR-C01-marhaba
\section{\emph{mar\d{h}aban} --- ``there is room for you''}
\label{lesson:marhaba}
\label{lesson:AR-C01-marhaba}

\begin{sounds}
Hear three small beats before looking at the spelling: \emph{mar--\d{h}a--ban}.
The middle consonant is made deeper in the throat than English \emph{h}; a
gentle approximation is enough.
\end{sounds}

\begin{cousinweb}
\ar{مرحبا} (\emph{mar\d{h}aban}), the casual ``hi.'' Root \textbf{r--\d{h}--b}
(\emph{spaciousness}): \emph{ra\d{h}uba} (to be wide), \emph{ra\d{h}b} (roomy)
$\to$ \emph{mar\d{h}aban} $=$ ``[a place of] welcome, there is room for you.''
Not an empty sound but a little sentence --- as \emph{buenos d\'{i}as} was once
a blessing. Reply: \emph{mar\d{h}abtayn}, ``two welcomes.''
\end{cousinweb}

% canonical-insertion: AR-W00-ra-ha
\section{\ar{ر ح} --- two shapes inside \emph{mar\d{h}aban}}
\label{lesson:ar-w00-ra-ha}
\label{lesson:AR-W00-ra-ha}

\ar{ر}, \emph{r\=aʾ}, curves down and left and refuses to join forward.
\ar{ح}, \emph{\d{h}\=aʾ}, is the deep consonant's open, dotless bowl. Trace
each once; cover them; write both once from memory and compare.

% canonical-insertion: AR-W00-ba-family-marhaba
\section{\ar{ب ت ث} --- one bowl, three dot choices}
\label{lesson:ar-w00-ba-family-marhaba}
\label{lesson:AR-W00-ba-family-marhaba}

One shared bowl gives exactly three new shapes: \ar{ب} has one dot below,
\ar{ت} has two above, and \ar{ث} has three above. The dots decide the letter;
they are not decoration. The greeting uses \ar{ب}. Point right to left through
\ar{م · ر · ح · ب · ا}, copy \ar{مرحبا} once, then cover it and write it once
from memory. Check especially that \ar{ب} kept its dot below.

\section{\ar{ال} \emph{(al-)} --- ``the,'' hiding in English \emph{algebra}}
\label{lesson:al}

\begin{sounds}
No new letters --- just \ar{ا} (alif) and \ar{ل} (l\=am), the two you already
read in \emph{sal\=am}, written together and attached to the front of a noun.
\end{sounds}

\begin{cousinweb}
\ar{ال} (\emph{al-}, ``the''). For $\sim$800 years Arabic was the
Mediterranean's language of science and trade, and English and Spanish absorbed
its words \emph{with al- attached}: \textbf{al}gebra (\emph{al-jabr}),
\textbf{al}cohol (\emph{al-ku\d{h}l}), \textbf{al}gorithm
(\emph{al-Khw\=arizm\=\i}), \textbf{al}chemy, \textbf{al}manac. Learning Arabic
\emph{al-} is meeting an old friend.
\end{cousinweb}

\begin{grammarlens}
\textbf{Sun letters.} Before certain consonants (\emph{s, sh, n, r, t, d}\ldots) the
\emph{l} of \emph{al-} assimilates and the letter doubles: \emph{al-}\,$+$\,%
\emph{sal\=am} $\to$ \emph{as-sal\=am}; \emph{al-}\,$+$\,\emph{n\=ur} $\to$
\emph{an-n\=ur}. Before ``moon letters'' (\emph{kh, k, b, m}\ldots) the
\emph{l} stays: \emph{al-khayr}. You can hear the rule inside
Spanish \emph{az\'{u}car} (from \emph{as-sukkar}).
\end{grammarlens}

% canonical-insertion: AR-C01-as-salamu-alaykum
\section{\emph{as-sal\=amu `alaykum} --- offer the full greeting by ear}
\label{lesson:as-salamu-alaykum}
\label{lesson:AR-C01-as-salamu-alaykum}

\begin{sounds}
Say two blocks before looking at the full line: \emph{as-sal\=amu}, then
\emph{`alaykum}. Begin the second with a light throat-tightening; an
approximation is enough today. The next two micro-lessons reveal its remaining
shapes in pairs.
\end{sounds}

\emph{as-sal\=amu `alaykum} $=$ \emph{as-sal\=amu}
(\emph{al-}\,$+$\,\emph{sal\=am}, sun-letter \emph{s} assimilated) $+$
\emph{`alaykum} (``upon you [all]'').
Literally ``the peace [be] upon you'' --- and note there is \textbf{no word for
``is''}: Arabic sets the two phrases side by side and lets ``is'' be understood
(where Spanish needs \emph{est\'{a}}).

\begin{culture}
The universal greeting; its reply is fixed: \emph{wa-`alaykum as-sal\=am} ---
``\emph{and} upon you the peace.'' You give
peace; it is returned. Any time, any register, real weight --- you are wishing
\emph{peace}, not saying ``hi.''
\end{culture}

% canonical-insertion: AR-W00-ayn-ya
\section{\ar{ع ي} --- open \emph{`alaykum} gently}
\label{lesson:ar-w00-ayn-ya}
\label{lesson:AR-W00-ayn-ya}

Only after speaking the greeting do you meet two shapes from its second block.
\ar{ع}, \emph{`ayn}, is the deep opening sound; \ar{ي}, \emph{y\=aʾ}, carries
two dots below when isolated. Trace each once, hide both, and write them once
from memory.

% canonical-insertion: AR-W00-kaf-waw-alaykum
\section{\ar{ك و} --- complete the greeting after the sound}
\label{lesson:ar-w00-kaf-waw-alaykum}
\label{lesson:AR-W00-kaf-waw-alaykum}

Add two final shapes: \ar{ك}, \emph{k\=af}, gives the \emph{k} in
\emph{`alaykum}; \ar{و}, \emph{w\=aw}, gives the reply's opening \emph{wa-},
``and.'' Find the familiar peace word inside \ar{السلام عليكم}; point through
\ar{عليكم} right to left; then copy the full greeting once. Cover it and write
\ar{عليكم} from memory. Finally add \ar{و} to begin the reply: \ar{وعليكم}.

% canonical-insertion: AR-W00-full-greeting-recall
\section{\emph{as-sal\=amu `alaykum} --- hear, write, repair}
\label{lesson:ar-w00-full-greeting-recall}
\label{lesson:AR-W00-full-greeting-recall}

Cover every Arabic model. Hear \emph{as-sal\=amu `alaykum}, then write the
greeting from memory. Reveal \ar{السلام عليكم}; compare from right to left;
circle one uncertain shape and repair it once. Stop there. Retrieval, not
mindless repetition, is the purpose of this three-minute lesson.

\section{\ar{صباح الخير} \emph{(\d{s}ab\=a\d{h} al-khayr)} --- ``morning of goodness''}
\label{lesson:sabah-al-khayr}

\begin{sounds}
Two new letters: \ar{ص} (\emph{\d{s}\=ad}, an \emph{emphatic}/heavy ``s'' --- a
different letter from \ar{س}) and \ar{خ} (\emph{kh\=aʾ}, a throat-scrape, the
same sound as \textbf{Spanish \emph{j}}). With the rest known, read \ar{صباح}
(\emph{\d{s}ab\=a\d{h}}, ``morning'') and \ar{خير} (\emph{khayr}, ``goodness'').
\end{sounds}

\begin{cousinweb}
\ar{صباح} (\emph{\d{s}ab\=a\d{h}}), root \textbf{\d{s}--b--\d{h}} (dawn/waking:
\emph{\d{s}ub\d{h}} dawn, \emph{a\d{s}ba\d{h}a} ``to become, reach morning'').
\ar{خير} (\emph{khayr}, ``good, goodness''), root \textbf{kh--y--r}. Together
\ar{صباح الخير} $=$ ``morning of the goodness'' --- \emph{al-khayr} keeps its
\emph{l} (moon letter \ar{خ}), a clean contrast with \emph{as-sal\=am}.
\end{cousinweb}

\begin{culture}
Reply: \ar{صباح النور} (\emph{\d{s}ab\=a\d{h} an-n\=ur}) --- ``morning of the
\emph{light}'' (\emph{al-}\,$+$\,\emph{n\=ur}, sun-letter \emph{n} assimilated).
You wish goodness; they wish you light.
\end{culture}

\section{\ar{مساء الخير} \emph{(mas\=aʾ al-khayr)} --- ``evening of goodness''}
\label{lesson:masa-al-khayr}

\begin{sounds}
One new mark: \ar{ء} (\emph{hamza}), a glottal stop --- the catch in the middle
of ``uh-oh.'' At the end of \ar{مساء} it gives \emph{mas\=aʾ}. Everything else
here you can already read.
\end{sounds}

\begin{cousinweb}
\ar{مساء} (\emph{mas\=aʾ}, ``evening''), root \textbf{m--s--w}: \emph{ams\=a}
``to become, reach evening'' --- the exact mirror of \emph{a\d{s}ba\d{h}a}.
Arabic names morning and evening as matched events (each with its own ``to
become'' verb), where French \emph{soir} and Spanish \emph{tarde} both merely
meant ``late.'' \ar{مساء الخير} $=$ ``evening of goodness''; reply
\ar{مساء النور} (\emph{mas\=aʾ an-n\=ur}).
\end{cousinweb}

\section{\ar{شكرا} \emph{(shukran)} --- ``thank you''}
\label{lesson:shukran}

\begin{sounds}
Two new letters: \ar{ش} (\emph{sh\=\i n}, ``sh'' --- it is \ar{س} \emph{plus
three dots}) and \ar{ك} (\emph{k\=af}, ``k,'' met in \emph{`alaykum}). Read
\ar{شكرا} right to left $\to$ \emph{shukran}.
\end{sounds}

\begin{cousinweb}
\ar{شكرا} (\emph{shukran}), root \textbf{sh--k--r} (\emph{gratitude}):
\emph{shukr} (thanks), \emph{sh\=akir} (thankful), \emph{mashk\=ur} (thanked).
Look: \emph{sh\=akir} and \emph{mashk\=ur} sit in the \emph{exact} patterns as
\emph{k\=atib} (writer) and \emph{makt\=ub} (written) from the root engine ---
learn a pattern once, it works on every root. (This same root, through Persian,
gave Hindi/Urdu \emph{shukriy\=a}.) Reply: \ar{عفوا} (\emph{`afwan}), ``it's
nothing.''
\end{cousinweb}

\section{The day of greetings}
\label{lesson:ar-c01-practice}
\label{lesson:AR-C01-practice}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
When & Greeting & Reply \\
\midrule
casual & \emph{mar\d{h}aban} & \emph{mar\d{h}abtayn} \\
any time & \emph{as-sal\=amu `alaykum} & \emph{wa-`alaykum as-sal\=am} \\
morning & \emph{\d{s}ab\=a\d{h} al-khayr} & \emph{\d{s}ab\=a\d{h} an-n\=ur} \\
evening & \emph{mas\=aʾ al-khayr} & \emph{mas\=aʾ an-n\=ur} \\

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Read these back right to left before checking: \ar{سلام}, \ar{مرحبا},
\ar{السلام عليكم}, \ar{صباح الخير}, \ar{مساء الخير}, \ar{شكرا}. About a dozen
letters, each learned inside a real greeting. Two engines did the work: the
three-consonant \textbf{root} (s-l-m, sh-k-r) and the attached \textbf{al-} with
its sun/moon assimilation. Next chapter: introducing yourself
(\emph{ism\=\i\ldots}, ``my name is'') and how Arabic marks ``you'' by gender
(\emph{anta} / \emph{anti}).

For the writing checkpoint, hear \emph{sal\=am}, hide the model, and write
\ar{سلام}. Reveal the model, compare every shape and connector, and repair one
feature. The goal is a recoverable message, not perfect calligraphy.
