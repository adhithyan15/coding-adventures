Marathi, chapter 41: A House, a Room, and the Stem That Bends.
4 lessons. All 4 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 4.
घर (ghar) — house, home.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
You can point at a place — इथे, तिथे — and you cannot yet NAME one.
[your turn — recall: say ithe, then tithe, then ask kuṭhe?]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

You'll want to know: घर.
घर — ghar — house, home.
माझं घर इथे आहे. — mājhaṁ ghar ithe āhe. — my house is here.
Two letters you have had since chapter six, and a word Marathi uses for both house and home — the building and the belonging, with no second word for the difference.
It comes from Sanskrit गृह (gṛha), and गृह is built on the root ग्रह् (grah-), "to grasp, to hold." You already own that root: it is the one under घेणे, to take. A house, in Marathi's oldest layer, is the thing that holds you.
घर (ghar) is neuter, like कुटुंब. That will matter the moment you put an adjective on it, and it matters now for माझं rather than माझा.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: ghar — house, home]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: mājhaṁ ghar ithe āhe]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — recall: say की and report that your house is here — mī mhaṇto kī …]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[question — say your answer, then pause 22 seconds]
Say that your house is here, then that it is there.

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Which taught verb shares a root with घर (ghar)? (घेणे — both from grah-, "to hold".) What gender is घर (ghar)? (Neuter.)
Sources: Wiktionary: घर (ghar).


Lesson 2 of 4.
खोली (khoolī) — room.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
A house has an inside. That inside needs a name before anything can be said to be in it.

You'll want to know: खोली.
खोली — khoolī — room.
माझ्या घरात एक खोली आहे. — mājhyā gharāt ek khoolī āhe. — there is one room in my house.
खोली (khoolī) is feminine, which its -ी ending announces before you are told. It is the word a rental notice uses, the word a hotel uses, and the word you would use for the room you sleep in — Marathi does not have a separate everyday noun for bedroom the way English does; it says the room you do the sleeping in.
The word is the noun of खोल (khool), "deep." A room is a depth cut into a house. Once you know that pair you also know a second, unrelated-looking word: खोली (khoolī) on its own can mean depth, which is the same noun doing its literal job.
That sentence above did something new to घर, and you should notice it now rather than be surprised by it: घर became घरा- (gharā-) before -त. The next lesson is about nothing else.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: khoolī — room]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: mājhaṁ ghar; ek khoolī]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — recall: say -ण्यासाठी and give a purpose for a room — jhopaṇyāsāṭhī]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[question — say your answer, then pause 20 seconds]
Say: my house, and one room.

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
What gender is खोली (khoolī), and what tells you? (Feminine; the -ी.) What adjective is it built on? (खोल, deep.)
Sources: Wiktionary: खोली (khoolī).


Lesson 3 of 4.
घरा- (gharā-) — the stem a noun takes before a postposition.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
घर (ghar) became घरात last lesson, and पुणे became पुण्यात back in chapter twelve. Neither one just took an ending. Both changed shape first.

Grammar Lens: the noun bends before anything is glued to it.
Marathi puts almost every place, time and direction relation on a postposition — a piece glued to the END of the noun. Chapter twelve showed you one. What it did not say is that the noun does not stay as it is: it first takes an oblique stem, and only then accepts the glue.
घर becomes घरा- (gharā-) becomes घरात पुणे becomes पुण्या- becomes पुण्यात खोली (khoolī) becomes खोली (khoolī)- becomes खोलीत.
Three classes, three answers, and the pattern is worth holding as a shape rather than a list. A neuter noun ending in a consonant takes -आ. A noun ending in -ए takes -या. A feminine noun in -ी does not change at all.
This is the single most load-bearing rule in the book so far, because nothing in the next three chapters works without it. Every one of the five postpositions they teach — madhye, var, khālī, javaḷ and samor — demands the oblique before it, and a Marathi sentence that skips it is not merely clumsy: it is wrong.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: ghar, then gharāt — and hear the extra ā]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: khoolī, then khoolīt — and hear that nothing was added]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: mī gharāt rāhto — I live in the house]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[question — say your answer, then pause 25 seconds]
Put ghar and khoolī into the -t locative and say which one changed shape.

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
What happens to a Marathi noun before a postposition? (It takes an oblique stem first.) Which of the three classes changes least? (Feminine -ी — not at all.)
