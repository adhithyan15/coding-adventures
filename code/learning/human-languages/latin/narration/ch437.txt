Lesson 1 of 5.
rudēns, rudentis — a ship's rope, a line.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a rowing bench, then the Latin for for cargo.

You'll want to know: rudēns, rudentis.
rudēns, rudentis — "a ship's rope, a line".
Nautae rudentēs trahunt et vēlum tollunt. The sailors pull the ropes and raise the sail.
Third declension, masculine: rudēns, rudentis m. The ropes of the rigging.

The word, taken apart.
The origin of rudēns is not settled: Lewis & Short call it unknown (ancient writers linked it with rudō 'to roar', from the creaking of the ropes), and EtymDB lists it without a parent.

Lesson 2 of 5.
fūnis, fūnis — a rope, a cord.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for for cargo, then the Latin for a ship's rope.

You'll want to know: fūnis, fūnis.
fūnis, fūnis — "a rope, a cord".
Nāvis fūne ad lītus ligātur. The ship is tied to the shore with a rope.
Third declension, usually masculine: fūnis, fūnis m. English funicular, a railway pulled by a rope, comes from it.

The word, taken apart.
fūnis is inherited and is usually traced to PIE bʰendʰ- 'to bind, to tie' (EtymDB); Lewis & Short also suggest, with a perhaps, the Sanskrit root for binding. English funicular comes from its diminutive fūniculus.

Lesson 3 of 5.
armāmenta, armāmentōrum — a ship's tackle, the rigging; equipment.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a ship's rope, then the Latin for a rope.

You'll want to know: armāmenta, armāmentōrum.
armāmenta, armāmentōrum — "a ship's tackle, the rigging; equipment".
Procella armāmenta nāvis frangit. The storm breaks the ship's rigging.
Second declension, neuter plural: armāmenta, armāmentōrum n. Masts, sails and ropes together. English armament comes from it.

The word, taken apart.
armāmenta is built on arma 'gear, equipment' (Lewis & Short) with the suffix -mentum. English armament comes from it.

Lesson 4 of 5.
gubernāculum, gubernāculī — a rudder, a steering oar.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a rope, then the Latin for a ship's tackle.

You'll want to know: gubernāculum, gubernāculī.
gubernāculum, gubernāculī — "a rudder, a steering oar".
Gubernātor gubernāculum tenet et nāvem ad portum dūcit. The helmsman holds the rudder and guides the ship to harbour.
Second declension, neuter: gubernāculum, gubernāculī n. Also the helm of the state. English govern comes from the verb gubernāre.

The word, taken apart.
gubernāculum is built on gubernō 'to steer' (Lewis & Short), which was borrowed from Greek kybernaō. English govern comes from gubernō through French.

Lesson 5 of 5.
rēmigō, rēmigāre — to row.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a ship's tackle, then the Latin for a rudder.

You'll want to know: rēmigō, rēmigāre.
rēmigō, rēmigāre — "to row".
Ventus nūllus est; itaque nautae rēmigant. There is no wind; so the sailors row.
A first-conjugation verb: rēmigō, rēmigāre; the perfect is rare. The rower is rēmex, rēmigis.

The word, taken apart.
rēmigō is built from rēmus 'oar' and agō 'to drive' (EtymDB; Lewis & Short): to drive the oars.
