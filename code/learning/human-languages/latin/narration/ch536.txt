Latin, chapter 536: Charm and sweet words.
6 lessons. All 6 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 6.
blandus, blanda, blandum — coaxing, flattering; charming.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for an admirer, then the Latin for a suitor.

You'll want to know: blandus, blanda, blandum.
blandus, blanda, blandum — "coaxing, flattering; charming".
Fēlēs blanda ad dominam venit. The coaxing cat comes to her mistress.
Soft and winning words, true or false. English bland, whose meaning has drifted.

The word, taken apart.
blandus is a native Latin adjective; Lewis & Short suggests a link with mollis "soft" and English mild, but the origin is not settled. English bland comes from it.

Lesson 2 of 6.
blanditia, blanditiae — flattery, coaxing.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a suitor, then the Latin for coaxing.

You'll want to know: blanditia, blanditiae.
blanditia, blanditiae — "flattery, coaxing".
Blanditiīs fīliae pater nōn resistit. The father does not resist his daughter's coaxing.
Feminine, first declension; often plural blanditiae — coaxing words.

The word, taken apart.
blanditia is built on blandus "coaxing, flattering" (Lewis & Short). English blandishment comes from the related verb blandior.

Lesson 3 of 6.
suāvis, suāve — sweet, pleasant.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for coaxing, then the Latin for flattery.

You'll want to know: suāvis, suāve.
suāvis, suāve — "sweet, pleasant".
Odor rosārum suāvis est. The scent of the roses is sweet.
Two endings. Sweet to every sense, and in manner. English suave.

The word, taken apart.
suāvis is inherited through Proto-Italic from a Proto-Indo-European word for "sweet" (EtymDB), and English sweet is a cousin. English suave comes from it.

Lesson 4 of 6.
dēliciae, dēliciārum — a darling, pet; delight.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for flattery, then the Latin for sweet.

You'll want to know: dēliciae, dēliciārum.
dēliciae, dēliciārum — "a darling, pet; delight".
Hic catulus dēliciae puellae est. This puppy is the girl's darling.
Feminine plural, first declension, often with a singular meaning: meae dēliciae! — my darling! English delicious.

The word, taken apart.
dēliciae is built on dēliciō "to allure", from dē- and laciō "to entice" (EtymDB; Lewis & Short). English delicious and delicacy come from the family.

Lesson 5 of 6.
dōnum, dōnī — a gift.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for sweet, then the Latin for a darling.

You'll want to know: dōnum, dōnī.
dōnum, dōnī — "a gift".
Amīcus mihi dōnum pulchrum mittit. My friend sends me a beautiful gift.
Neuter, second declension, from dō — to give. English donation.

The word, taken apart.
dōnum is inherited from a Proto-Indo-European word for "gift", built on the root of dō "to give" (EtymDB). English donor and donation come from the family.
