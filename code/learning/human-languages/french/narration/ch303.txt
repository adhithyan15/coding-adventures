French, chapter 303: Quand la terre tremble.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
le tremblement de terre — the earthquake.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the French for to explode, then the French for the spark.

You'll want to know: le tremblement de terre.
le tremblement de terre — "the earthquake".
Le tremblement de terre a duré trente secondes. The earthquake lasted thirty seconds.
News reports also say le séisme.

The word, taken apart.
tremblement is built on trembler, inherited from Vulgar Latin tremulāre, built on Latin tremulus "trembling" (Lewis & Short); terre is inherited from Latin terra "earth" (EtymDB).

The word, taken apart.
avalanche was borrowed from Franco-Provençal avalançhe (EtymDB), an Alpine word, shaped by avaler "to go down".

Un éboulement a coupé la route de la montagne. A rockfall cut off the mountain road.
Signs in the mountains warn: chute de pierres.

The word, taken apart.
éboulement is built on ébouler "to crumble down"; the origin of that verb is not settled (EtymDB lists no parent).

Lesson 4 of 5.
effondrer — to collapse, to cave in (as s'effondrer).

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the French for the avalanche, then the French for the rockfall.

You'll want to know: effondrer.
effondrer — "to collapse, to cave in (as s'effondrer)".
Le vieux pont s'est effondré. The old bridge collapsed.
Mostly reflexive. Also of a person: elle s'est effondrée en larmes — she broke down in tears.

The word, taken apart.
effondrer comes from Old French esfondrer (EtymDB), built on fond "bottom", from Latin fundus "bottom, ground" (Lewis & Short).

Lesson 5 of 5.
les décombres — the rubble, the debris.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the French for the rockfall, then the French for to collapse.

You'll want to know: les décombres.
les décombres — "the rubble, the debris".
Les secours cherchent dans les décombres. The rescue teams are searching in the rubble.
Plural only.

The word, taken apart.
décombres is built on Old French descombrer "to clear away", the opposite of encombrer "to block"; the root is usually traced to a Gaulish word for a blockage, and the origin is not settled (EtymDB lists no parent).
