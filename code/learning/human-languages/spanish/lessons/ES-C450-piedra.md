## You'll want to know: la piedra
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-C450-SUELO-03]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C444-CASA-02] -->

**la piedra** — the stone.

*Una casa de piedra* — a stone house. *Hay piedras en el camino* — there are
stones on the path. *De piedra* also works as a description of a person who
has not moved: *me quedé de piedra*, I was stunned.

For the material in the mass — quarried, cut, built with — Spanish stays
singular: *un muro de piedra*. For the things you can pick up, plural:
*piedras*.

## Grammar Lens: the same vowel trick, one row over
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C450-SUELO-03, ES-LEX-C450-SUELO-02] -->

You have already watched a stressed **o** break into **ue** in *rueda*. A
stressed **e** does the same thing and becomes **ie**:

| Latin | Spanish | what broke |
|---|---|---|
| petra | la p**ie**dra | e under stress |
| rota | la r**ue**da | o under stress |
| herba | la h**ie**rba | e under stress |

Three of this chapter's words are the same sound change caught at different
moments. Once you hear *ie* and *ue* as stressed vowels that gave way, a large
part of Spanish spelling stops looking arbitrary.

And the test still works: *petrificar* keeps the plain **e**, because the
stress has moved off it.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C450-SUELO-03, ES-LEX-C448-MUDA-03] -->

Latin **petra**, taken from Greek **pétra**, a rock.

Latin already had its own word for stone, *lapis*, and it is the one that
survived in the learned vocabulary — *lapidario*, and English *dilapidated*.
The Greek borrowing won out in ordinary speech instead, which is the reverse
of what usually happens.

The name **Pedro** is the same word: Greek *pétros*, a stone, handed out as a
nickname and then carried by enough people to become a name in its own right.
So *Pedro* and *piedra* are one word twice, and a **petrol** is an oil that
comes out of rock.
