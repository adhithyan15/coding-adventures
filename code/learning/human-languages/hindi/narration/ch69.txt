Hindi, chapter 69: Where a Clause Ends, and What Goes Inside One.
3 lessons. All 3 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 3.
The verb goes last — the verb comes last — a rule every sentence in this book has already obeyed and no lesson has ever said.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say two sentences you have had since chapter two and chapter five: मेरा नाम मीरा है, and मैं हिंदी बोलता हूँ. Now listen to where each one ENDS.

Grammar Lens: a rule you have been obeying for sixty-eight chapters.
Every Hindi sentence in this book puts its verb at the end:
मेरा नाम मीरा है — my name Mira is.
मैं हिंदी बोलता हूँ — I Hindi speak.
यह किताब है — this book is.
You have seen this a hundred times and no lesson has ever said it out loud. It is being said now because you are about to start joining clauses, and the moment a sentence has two halves you need to know where each half stops. The verb is the boundary marker. In चाय है लेकिन दूध नहीं है, the first है is what tells the ear the first clause has ended.
English is the odd one here, not Hindi. Hindi's order is subject, object, verb — so is Japanese's, so is Turkish's, and so is roughly half the world's. English puts the verb in the middle and makes it look normal.
The practical form of the rule: when you build a Hindi sentence, decide the verb first and then say it last. A learner who starts translating word by word from English will hit the verb too early, every time.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: the three sentences, and stop hard on the last word of each]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: where the first clause of the lekin sentence ends — at the first है]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: the order Hindi uses — subject, object, verb]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[question — say your answer, then pause 25 seconds]
Name the last word of each of the three sentences, and say what all three have in common.

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Where does the verb of a Hindi sentence go? (Last.) Why does that matter more now than it did in chapter two? (Because a joined sentence has two halves, and the verb is what marks the end of each.)


Lesson 2 of 3.
कि (ki) — "that".

Warm-up.
[pause 3 seconds]
Retrieve the two items before this one by name: लेकिन, which sets two clauses against each other, and the rule that the verb comes last. Then say मैं सोचता हूँ — I think. You have had that verb for a long time and have never once been able to say WHAT you think.

Grammar Lens: कि opens the room the thought goes into.
मैं सोचता हूँ कि चाय ठीक है. — maiṁ sochtā hūṁ ki chāy ṭhīk hai. — I think that the tea is fine.
Word for word: I think that tea fine is. Everything after कि (ki) is an ordinary complete Hindi sentence — चाय ठीक है — with its verb in the ordinary last place, doing the job of an object.
That is the trick worth seeing, and it is why the last lesson came first: you do not need any new grammar to report a thought. You need one word and a sentence you could already say. Every sentence in this book just became something you can think, know or say about.
लेकिन joins two halves as EQUALS. कि (ki) does something different: it puts one clause INSIDE the other, as the second verb's object. Hindi keeps the main clause first — सोचता हूँ comes before what is thought, never after — so the word order is the one you would say anyway.
कि (ki) is not an inherited word. It is Persian که (ke), taken whole, and it pushed aside the older Sanskrit-derived form in exactly this slot. Marathi imported the same word as की. Two languages, one borrowing, the same job.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: chāy ṭhīk hai on its own]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: maiṁ sochtā hūṁ ki chāy ṭhīk hai]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: what changed inside the second half when you put it after कि (ki) — nothing]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[question — say your answer, then pause 30 seconds]
Say the tea is fine, then say that you think the tea is fine.

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
What goes after कि (ki)? (A whole ordinary sentence, verb last, unchanged.) How is कि (ki) unlike लेकिन? (लेकिन joins two equals; कि (ki) puts one clause inside the other.)

Lesson 3 of 3.
Two clauses, two verbs, two ends — the verb at the end of each half, and a whole clause inside another.

Warm-up.
[pause 5 seconds]
A joined Hindi sentence has two verbs in it, and each one is the last word of its own half. Say the lekin sentence and point at both.
