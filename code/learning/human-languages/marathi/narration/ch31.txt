Marathi, chapter 31: Words That Need Two Places.
4 lessons. All 4 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 4.
ना … ना … (nā … nā …) — neither, nor.

Warm-up.
[pause 3 seconds]
Retrieve the last two joining words by name: the one that offers a choice (किंवा) and the one that takes something back (पण). Say a sentence with each before you go on.

Grammar Lens: the word that comes in a pair.
ना चहा ना दूध. — nā chahā nā dūdh. — Neither tea nor milk.
Every joining word so far went in ONE place, between the halves. This one goes in two, in FRONT of each half, and it must be said both times. Say it once and the sentence is unfinished.
ना is the short form of नाही, the no you have had since the courtesy chapter, and it carries the same ancient na-. Doubling it is what makes it a joiner rather than a plain refusal: the first ना sweeps the first thing away, the second sweeps the second, and the pair means both of them, gone.
English does exactly this with neither … nor, which is why the English needs two words too. A one-word translation would be a lie about the shape.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: chahā āṇi dūdh — both offered]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: nā chahā nā dūdh — both refused]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: where ना sits, against where आणि sits — in front of each half, not between them]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[question — say your answer, then pause 20 seconds]
Refuse both tea and milk in one Marathi phrase.

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
How many times must ना be said? (Twice.) Where does it go, compared with आणि? (In front of each half, not between them.)


Lesson 2 of 4.
… ना? (… nā?) — "isn't it?"

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
You have just used ना twice in a row to refuse two things. The same syllable does something completely different when it comes once, and at the end.

Grammar Lens: hanging a check on the end.
चहा आहे ना? — chahā āhe nā? — There's tea, isn't there?
Take any statement you can already make. Put ना on the end. It is now a check: you are telling the listener what you believe and inviting them to confirm it. The answer comes back as हो or नाही.
This is worth more than its size. A Marathi conversation is full of these, and they are how a beginner keeps a turn going without having to build a question from scratch. One syllable turns every sentence in this book into something a listener has to answer.
Notice how far the negative ना has travelled: doubled in front, it wipes two things out; single at the end, it politely asks for a yes. The tag is the old negative used as an invitation — the same move English makes in …, isn't it?, where a negative asks for agreement.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: chahā āhe nā? then answer yourself — ho]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: malā marāṭhī yete nā? — I know Marathi, don't I?]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: nā chahā nā dūdh — and hear the same syllable doing the opposite job]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[question — say your answer, then pause 20 seconds]
Turn 'chahā āhe' into a check, then answer it in the affirmative.

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
What does one ना at the END of a sentence do? (Asks the listener to confirm it.) And two ना in FRONT? (Refuses both things.)


Lesson 3 of 4.
दुसरा (dusrā) — the other one.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Count to five out loud. You have owned एक since the numbers chapter. It has been waiting for a partner.

You'll want to know: दुसरा.
दुसरा — dusrā — the other, the second.
दुसरा (dusrā) is दोन ("two") wearing an ordinal ending: literally two-th. Marathi builds it the way English builds second out of nothing you can see, and Marathi's is the more honest of the two — you can still read the two inside it.
Like every -आ word in this book it agrees: दुसरा (dusrā) for a masculine thing, दुसरी feminine, दुसरं neuter.

Grammar Lens: एक … दुसरा … — sharing a pair out.
एक चहा आणि दुसरा पाणी. — ek chahā āṇi dusrā pāṇī. — One is tea and the other is water.
Set एक in front of the first thing and दुसरा (dusrā) in front of the second, and the pair is now shared out rather than merely listed. आणि would only stack them; this frame says this one, and that one. It is the last of the joining shapes that needs a word at BOTH ends, and it is how Marathi distributes a choice a speaker has already made.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: ek chahā āṇi dusrā pāṇī]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: the number hiding inside dusrā — don]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: the three agreeing forms — dusrā, dusrī, dusraṁ]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[question — say your answer, then pause 25 seconds]
Share a pair out: one is tea, the other is milk.

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
What number is दुसरा (dusrā) built on? (दोन.) What does putting एक and दुसरा (dusrā) at the two ends do that आणि alone cannot? (Shares the pair out — this one, that one.)

Lesson 4 of 4.
Words that need two places — the three joining shapes that need a word at more than one place.

Warm-up.
[pause 5 seconds]
Chapter thirty gave you three joiners that each need ONE place. Name them. Chapter thirty-one gave you three that need more.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]

[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: ek chahā āṇi dusrā pāṇī]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: chahā āhe nā? and answer ho]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[question — say your answer, then pause 35 seconds]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Which of this chapter's three shapes puts its word at the END? (The tag ना.) Which two put a word in FRONT of each half? (ना … ना and एक … दुसरा (dusrā).) Then stop.
