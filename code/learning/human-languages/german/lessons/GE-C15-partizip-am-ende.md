# The participle waits at the back

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GE-GRAMMAR-PERFEKT-ER-01, GE-LEX-GEMACHT-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] *Er hat das gemacht.* Something happened in that sentence that has
not been named yet, and it is the one thing about the *Perfekt* English cannot
copy.

## Grammar Lens: where the participle goes
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[GE-GRAMMAR-PARTIZIP-FINAL-01]; assesses=[GE-GRAMMAR-VERB-SECOND-02] -->

> The participle goes to the **end of the clause**, not next to *haben*.

You already know that the working verb takes the second position in a German
sentence. The *Perfekt* splits the verb in two and puts the halves at opposite
ends: *haben* in its usual second slot, the participle at the very back. Between
them sits everything else the sentence has to say.

Grammarians call that shape a **bracket**, and once you hear it you cannot
unhear it — a German sentence opens a verb and does not close it until the last
word. It is also why German listeners wait: until the bracket shuts, you do not
yet know what was done.

English refuses this outright. *I have German learned* is not a sentence, and no
amount of context rescues it, because English keeps its two verb pieces welded
together and pushes everything else behind them.
