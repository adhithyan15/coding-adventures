# binā ārṭikal — the word Hindi does not have

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[HI-LEX-C40-DEIXIS-02] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say **वह**, "that." Now try to say "the book" in Hindi, and read on
when you find you have no word for *the*.

## You'll want to know: there is no article
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[HI-LEX-C86-PRON-05]; assesses=[] -->

**Hindi has no article.** No word for *the*, and no word for *a*.

This is the largest single structural difference between Hindi and English, and
it is worth being told once rather than discovered slowly:

> **किताब** — *kitāb* — can mean **a book** or **the book**.

The bare noun covers both. Nothing is missing from the sentence; the slot the
English article fills does not exist.

## Grammar Lens: what does the work instead
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[HI-LEX-C86-PRON-05, HI-LEX-C40-DEIXIS-02] -->

Hindi has three ways to be more specific when it needs to be, and you have all
three already:

| want to say | Hindi reaches for | example |
|---|---|---|
| a book, one book | **एक** | ek kitāb |
| that book, the one there | **वह** | vah kitāb |
| plain "book" | nothing at all | kitāb |

**एक** is the numeral *one*, pressed into service when indefiniteness matters —
which is why *ek chāy dījiye* is "a tea, please" rather than a fixed café
formula. It was a number the whole time.

**वह** points, and pointing is how Hindi says *the* when *the* really means
*that particular one*.

Most of the time neither is needed, and this is the habit to build: **do not
reach for a word to translate *the*.** There isn't one, and putting *vah* in
every place English puts *the* sounds as odd as saying *that book* in English
every time you mean *the book*.
