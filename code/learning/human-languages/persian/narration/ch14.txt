Lesson 1 of 5.
خواهر (khâhar) — sister, the family word the earlier tranche skipped.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say باران, and whether it has an English cousin. The sky words are done; the family the earlier tranche built — مادر، پدر، برادر، دختر — is missing exactly one member.

You'll want to know first — one word, a familiar silent letter.
خواهر — khâhar — sister.
Five letters, all already yours: خ و ا ه ر. The و here is silent, exactly the pattern خواندن taught: written after خ and before ا, never pronounced. Read it kh-â-har.

The word, taken apart — brother's missing counterpart.
خواهر (khâhar) continues Middle Persian xwāhar, from Indo-European swésōr, "sister" — as secure a cognate as برادر's own bʰréh₂tēr. English kept it as sister (through Old Norse, replacing the native Old English form), Latin as soror ( becomes sorority), and Sanskrit as svasṛ. مادر، پدر، برادر، دختر، خواهر (khâhar) now give the track all five of Indo-European's plainest kinship words.

Lesson 2 of 5.
پسر (pesar) — son, دختر's missing counterpart.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say خواهر (khâhar), and its secure English cousin. Then say برادر — its own family tranche taught دختر, "daughter," but never gave her brother's counterpart a lesson of its own, until now.

You'll want to know first — one word.
پسر — pesar — son.
Three letters, all already yours: پ س ر — پ pe, from پرسیدن, then s, r.

The word, taken apart — a cousin only inside the family.
پسر (pesar) continues Old Persian puça, cognate with Sanskrit putra, "son" — an Indo-Iranian word Persian and Sanskrit still share with each other. English son is not part of this family: it descends from an entirely different Indo-European root, so — as with باران and رفتن before it — no English cousin is claimed here. پسر (pesar) and دختر now give the track a complete pair for "son" and "daughter," even though only one of the two has an English relative.

Lesson 3 of 5.
مرد (mard) — man, but its root says "mortal".

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say پسر (pesar), and the Sanskrit word it shares a root with. This lesson's word looks like it should be an easy English cousin. It is not.

You'll want to know first — one word.
مرد — mard — man.
Three letters, all already yours: م ر د — m, r, d.

The word, taken apart — a false friend worth naming.
مرد continues Middle Persian mard, from Indo-European mer-, "to die." Its literal sense is not "man" at all — it is "mortal," "one who dies." The same root gives Latin mortuus, "dead" ( becomes English mortal, mortician), Greek brotós ( becomes ambrosia, "not mortal"), and Sanskrit martya. English man looks like an obvious match for مرد and is not one — it descends from a completely different root — so this is one place where guessing a cousin from sound alone would mislead rather than help.

Lesson 4 of 5.
زن (zan) — woman, a root hiding behind a royal title.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say مرد (mard), and why it is not a cousin of English man. This lesson's word finds its English cousin in an unexpected place.

You'll want to know first — one word.
زن — zan — woman.
Two letters, both already yours: ز ن — ز ze, from زبان, then n.

The word, taken apart — a queen's hidden root.
زن continues Middle Persian zan, from Indo-European gʷḗn(h₂)-, "woman." English kept the same root, but only inside a narrowed, elevated word: queen was once simply "woman," the way مرد (mard) narrowed "mortal" down to "man." Greek kept the plain sense directly as gynē ( becomes gynecology), and Russian as žena. مرد (mard) and زن give the track its first pair of person words that are not family roles.

Lesson 5 of 5.
دوست (dust) — friend, and everything this tranche has built.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say مرد، زن (mard, zan) — man, woman. One more person word closes the tranche, and it is a word this track has half-taught already.

You'll want to know first — one word, already half-known.
دوست — dust — friend.
Four letters, all already yours: د و س ت — d, long u, s, t. The compound verb for "to love" already ran on this exact word; here it stands alone, as the person it names.

The word, taken apart — the one chosen.
دوست (dust) goes back through Old Persian dauštā to Indo-European ǵews-, "to taste, to choose" — a friend is the one chosen. English inherited the root directly as choose; Latin took it as gustus, "taste," giving English gusto and disgust. The compound-verb lesson that first taught this root only ever used دوست (dust) glued to داشتن; this is its first lesson as a free-standing noun.
