Bengali, chapter 11: Friend, Family, Brother, and Sister.

Lesson 1 of 4.
বন্ধু (bôndhu) — "friend," someone you are bound to.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, for letter shapes on the page. You can listen to everything else now — leave the section called The letters in this word until you have stopped, and I will say so again when we reach it.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Chapter 2 taught you to exchange names. This chapter names the people themselves — starting with the one whose acquaintance you were glad to make.

You'll want to know: বন্ধু.
বন্ধু — bôndhu — friend.
আমার বন্ধু (āmār bôndhu) — my friend. One word, whether that friend is a man or a woman: Chapter 7 already told you Bengali carries no grammatical gender on its verbs, its adjectives, or its nouns, and বন্ধু is the plainest place to feel it — no ending changes, no second word to learn.

The letters in this word.
বন্ধু (bôndhu) is ব (bô) + ন্ধ (ndhô, a conjunct: ন joined to the breathy dental ধ) + ু (u). The conjunct is two consonants pressed together with no vowel between them, said as one cluster: n-dhu.

The word, taken apart.
বন্ধু (bôndhu) is Sanskrit বন্ধু (bôndhu) (bandhu), kept essentially whole rather than worn down the way চা or ভাত were — Bengali took this one straight from Sanskrit rather than inheriting it through centuries of sound change. It is built on √বন্ধ্ (√bandh, "to bind, to tie"), from PIE bʰendʰ- — and here, unusually, the English cousin is not buried: bind, bond and band all come from the very same root. Bengali did not need to explain what a friend is; the word does it for you. A বন্ধু (bôndhu) is, at the root, someone you are bound to.

Is বন্ধু (bôndhu) a word Bengali inherited through sound change, or one it kept whole from Sanskrit? (Kept whole — a tatsama.) Does বন্ধু (bôndhu) change for a friend who is a woman? (No — Chapter 7 already told you why: no grammatical gender, anywhere in the language.)


Lesson 2 of 4.
পরিবার (pôribār) — "family," what surrounds you.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, for letter shapes on the page. You can listen to everything else now — leave the section called The letters in this word until you have stopped, and I will say so again when we reach it.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Last lesson named one person you are bound to. This one names the whole group.

You'll want to know: পরিবার.
পরিবার — pôribār — family.
আমার পরিবার (āmār pôribār) — my family.

The letters in this word.
পরিবার (pôribār) is প (pô) + রি (ri) + বা (bā) + র (r, silent at the end, a bare consonant with nothing written after it). Four syllables in the spelling, three said aloud: pô-ri-bār.

The word, taken apart.
পরিবার (pôribār) is Sanskrit পরিবার (pôribār) (parivāra), a second word Bengali kept whole from Sanskrit rather than letting it wear down — like বন্ধু (bôndhu), another tatsama. It is built from pari-, "around, about" (PIE peri-, the same prefix English itself borrowed for peri-meter and peri-phery), plus √বৃ (√vṛ, "to cover, to surround," PIE h₂wer-). That root's secure cousins are Latin operiō, "to cover," and Lithuanian suverti, "to shut" — no English word is claimed here, since none is secure. Put the pieces together and a পরিবার (pôribār) is, at the root, what stands around you and covers you.

Lesson 3 of 4.
ভাই (bhāi) — "brother," which never stopped being the word "brother".

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, for letter shapes on the page. You can listen to everything else now — leave the section called The letters in this word until you have stopped, and I will say so again when we reach it.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Two tatsamas so far, both kept whole from Sanskrit. This one took the other road — worn down by sound change until it barely looks related to where it started. Except it does.

You'll want to know: ভাই.
ভাই — bhāi — brother.
আমার ভাই (āmār bhāi) — my brother.

The letters in this word.
ভাই is ভা (bhā, the breathy b from ভাত and ভালো) plus ই (i), a full vowel letter rather than a sign — the two vowels run together into one syllable, bhāi.

The word, taken apart.
ভাই is Sanskrit ভ্রাতৃ (bhrātṛ), worn down through Magadhi Prakrit bhāi — a tadbhava this time, not a tatsama like বন্ধু (bôndhu) and পরিবার (pôribār): Bengali inherited this one through centuries of sound change rather than lifting it whole. And PIE bʰréh₂tēr sits behind bhrātṛ, the same root that gives English brother, Latin frāter and Greek phrátēr. Nothing here is a cousin standing in for the real thing: ভাই and "brother" are, six thousand years apart, the same word.
Bengali also uses family words to address people outside the family: an older man might be called দাদা (dādā, "elder brother") as a mark of respect, whether or not he is any relation at all. That is a light touch on a habit this chapter will not turn into a system — but it is worth naming, since it means বন্ধু (bôndhu) and ভাই (bhāi) are not always as far apart as they look.

Lesson 4 of 4.
বোন (bon) — "sister," and the root it shares with rice.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, for letter shapes on the page. You can listen to everything else now — leave the section called The letters in this word until you have stopped, and I will say so again when we reach it.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
ভাই (bhāi) turned out to be the very word "brother." This one does not — and where it comes from instead is the surprise this chapter has been saving.

You'll want to know: বোন.
বোন — bon — sister.
আমার বোন (āmār bon) — my sister.

The letters in this word.
বোন is ব with the vowel sign ো (bo) plus ন (n), silent at the end — one plain consonant, no cluster, so the inherent vowel drops just as it did on দুধ and ভাত: bon, not bono.

The word, taken apart.
English "sister" descends from PIE swésōr. বোন (bon) does not. It is a tadbhava through Magadhi Prakrit bahiṇī, from Old Indo-Aryan baghinī — a doublet of Sanskrit's own ভগিনী (bhaginī), "one with a share," built on ভগ (bhaga), "a share, a portion, good fortune." And bhaga is a noun from the very same verb that named this chapter's rice two lessons ago: √ভজ্ (√bhaj, "to divide, to share out"), PIE bʰeh₂g-. ভাত is "the portion given"; বোন (bon) is "the one with a portion." Bengali's word for sister and Bengali's word for rice are, underneath, cousins. Its word for sister and English's are not.
The same light touch as last lesson's দাদা applies here: an older woman may be addressed as দিদি (didi, "elder sister") as a mark of respect, family or not.
