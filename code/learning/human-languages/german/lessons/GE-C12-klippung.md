# The two clipped digits

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GE-GRAMMAR-ZIFFER-PLUS-ZEHN-01, GE-LEX-SECHS-01, GE-LEX-SIEBEN-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] The rule holds for every number in this chapter. Two of them shorten
the digit first, and it is worth knowing which before you meet them.

## Grammar Lens: two digits get shortened
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[GE-GRAMMAR-ZAHL-KLIPPUNG-01]; assesses=[GE-LEX-SECHS-01, GE-LEX-SIEBEN-01] -->

| the digit | before *zehn* it becomes | why |
|---|---|---|
| *sechs* | *sech-* | the *s* would collide with the *ts* of *zehn* |
| *sieben* | *sieb-* | the *-en* is an unstressed syllable too many |

Both are the mouth economising, not the grammar changing. It is still a digit
with *zehn* on the end; the digit has been worn smooth at the join and nothing
more.

## Why it's said this way
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GE-GRAMMAR-ZAHL-KLIPPUNG-01, GE-GRAMMAR-ZIFFER-PLUS-ZEHN-01, GE-LEX-FUENFZEHN-01] -->

English did the same thing and did more of it. *Three* became *thir-* and
*five* became *fif-*, and nobody thinks of *thirteen* as irregular — it is a
worn compound, exactly like these two.

So the honest scoreboard is: **English clips two digits, German clips two
digits, and they are not the same two.** German keeps *fünf* whole where English
does not; English keeps *six* and *seven* whole where German does not. Neither
language is tidier. Both are old, and old words get worn.
