\chapter{Telling the Time}
\label{ch:time}

Something new happens here. German's numbers were homegrown (\emph{eins,
zwei}), and its weekdays were Germanic gods (\emph{Donnerstag}). But its word
for the \textbf{clock} --- \textbf{Uhr} --- is borrowed from Latin, while noon
and midnight swing back to native German.
\emph{Practice lessons: \texttt{lessons/GE-C08-*.md}.}

\section{\emph{Uhr} --- the clock, and a Roman word in the German day}
\label{lesson:uhr}

Time-keeping came to the German world with Roman and monastic clocks, and the
Latin word rode in with them.

\begin{sounds}
\emph{Uhr} $=$ \emph{oor}, a long \emph{u} then a soft \emph{r}: one syllable.
\end{sounds}

\begin{cousinweb}
\textbf{Uhr} comes from Latin \textbf{h\={o}ra}, ``hour'' --- the \emph{same}
word that became French \emph{heure}, Italian \emph{ora}, and English
\textbf{hour}. German took it in (through medieval Latin and Low German
\emph{\={u}r(e)}) and made it \emph{Uhr}.
\end{cousinweb}

That is the striking part: three layers of the German day, three different
origins.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
what & German & origin \\
\midrule
the \textbf{numbers} & \emph{eins, zwei, drei} & native Germanic (twins of English) \\
the \textbf{weekdays} & \emph{Montag, Donnerstag} & Germanic gods (Latin \emph{months}) \\
the \textbf{clock} & \emph{Uhr} & Latin loan (\emph{h\={o}ra}) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

German kept its own numbers and gods, but for the \emph{machine that measures
hours} it reached for Rome. German also has a native word, \textbf{Stunde},
``an hour's span'' --- but for clock-reading, ``o'clock,'' it is \emph{Uhr}.

\begin{grammarlens}
\textbf{``\emph{Es ist \ldots{} Uhr}.''} To tell the time, German says
\textbf{es ist} (``it is'') $+$ the number $+$ \textbf{Uhr}:\\[3pt]
\emph{Es ist ein Uhr.} --- ``It is one o'clock.''\\[3pt]
\emph{Es ist zwei Uhr.} --- ``It is two o'clock.''\\[3pt]
\emph{Es ist zehn Uhr.} --- ``It is ten o'clock.''\\[3pt]
\emph{Uhr} does not add a plural here (not \emph{zwei Uhren} for the time) ---
it works like English \textbf{o'clock}: \emph{zwei Uhr} $=$ ``two o'clock.''
(\emph{Uhren}, the plural, means ``clocks,'' the objects.)
\end{grammarlens}

\section{\emph{Mittag} and \emph{Mitternacht} --- the native middle of day and night}
\label{lesson:mittag-mitternacht}

The clock-word \emph{Uhr} was Latin. But noon and midnight swing back to
\textbf{native German}: \emph{Mittag} and \emph{Mitternacht} are plain Germanic
compounds --- ``the day's middle'' and ``the night's middle.''

\begin{morphologybox}
Both start with \textbf{Mitte}, ``middle'' (cousin of English \textbf{mid},
\textbf{middle}, and of Latin \emph{medius}).
\end{morphologybox}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
German & $=$ & literally \\
\midrule
\emph{Mittag} & \emph{Mitte} $+$ \emph{Tag} & ``\textbf{mid}-\textbf{day}'' (noon) \\
\emph{Mitternacht} & \emph{Mitter} (\emph{Mitte}) $+$ \emph{Nacht} & ``\textbf{mid}-\textbf{night}'' (midnight) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{cousinweb}
\textbf{Mittag} $=$ ``mid-day.'' \emph{Tag} (``day'') you met in \emph{Montag,
Dienstag}; put ``middle'' in front and you get noon. Its meaning matches French
\emph{midi} (\emph{medius di\={e}s}) exactly --- same idea, but German built it
from its \emph{own} words instead of borrowing the Latin one.\\[3pt]
\textbf{Mitternacht} $=$ ``mid-night.'' \emph{Nacht} (``night'') is the twin of
English \textbf{night} (remember \emph{acht}/\textbf{eight},
\emph{Nacht}/\textbf{night} --- the \emph{-cht}/\emph{-ght} pair). So
\emph{Mitternacht} and English \textbf{midnight} are the same compound in two
Germanic tongues.
\end{cousinweb}

To tell the time with them: \emph{Es ist Mittag.} --- ``It is noon.''
\emph{Es ist Mitternacht.} --- ``It is midnight.''

So German splits the day three ways by origin: \textbf{Uhr} (Latin) for the
hours, \textbf{Mittag}/\textbf{Mitternacht} (native) for its pivots. Next
chapter builds on this toward months and family.
