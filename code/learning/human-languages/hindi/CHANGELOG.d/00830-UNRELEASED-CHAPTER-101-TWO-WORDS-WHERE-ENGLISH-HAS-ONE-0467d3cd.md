### Hindi has no prepositions

That is the claim the whole chapter rests on, and it is not a simplification.
**दोस्त के साथ** is, piece by piece, *the friend's company*: a noun, a
possessive, and a second noun that happens to name a relation. **घर के पास** is
*the house's nearness*.

Reading it that way pays for itself three times over:

- The middle word explains itself. It is **के** and never **का** because a
  further word follows, and a possessive in front of a further word goes
  oblique — the same rule chapter 92 taught on possessives and chapter 99
  taught on nouns, applied a third time rather than introduced a first time.
- A pronoun needs no middle word, and that stops being an exception. **मेरे
  पास** has no **के** because **मेरे** is already carrying it, audibly, as its
  own **-ए**.
- Every further relation costs exactly **one word**. Slot 3 is a swappable
  position, so six relations arrive for the price of six nouns.

| slot 1 | slot 2 | slot 3 |
|---|---|---|
| दोस्त | के | साथ |
| घर | के | पास |
| करने | के | लिए |

### पास was hiding in a phrase taught nine chapters ago

`HI-C84-mere-paas` taught **मेरे पास** in chapter 92 as the way to say *I have*,
because Hindi has no verb for having — and it never said what **पास** means.

It means **near**. It has meant *near* the whole time, and the possessive sense
is the metaphor built on top: whatever is beside you is, for practical
purposes, yours. The learner met the borrowed meaning first because that is the
one a beginner needs first. `HI-C93-ke-paas` supplies the original.

### The fifth kind of word to wear the oblique

| kind of word | plain | before a postposition |
|---|---|---|
| possessive | मेरा | मेरे |
| demonstrative possessive | उसका | उसके |
| own | अपना | अपने |
| noun | बच्चा | बच्चे |
| **infinitive** | **करना** | **करने** |

The infinitive is the last of the five and costs no new rule: **-ना** ends in
**-आ**, so it bends exactly as **बच्चा** does. It *can* bend like a noun
because in Hindi it **is** one — **करना** is both *to do* and *the doing*, which
is why it could already stand where a noun stands.

### Half of POST-10's note was stale

The note said *"Neither the postposition nor the oblique infinitive is taught."*
`HI-C84-mere-liye` has taught **मेरे लिए** since chapter 92.

What was genuinely missing was **के लिए after a noun** — which needs the
separate **के** that a pronoun carries inside itself — and the oblique
infinitive. Only the second of those is machinery. This is the seventh stale
note the campaign has found and the third distinct variety: not false when
written and not made false by later work, but **half true**, precise about the
grammar and wrong about the vocabulary.

`HI-A1-POST-07`'s and `HI-A1-POST-08`'s notes were both exactly right and
needed no correction. POST-07's examples are constructible as printed — mock 1's
`apne dost ke sāth` and mock 2's `apne parivār ke sāth` needed nothing beyond
the one new word, because *apnā*, *dost* and *parivār* were all already taught.

### The distinction the place lessons drill

**पास answers a different question from the other two.**

| slot-three word | what it tells you |
|---|---|
| पास | **how far** — close, no direction |
| सामने | **which side** — facing |
| पीछे | **which side** — away |

A shop can be *near* a station and still be *behind* it. Mock 2 tests exactly
this: listening item 6 is `bas station ke sāmne hai` and item 11 is `nahīṁ, pās
hai` — the second concedes the weaker claim because the question asked for
less. The synthesis lesson stages that exchange and tells the reader not to
upgrade **पास** into a side.
