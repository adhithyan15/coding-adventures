# کو (ko) — to, and the one it happens to

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[UR-GRAMMAR-OBLIQUE, UR-GRAMMAR-DATIVE-EXPERIENCER] -->

[PAUSE 2s] What happens to **کمرہ** when a postposition follows it? Then say the
sentence from chapter eight that means **I like mangoes**.

## You'll want to know: کو
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[UR-GRAMMAR-KO]; assesses=[UR-GRAMMAR-DATIVE-EXPERIENCER, UR-LEX-MAAN] -->

> **ماں کو** — *māṅ ko* — **to the mother**, or **the mother**, as the one it
> happens to.

Chapter eight taught *mujhe ām pasand haiṅ* — *mangoes are pleasing to me* — and
then left you unable to say it about anybody else, because *mujhe* looked like
one word.

It is not. *Mujhe* is *mujh* plus **کو**, fused so long ago that the seam has
closed. Now that you have the postposition on its own, the frame opens to
everybody:

> **ماں کو** *ām pasand haiṅ* — *māṅ ko ām pasand haiṅ* — **mother likes
> mangoes.**

*Mujhe* and *pasand* stay in romanization here. Between them they need three
letters the script ladder has not reached — *jīm*, *do chashmī he* and *dāl* — and this
book does not print a letter before it has taught it.

## Grammar Lens: two jobs, one word
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[UR-GRAMMAR-KO, UR-GRAMMAR-OBLIQUE, UR-LEX-KAMRA] -->

**کو** marks the person a thing is **given to**, and the person a thing is
**done to**. English needs *to* for the first and word order alone for the
second; Urdu uses one postposition for both.

And it takes the oblique in front of it, like every postposition:

- **کمرہ** → **کمرے کو**

A postposition never touches a direct-shape noun. That rule has no exceptions
and it is worth over-learning now, because six more postpositions are coming.
