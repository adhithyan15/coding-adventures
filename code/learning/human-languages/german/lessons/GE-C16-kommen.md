# kommen — "to come"

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GE-GRAMMAR-PERFEKT-SEIN-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Motion takes *sein*. Here is the motion verb you will use as often
as *gehen*, and it is an English word you already own.

## You'll want to know: kommen
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[GE-LEX-KOMMEN-01]; assesses=[GE-GRAMMAR-VERB-ENDINGS-01, GE-GRAMMAR-PERFEKT-SEIN-01] -->

> **kommen** — "to come."

> **ich bin gekommen** — "I came."

The infinitive wears the **-en** you know. The participle is **gekommen**: the
*ge-* wrap in front, and *-en* rather than *-t* at the back, because this is one
of the old strong verbs rather than a regular one.

Coming is arriving somewhere you were not. That is motion with a result, so it
takes *sein*, exactly as *gehen* does.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GE-LEX-KOMMEN-01] -->

**kommen** and English **come** are the same word, both from Germanic
**\**kwemaną***. No Latin anywhere in the line — French *venir* and Spanish
*venir* come from Latin *venīre* instead, which is where English gets *advent*,
*convene* and *venue*.

So English, as usual, owns both halves: the everyday **come** from the Germanic
side, the formal **convene** from the Latin.

## Sounds you'll need
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GE-LEX-KOMMEN-01] -->

- `vowel-o` — the **o** is short and open, the vowel of English *cot*, not the
  *uh* English puts in *come*.
- `ge-prefix` — *gekommen* keeps the unstressed **ge-**; the stress is on
  *-KOMM-*.
