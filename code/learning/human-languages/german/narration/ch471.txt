German, chapter 471: Tränen und Trost.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
die Träne — tear (from crying).

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the German for excitement, then the German for to fidget.

You'll want to know: die Träne.
die Träne — "tear (from crying)".
Beim Abschied hatte sie Tränen in den Augen. When they said goodbye, she had tears in her eyes.
Plural: die Tränen. In Tränen ausbrechen — to burst into tears.

The word, taken apart.
Träne is inherited from Middle High German træne "tears", a form of trahen, from Old High German trahan "tear" (EtymDB); it is usually traced to Proto-Germanic trahnuz "tear".

Lesson 2 of 5.
schluchzen — to sob.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the German for to fidget, then the German for tear.

You'll want to know: schluchzen.
schluchzen — "to sob".
Das Mädchen schluchzte leise in seinem Zimmer. The girl was sobbing quietly in her room.
Stronger than weinen.

The word, taken apart.
schluchzen is usually explained as an imitative word beside schlucken "to swallow"; EtymDB lists it without a source, so the details are not settled.

Lesson 3 of 5.
der Trost — comfort, consolation.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the German for tear, then the German for to sob.

You'll want to know: der Trost.
der Trost — "comfort, consolation".
Ein warmer Tee war ein kleiner Trost. A warm tea was a small comfort.
No plural. Ein schwacher Trost — cold comfort.

The word, taken apart.
Trost is inherited from Old High German trōst "consolation", from Proto-Germanic traustą (EtymDB), built on an adjective meaning "firm, strong"; English trust is a cousin.

Lesson 5 of 5.
das Mitleid — pity, compassion.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the German for comfort, then the German for to comfort.

You'll want to know: das Mitleid.
das Mitleid — "pity, compassion".
Ich habe Mitleid mit dem kranken Hund. I feel sorry for the sick dog.
No plural. Mitleid haben mit — to feel sorry for.

The word, taken apart.
Mitleid is built on mit "with" and Leid "sorrow"; EtymDB traces it to Middle High German mitelīden "to suffer with". It is usually seen as modelled on Church Latin compassio "fellow-suffering" (Lewis & Short).
