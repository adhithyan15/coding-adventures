Italian, chapter 412: Pedoni e vigili.
6 lessons. All 6 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 6.
il vigile — the traffic warden, the local police officer.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Italian for the light, then the Italian for to unscrew.

You'll want to know: il vigile.
il vigile — "the traffic warden, the local police officer".
Il vigile ci ha fermato all'incrocio. The warden stopped us at the crossroads.
In full il vigile urbano; i vigili del fuoco are firefighters.

The word, taken apart.
vigile is a learned borrowing from Latin vigil 'awake, watchful' (Lewis & Short): the one who keeps watch.

What you've built.

You'll want to know: il pedone.
il pedone — "the pedestrian".
Attenzione ai pedoni! Watch out for pedestrians!
La zona pedonale is a pedestrian zone. In chess, il pedone is a pawn.

The word, taken apart.
pedone is inherited from Late Latin pedō 'foot soldier', built on pēs, pedis 'foot' (EtymDB).

Lesson 3 of 6.

You'll want to know: le strisce.
le strisce — "the zebra crossing; the stripes".
Attraversa sulle strisce, è più sicuro. Cross at the zebra crossing, it is safer.
Singular la striscia, a stripe; in full le strisce pedonali.

The word, taken apart.
strisce is the plural of striscia 'stripe', which EtymDB traces to Latin stria 'furrow, groove'; it is inherited.

investire — "to run over; to invest".
Una macchina ha investito un gatto. A car ran over a cat.
With money it means to invest: investire in una casa.

The word, taken apart.
investire is a learned borrowing from Latin investīre 'to clothe, to cover' (EtymDB), built on vestis 'clothing'.

Lesson 5 of 6.
la contravvenzione — the fine, the traffic ticket.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Italian for the zebra crossing, then the Italian for to run over.

You'll want to know: la contravvenzione.
la contravvenzione — "the fine, the traffic ticket".
Il vigile mi ha fatto una contravvenzione. The warden gave me a ticket.
Formal; in speech people say la multa.

The word, taken apart.
contravvenzione is a learned borrowing from Medieval Latin contrāventiō, built on contrā 'against' and venīre 'to come': coming against a rule.
