Tamil, chapter 56: How Much.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
சிறிது (siṟitu) — a little.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new run of words: what did nakam mean, and what did elumbu mean?

You'll want to know: சிறிது.
சிறிது (siṟitu) — "a little".
You already have siṟiya, "small". சிறிது is the same root turned into an amount: not a small thing but a small quantity of something. Held a dish and asked how much, you say siṟitu and you get a little.
Tamil has a koñcam as well, which is the one you will hear across a table, and which Telugu shares as koñcem. சிறிது (siṟitu) is the more formal of the two, and the one you would write down.

What you've built.
The first of five answers to "how much?".

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: siṟitu]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: it once more, slowly]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: elumbu, then siṟitu, and keep the second one short]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
What does சிறிது (siṟitu) mean? ("a little".) Say it once more.


Lesson 2 of 5.
நிறைய (niṟaiya) — a lot.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: what did siṟitu mean?

You'll want to know: நிறைய.
நிறைய (niṟaiya) — "a lot".
The other end of the same question. நிறைய (niṟaiya) is built on niṟai, to fill, so a lot in Tamil is a fullness: as much as the thing will hold and no argument about it.
Telugu says ఎక్కువ (ekkuva) and builds it out of nothing to do with filling — a reminder that two languages can land on the same everyday answer by two quite different routes.

What you've built.
Two, and they are opposite ends of one question.

[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: it once more, slowly]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: siṟitu, then niṟaiya, the little and the lot]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
What does நிறைய (niṟaiya) mean? ("a lot".) Say it once more.


Lesson 3 of 5.
குறைவு (kuṟaivu) — a shortage, less than there should be.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: what did niṟaiya mean?

You'll want to know: குறைவு.
குறைவு (kuṟaivu) — "a shortage, less than there should be".
kuṟai is to fall short, and குறைவு (kuṟaivu) is the falling-short itself. That makes it a narrower word than the English "less": it does not merely compare two amounts, it says one of them did not come up to the mark. There is a small complaint folded inside it.
Telugu's word for the same ground is తక్కువ (takkuva).

What you've built.
Three: a little, a lot, and not as much as there ought to be.

[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: it once more, slowly]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: niṟaiya, then kuṟaivu, plenty and then not enough]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
What does குறைவு (kuṟaivu) mean? ("a shortage, less than there should be".) Say it once more.


Lesson 4 of 5.
தேவையில்லை (tēvaiyillai) — there is no need.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: what did kuṟaivu mean?

You'll want to know: தேவையில்லை.
தேவையில்லை (tēvaiyillai) — "there is no need".
Two pieces, and the second one has been yours since the early writing lessons: tēvai is a need, illai is the no you learned to put on paper. Together they are how Tamil turns something down without turning the person down.
A flat illai refuses the thing. தேவையில்லை (tēvaiyillai) says there is no call for it, which leaves everybody their footing. Telugu does the same work in one shorter piece, వద్దు (vaddu).

What you've built.
Four, and this one is the polite way out.

[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Lesson 5 of 5.
ஆகட்டும் (ākaṭṭum) — let it be so.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: what did tēvaiyillai mean?

You'll want to know: ஆகட்டும்.
ஆகட்டும் (ākaṭṭum) — "let it be so".
āku is to become, to come about. ஆகட்டும் (ākaṭṭum) is "let it come about" — what you say when somebody proposes something and you are content to let it happen. It agrees without promising to do anything.
The ām you have been saying for yes is that same āku worn down by a great deal of use, which is why the two sit so alike in the mouth. Telugu's nearest of these is అలాగే (alāgē), "just so, alright then".

What you've built.
Five answers to an offer: a little, a lot, not enough, no need, and yes — let it happen.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: ākaṭṭum]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: it once more, slowly]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: all five in order — siṟitu, niṟaiya, kuṟaivu, tēvaiyillai, ākaṭṭum]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
What does ஆகட்டும் (ākaṭṭum) mean? ("let it be so".) Say it once more.
