# hablo — one form, two readings

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-GRAMMAR-PRESENT-INDICATIVE-COMPLETE, ES-GRAMMAR-PROGRESSIVE-ESTAR-GERUND] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Somebody phones and asks what you are doing. Answer with *comer*, in
the present, without using the *-iendo* form.

[PAUSE 1s] You said *como*. Now think about what that sentence actually claimed.

## Grammar Lens: it was doing both all along
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-GRAMMAR-PRESENT-ACTUAL-AND-DURATIVE]; assesses=[ES-LEX-AHORA] -->

> *Como.* — I eat. **And also**: I'm eating, right now, this minute.

[PAUSE 2s] One form, two readings. The **durative** one is the habit — *I eat
at eight*. The **actual** one is this moment — *I'm eating, don't ring back*.
English keeps two shapes; Spanish keeps one.

[PAUSE 2s] You have been using both since the very first verb, without anybody
naming either.

## Why it's said this way
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-GRAMMAR-PROGRESSIVE-IS-OPTIONAL]; assesses=[ES-GRAMMAR-PROGRESSIVE-NOT-FUTURE] -->

Which raises the obvious question about the form you learned nineteen chapters
ago. If *como* already means *I'm eating*, what is *estoy comiendo* for?

[PAUSE 2s] **Insistence.** The progressive is not the ordinary way to say what
you are doing now — it is the way to say it when the *now* is the point.

> — *¿Qué haces?* — *Como.* (perfectly normal)
> — *¡Estoy comiendo!* — I'm **in the middle of eating**, leave me alone.

[PAUSE 2s] So the progressive is **optional** where English's is compulsory. An
English speaker reaches for *estoy comiendo* every time and sounds oddly
emphatic; a Spanish speaker uses the plain present and saves the long form for
when it means something.

[PAUSE 2s] This is worth more than it looks. It means you already had a
complete present tense before that chapter, and the progressive was an addition
rather than a repair.

[PAUSE 2s] How many readings does the plain present carry? (**Two** — the habit
