Malayalam, chapter 39: Tea, Coffee, Milk.
3 lessons. All 3 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 3.
ചായ (chaaya) (chāya) — "tea," which walked to Kerala rather than sailed.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Two hearts done. Now the drink you would politely ask for first, in any Kerala home — and a chance to put Chapter 8's ദയവായി back to work.

You'll want to know.
ചായ (chaaya) — chāya — tea.
ദയവായി ഒരു ചായ (chaaya). — dayavāyi oru chāya. — "A tea, please."
Dayavāyi has been yours since Chapter 8; oru, "a/one," you have already met attached to nāḷ and maṇi.

The word, taken apart.
ചായ (chaaya) traces to Northern Chinese chá, carried overland along Central Asian trade routes into Persian چای (chāy), and from there into Hindi-Urdu chāy and Malayalam chāya alike — the same land route most of North and South India took for their word for tea. Tea that instead reached Europe by sea kept a different, southern Chinese form, which is why English says tea and French says thé — one leaf, two roads: chai by land, tea by sea.

Grammar Lens: the two hearts, and the spine node so far.
ദയവായി ഒരു ചായ (chaaya) leans on the very dative Chapter 6 named, ‑ikku/‑inu — the same suffix beside Tamil's ‑ukku, Telugu's ‑ku, and Kannada's ‑ge. This chapter carries both halves of the spine node ദയവായി and ഹൃദയം/നെഞ്ച് already opened: asking politely, and — if you ask for the wrong thing — repairing with ക്ഷമിക്കണം.

Lesson 2 of 3.
കാപ്പി (kaapi) (kāppi) — "coffee," which sailed while tea walked.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Chāya travelled overland. This drink took the opposite road — and arrives from further away.

You'll want to know.
കാപ്പി (kaapi) — kāppi — coffee.
ദയവായി ഒരു കാപ്പി (kaapi). — dayavāyi oru kāppi. — "A coffee, please."

The word, taken apart.
കാപ്പി (kaapi) comes from English coffee, borrowed during the colonial period and reshaped to Malayalam's own consonants and vowels. English's own word for the drink is itself a loan, several times over: Arabic قهوة (qahwah) became Turkish kahve, then Italian caffè, then English coffee, before finally landing in Kerala as kāppi.
Set the two drinks side by side: ചായ (chaaya) walked overland from China, through Persia, into North and South Indian speech alike. കാപ്പി (kaapi) sailed from Arabia, through Ottoman Turkey and Renaissance Italy, into English, and only then into Malayalam. Two drinks, two opposite roads — one over land, one over sea — meeting on the very same tea-shop counter.

Lesson 3 of 3.
പാൽ (paal) (pāl) — "milk," where Tamil agrees completely.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Chapter 15's വെള്ളം broke away from Tamil's water-word entirely. This drink, the third in the chapter, does the opposite.

You'll want to know.
പാൽ (paal) — pāl — milk.
ദയവായി ഒരു പാൽ (paal). — dayavāyi oru pāl. — "A milk, please."

The word, taken apart.
പാൽ (paal) is native Dravidian, from Proto-South-Dravidian pāl, "milk." Tamil keeps the shape unchanged: பால் (pāl), letter for letter the same as Malayalam. Kannada shows its own regular p‑to‑h sound law here too, giving ಹಾಲು (hālu) — the identical shift that turns Malayalam's pōkuka into Kannada's hōgu.
Set beside Chapter 15's finding, this is the mirror image: വെള്ളം (water) broke from Tamil's nīr entirely, choosing a completely different root; പാൽ (paal) (milk) shows the opposite — perfect three-way agreement in shape, with only Kannada's regular sound law setting its form apart. Two everyday liquids, two entirely different outcomes.
