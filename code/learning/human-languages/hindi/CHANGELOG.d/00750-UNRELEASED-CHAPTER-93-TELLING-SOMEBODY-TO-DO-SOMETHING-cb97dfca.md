### The grid from the possessive chapter does the work again

The previous chapter established that **तू / तुम / आप** is one decision. This
one shows the decision reaches the verb:

| level | possessive | copula | command |
|---|---|---|---|
| तू | तेरा | है | बोल |
| तुम | तुम्हारा | **हो** | **बोलो** |
| आप | आपका | हैं | **बोलिए** |

Read a row straight across. Choosing a person picks the whole row, so this is
one decision rather than four separate facts — which is why three inventory
points cost one chapter.

The **तू** row is taught for **recognition only**, and the lesson says so: the
risk of using it wrongly is much larger than the benefit of using it at all.

### An old lump comes apart

`HI-A1-V-16`'s note recorded that the **-iye** form existed in the corpus only
*"inside two fixed courtesy phrases."* It was right, and the fix is satisfying:

> **माफ़ कीजिए** is *karnā*, "to do," with the respectful imperative ending.
> *Māf kījiye* is literally **"please do forgiveness."**

The track has been giving a respectful command since its ninth chapter without
the reader being told it was one. Now the ending is general — *boliye*,
*suniye*, *baiṭhiye* — rather than two memorised lumps.

### Politeness is built in, not added on

| | command | how politeness is carried |
|---|---|---|
| English | speak | by adding *please* |
| Hindi | बोलिए | by the ending |

**बोलिए** already contains the respect, so it needs no *kṛpayā*. Adding one is
warmer, not more correct.

### The negative Hindi keeps for commands

`HI-A1-V-17`'s note names the exact item it is tested by — *Mock 2 reading item
3 is `yahan mat baithiye`* — and the synthesis lesson reads that sentence and
takes it into its three taught pieces.

**मत** is a separate negative reserved for commands, distinct from the statement
negative the track already had. English uses *not* for both, which is exactly
why an English speaker reaches for the wrong one.

The chapter also explains why a reading paper's notices are full of **-iye**: a
sign does not know who is reading it, so it addresses a stranger, and a stranger
takes **आप**.

### The copula, completed

`HI-A1-V-03`'s complaint was precise: **ho** occurred inside the age frame as a
lump, and no atom introduced it independently of that one memorised question.
The set is now whole — *hūṅ, ho, haiṅ, hai* — and the lesson shows the level
matching end to end, so that *āp ho* and *tum boliye* are visible as the
mismatches they are.

### Two gates caught things before generation

**Two tables were four columns wide**, and one of them carried its content under
an **unlabelled first column** — the exact shape `chapter-policy` calls
unspeakable. Both were reshaped to three labelled columns, so the narration
refusal count never moved.

**Two independent vowel letters were untaught.** Naming the endings in
Devanagari would have printed **इ** and **ओ**, which the corpus shows but never
teaches. The endings are named in romanization instead — **-o** and **-iye** —
which is also how a reader says them. No exemption list was edited.

### One count rose for an honest reason

`forward-language` **10 → 11**. Unlike the two false-positive classes recorded
earlier in this campaign, this one is **real and was predicted before the
lesson was written**: chapter 91 teaches the idiom *burā mat māno*, which uses
**मत** nineteen lessons before this chapter introduces it.

The occurrence was counted in advance — the standalone word appears in exactly
one earlier lesson, every other hit being a substring of *anumati* or *matsya* —
and the cost was judged worth paying, because *burā mat māno* is the phrase a
learner meets first and the alternative is removing a real idiom from a merged
lesson to flatter a metric.

`measurement-blind` **6 → 7**, the one new *síntesis* lesson. HL-C377 again; not
relabelled.
