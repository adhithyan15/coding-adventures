# el importe — the amount

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C463-GUIA-03, ES-LEX-IMPORTANTE] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say **importar** and **importante**. Today's noun is the third
member of that family, and it is the one that appears on a bill.

## You'll want to know: el importe
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-C474-PAGO-02]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C474-PAGO-01] -->

**el importe** — the amount, the sum.

> Hay que pagar el **importe** completo.

*El importe total* — the total. *El importe del curso* — the fee for a class.
This is a form-and-invoice word: in speech people say *cuánto es*, but anything
printed says *importe*.

## Grammar Lens: one verb, three jobs
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C474-PAGO-02, ES-LEX-C463-GUIA-03] -->

[Importar](./ES-C463-importar.md) told you two of these. Here is the third,
and seeing them together is what makes the word stick.

| the sense | example |
|---|---|
| to matter | *no me importa* |
| to import goods | *importar café* |
| **to amount to** | **el importe** |

The thread is Latin **in-** plus **portare**, to **carry in**. Goods carried in
are imports. A thing that carries weight *matters*. And what a bill **carries
in** for you to pay is its *importe*.

So the noun is not a fourth meaning bolted on. It is the same carrying, applied
to money.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C474-PAGO-02, ES-LEX-IMPORTANTE] -->

*Portare* is the carrying verb, and once you have it a great many words open at
once. Spanish has already given you two of its children without saying so:

- **el deporte** — *dis-* plus *portare*, carrying yourself away from work;
- **el comportamiento** — carrying yourself **with**, which is behaviour.

English carries the same root in **port**, **portable**, **export**,
**transport** and **report** — a thing carried back.

And *importante* is literally **carrying-in-ish**: something that brings weight
with it.
