Latin, chapter 143: Hard, Soft, Lukewarm, Ancient, Narrow.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
dūrus, dūra, dūrum — hard.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a flute, then the Latin for a lyre.

You'll want to know: dūrus, dūra, dūrum.
dūrus, dūra, dūrum — "hard".

The word, taken apart.
dūrus has no sure origin; Lewis & Short call it doubtful. A link with Proto-Indo-European deru-, "firm, solid", the root of words for tree, is often proposed (EtymDB 2.1).

Lesson 2 of 5.
mollis, molle — soft.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a lyre, then the Latin for hard.

You'll want to know: mollis, molle.
mollis, molle — "soft".

The word, taken apart.
mollis is inherited, usually traced to Proto-Indo-European moldus, "soft, weak", from mel-, "soft" (EtymDB 2.1); the ld became ll. Greek amalos, "weak", is compared (Lewis & Short).

Lesson 3 of 5.
tepidus, tepida, tepidum — lukewarm.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for hard, then the Latin for soft.

You'll want to know: tepidus, tepida, tepidum.
tepidus, tepida, tepidum — "lukewarm".

The word, taken apart.
tepidus is built on tepeō, "to be warm" (Lewis & Short), with -idus, the ending of state adjectives. tepeō is usually traced to Proto-Indo-European tep-, "warm".

Lesson 4 of 5.
antīquus, antīqua, antīquum — ancient, old-fashioned.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for soft, then the Latin for lukewarm.

You'll want to know: antīquus, antīqua, antīquum.
antīquus, antīqua, antīquum — "ancient, old-fashioned".

The word, taken apart.
antīquus is built on ante, "before" (Lewis & Short), and is usually traced to Proto-Indo-European h2enti-h3kwos, "having been before" (EtymDB 2.1): what stood before, so old.

Lesson 5 of 5.
angustus, angusta, angustum — narrow.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for lukewarm, then the Latin for ancient.

You'll want to know: angustus, angusta, angustum.
angustus, angusta, angustum — "narrow".

The word, taken apart.
angustus is built on angō, "to squeeze, to choke" (Lewis & Short), usually traced to Proto-Indo-European h2engh-, "narrow, tight" (EtymDB 2.1). English anguish and anxious belong to the same family.
