# el jefe — the one at the head

## You'll want to know: el jefe
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-C462-JEFE-03]; assesses=[ES-LEX-TRABAJO] -->

**el jefe** — the boss, the head of something.

*Hablar con el jefe* — to speak to the boss. *La jefa de personal* — the head
of HR. *El jefe de estación* — the stationmaster.

The feminine is **la jefa**, formed normally, and both are ordinary words at
work — not slang and not deferential.

## Grammar Lens: the j that used to be something else
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C462-JEFE-03, ES-LEX-CABEZA-01] -->

**Jefe** came from French *chef*, and the **ch** became a **j**. Spanish did
that to a whole shelf of borrowings:

| French | Spanish |
|---|---|
| **ch**ef | el **j**efe |
| **j**ardin | el **j**ardín |
| **j**amais | — |

The **j** is the *jota*, the sound at the back of the throat, and it is a
latecomer: medieval Spanish did not have it. Words spelled with **j** or with
**g** before *e* and *i* all shifted to it at roughly the same time, a few
hundred years ago, which is why *jefe*, *mujer* and *gente* now share a sound
that none of them started with.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C462-JEFE-03, ES-LEX-CABEZA-01] -->

French **chef** is Latin **caput**, **head** — and *caput* is the same word
inside **la cabeza**.

That needs one correction to something you were told earlier. When you learned
*la cabeza*, French was described as having dropped *caput* altogether for
*testa*, a pot. That is true of the **body part** and only of it: *tête*
replaced *caput* for the thing on your shoulders, while *caput* itself stayed
on in French as *chef*, a head in the sense of a chief. It is that surviving
branch Spanish later borrowed.

So *el jefe* and *la cabeza* are one Latin noun arriving twice: once worn down
through Spanish speech into the body part, once borrowed back from French as
the person in charge. A boss is literally **a head**, exactly as in English *head
of department*.

The family is large: **el capítulo** (a chapter, a little head), **la capital**
(the head city), **acabar** — to finish, to bring to a head — and English
**captain**, **chief**, **chef**, **capital** and **cape**. A *chef* is the
head of a kitchen, and *chief* and *jefe* are the same word in two languages.
