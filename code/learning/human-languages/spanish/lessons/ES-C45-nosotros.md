# nosotros — the "we" the verb already says

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-NOS-OBJECT, ES-GRAMMAR-NOS-DIRECT-OBJECT] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say "they love us." (*Nos quieren*.)

[PAUSE 1s] *Nos* is "us." Today it becomes "we" — and you will find you have
been saying "we" for a long time without it.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-NOSOTROS, ES-ETYMON-NOS-OTROS]; assesses=[ES-GRAMMAR-AR-PRESENT-1PL] -->

*Nos* is Latin **nōs**, which meant both *we* and *us* — the language had not
yet split them into two words.

[PAUSE 2s] You have been saying "we" without it. The *-mos* ending on
*hablamos*, *comemos*, *tenemos* already means **we** — it is the verb's own
"we" ending, Latin **-mus** (*amāmus*, "we love"), a different word from *nōs*
that happens to mean the same person. That is why *hablamos* alone is a whole
sentence, and the pronoun is extra.

And the word for "we" itself:

> **nosotros** — *we*, literally **nos** + **otros**, "we **others**."

[PAUSE 2s] Spanish added *otros* to the plain *nos* somewhere in the Middle
Ages, to make clear the speaker meant *this* group and not the person being
addressed. Catalan (*nosaltres*) and Occitan (*nosautres*) did the same;
standard French and Italian did not, and in Spanish the seam is still visible
in the word.
