You'll want to know: yéye.
yéye — "grandpa on the father's side".
wǒ yéye jīnnián bāshí suì — My grandpa is eighty this year.
Its characters come later; for now it is a word for the ear.

The word, taken apart.
yéye is one syllable, yé, said twice, the second time with no tone. No dictionary we use records an older form, so its history is not settled.

Lesson 2 of 7.
nǎinai — grandma on the father's side.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Mandarin for satisfied, then the Mandarin for grandpa on the father's side.

You'll want to know: nǎinai.
nǎinai — "grandma on the father's side".
nǎinai hé yéye dōu hěn hǎo — My grandma and grandpa are both well.
Its characters come later; for now it is a word for the ear.

The word, taken apart.
nǎinai is built from nǎi, said twice, the second time with no tone. It is inherited from Middle Chinese nreaX (Guangyun, in Baxter's transcription), which spells the character another way and glosses it 'milk', the nǎi of niúnǎi; read nejX, the same character is glossed as what the people of Chu called their mother. How it came to mean a father's mother is not settled.

Lesson 3 of 7.
小孩 (xiǎohái) — child; kid.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Mandarin for grandpa on the father's side, then the Mandarin for grandma on the father's side.

You'll want to know: 小孩.
小孩 — xiǎohái — "child; kid".
那个小孩八岁 nàge xiǎohái bā suì — That kid is eight.
The new character, in pieces: 孩 (hái) — nine strokes: 子 on the left, its last stroke rising; on the right a dot, a bar, a stroke that falls to the left and turns up, two strokes falling to the left, and a dot. The next lesson puts it in your hand.

The word, taken apart.
小孩 (xiǎohái) is built from 小 'small' and 孩 (hái) 'a child', both inherited from Middle Chinese, sjewX and hoj (Guangyun, in Baxter's transcription), which glosses the second 'a newborn child'.

Script — 子, then a dot, a bar and four strokes.
孩 (hái).
Two pieces, left before right.
The left piece, 子, three strokes. A bar that turns down to the left, a vertical with a hook, then the last stroke: on the left of a character it rises to the right instead of lying flat.
The right piece, six strokes. A dot on top. A bar under it. A stroke that falls to the left and turns up to the right. A long stroke falling to the left. A shorter stroke falling to the left under it. Then a dot at the lower right.
3 + 6 = nine.

[once you have stopped driving — this part needs your eyes or your hands; if you are driving, skip ahead to the next part]
Guided Practice.
[once you have stopped driving — write: 孩 (hái), all nine, with the model in front of you]
[once you have stopped driving — cover: write it again from memory, and count the pieces, not the strokes]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
How many strokes in 孩 (hái)? (Nine.) Which piece is on the left? (子.) What changes in 子 here? (Its last stroke rises to the right.)
Source: Hanzi Writer Data 孩 (hái), pinned stroke paths and medians.


Lesson 5 of 7.
子女 (zǐnǚ) — sons and daughters; children.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Mandarin for grandma on the father's side, then the Mandarin for a kid.

You'll want to know: 子女.
子女 — zǐnǚ — "sons and daughters; children".
他们有三个子女 tāmen yǒu sān ge zǐnǚ — They have three children.
Two third tones meet in this word: zǐ is said with a rising tone before nǚ, and the spelling keeps both marks.

The word, taken apart.
子女 (zǐnǚ) is built from 子 'a son' and 女 'a daughter', both inherited from Middle Chinese, tsiX and nrjoX (Guangyun, in Baxter's transcription).

qīnqi — "relative; family member".

The word, taken apart.
qīnqi is built from qīn 'close' and qi, both inherited from Middle Chinese, tshin and tshek (Guangyun, in Baxter's transcription), which glosses the first 'to love' and 'near', and the second with this very word. In the word the second loses its tone.
