Persian, chapter 291: Then, And In The End --- A Story In Order.
6 lessons. You can do the first one in the car; after that you will want to have stopped.


Lesson 1 of 6.
تعریف کردن (ta'rif kardan) — to tell what happened.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say "a story", then "I was reading". (Dâstân, mikhândam.)
[pause 1 second]
Chapter two hundred and ninety gave you the two pasts a story is made of. This is the verb for telling one.

You'll want to know: تعریف کردن.
تعریف کردن — ta'rif kardan — "to tell", of a story or of what happened.
او داستان سفر را تعریف کرد. — u dâstân-e safar râ ta'rif kard — She told the story of the trip.
It runs on kardan, so the tenses come for free: ta'rif mikonam, I tell; ta'rif kardam, I told; ta'rif mikardam, I was telling.
Put az, "from", in front of a thing and the verb turns to praise: از هتل تعریف کرد, az hotel ta'rif kard, "he spoke well of the hotel".
Say it ta'-rif, with a small catch after ta, as if the word drew breath. That catch is a letter of its own, and the next lesson puts it in your hand.

The word, taken apart.
ta'rif is borrowed from Arabic taʿrīf (EtymDB 2.1). It is usually explained as "making known", a noun on the root ʿ-r-f of Arabic ʿarafa, "to know". Kardan is the Persian verb "to do" from chapter seven. So ta'rif kardan is "to do a making-known": you tell a story, and now the listener knows it. To praise a hotel is to make its good side known.

[once you have stopped driving — this part needs your eyes or your hands; if you are driving, skip ahead to the next part]
Script you'll notice: ع.
ع — 'eyn, the small catch written ' in this book's romanization.
It is the second letter of تعریف, straight after ت. In Persian it is a light stop in the throat, ta'-rif, or no more than a slightly longer vowel before it. Arabic says it deeper in the throat; Persian does not.
It changes shape more than most letters. At the start of a word it is an open hook, عـ. In the middle it closes into a small flat loop, ـعـ, which is the shape inside تعریف. Standing alone, ع ('eyn), the hook sits on a tail that curls down below the line.
Almost every Persian word with ع ('eyn) came from Arabic, and Persian has thousands of them.

Writing: ع.
Put your pen on ع ('eyn) and follow its line. Copy the shape you can see — slowly, and larger than it is printed.
This book does not yet tell you where to start this letter or which way to travel. It is not written down here until it can be written down with a source. Copying what is in front of you needs no such source, and it is how the shape gets into your hand in the meantime.

Lesson 3 of 6.
اول، بعد، در آخر (avval, ba'd, dar âkhar) — first, then, in the end.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say "first", then "then, afterwards", then "at last". (Avval, sepas, bâlâkhare.)
[pause 1 second]
You hold the words already. This lesson strings them through a run of events, and adds the one people use most in speech.

Grammar Lens: three events, in order.
اول صبحانه خوردم. بعد به کتابخانه رفتم. در آخر به خانه برگشتم. avval sobhâne khordam. ba'd be ketâbkhâne raftam. dar âkhar be khâne bargashtam. First I had breakfast. Then I went to the library. In the end I went back home.
بعد, ba'd, "then", opens the next event. It does the job sepas does, and it is the one you hear at a kitchen table; sepas sounds written.
With az after it, ba'd az is "after": بعد از صبحانه, ba'd az sobhâne, "after breakfast". Pas az, from chapter one hundred and fifty-two, means the same and sounds more formal.
The events stay in the plain past, one after another, each with its edge.

Grammar Lens: in the end, and at last.
در آخر, dar âkhar, is "in the end": dar, "in", and âkhar, "the end". It closes the run.
Bâlâkhare, "at last", closes it too, with relief in it: the thing you were waiting for has come. Bâlâkhare residam: I got there at last.

The word, taken apart.
ba'd is borrowed from Arabic baʿd, "after" (EtymDB 2.1), and it carries the catch you wrote one lesson ago: ب, then ع ('eyn), then د. Avval and âkhar are Arabic as well (EtymDB 2.1). In these three sentences the words that order the events are Arabic, and the verbs are Persian.

Lesson 4 of 6.
وقتی (vaqti) — when, and a story told in order.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say "first", "then", "in the end". (Avval, ba'd, dar âkhar.) Then ask "when?" (Che vaqt?)

Grammar Lens: vaqti, the joint for a moment.
وقتی, vaqti, is "when" in the middle of a story, not in a question. It ties what follows to a moment:
وقتی به هتل رسیدم، باد میوزید. — vaqti be hotel residam, bâd mivazid — When I got to the hotel, the wind was blowing.
Vaqti takes the event, residam, and the rest paints what was going on at that moment, mivazid.
It is vaqt, "time", with -i: "at the time that". Vaqt is the word inside che vaqt and sar-e vaqt. The letter ق is written q here, so chapter seventy's ghatâr, "a train", appears as qatâr.

Grammar Lens: bâ, "with".
با, bâ, is "with": bâ yek dust, with a friend. For travel it is "by": bâ qatâr, by train. And bâ ham is "together".

Grammar Lens: the order a told story keeps.
A story you tell, that you ta'rif mikonid, keeps three parts in one order. First the time and the scene, in bud and the mi- past: sâl-e gozashte... havâ sard bud. Then the events, in the plain past, strung on avval, ba'd and vaqti. Then the close, with dar âkhar.
سال گذشته با قطار به تهران رفتم. وقتی رسیدم، هوا سرد بود. اول به هتل رفتم. بعد با یک دوست شام خوردم. در آخر به هتل برگشتم. sâl-e gozashte bâ qatâr be Tehrân raftam. vaqti residam, havâ sard bud. avval be hotel raftam. ba'd bâ yek dust shâm khordam. dar âkhar be hotel bargashtam. Last year I went to Tehran by train. When I arrived, it was cold. First I went to the hotel. Then I had dinner with a friend. In the end I went back to the hotel.

Why it's said this way.

Warm-up.

What you've built.
Two chapters, and a whole story fits in your mouth. The time first, with gozashte. The scene in bud and the mi- past. The events in the plain past, strung on avval, ba'd and vaqti. The close with dar âkhar. Bâ tells you who came along and how you travelled, and ta'rif kardan is what you are doing while you say it.
