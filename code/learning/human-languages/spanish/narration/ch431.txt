Spanish, chapter 431: El Pedido Y La Devolución.
7 lessons. All 7 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 7.
el pedido — what you asked for.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
You can say comprar and la tienda. Now say the thing itself — the parcel that has not arrived yet — and see whether you have a word for it.

You'll want to know: el pedido.
el pedido — the order.
Not the act of ordering: the thing ordered. The box on its way to you, the line on the restaurant's docket, the number you quote on the phone.

The word, taken apart.
el pedido is the past participle of pedir, to ask for, used as a noun. What was asked for. Spanish does this constantly, and once you see it you will see it everywhere:
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
verb: pedir. the noun made from it: el pedido.
verb: llamar. the noun made from it: la llamada.
verb: entrar. the noun made from it: la entrada.
You already hold la entrada for a ticket. It is the entered thing by the same move. A participle that hardens into a noun is one of the cheapest ways this language makes new words, and it costs you no new ending.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: el pedido]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: I bought it in the shop, and the order has not arrived]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: which verb el pedido was made from]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
The order? (el pedido.) From which verb? (pedir.) Does it mean the asking or the thing asked for? (The thing.)


Lesson 2 of 7.
devolver — to turn it back.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say el pedido. Say pagar. The order came, you paid, and it is the wrong size. Say what you do next.

You'll want to know: devolver.
devolver — to give back, to return a thing.
A thing, not a place. You devolver a jumper, a book, a borrowed charger. For going back somewhere yourself, Spanish uses volver on its own.

The word, taken apart.
de- back, plus volver, to turn. To turn a thing back.
That is the whole word, and it tells you the grammar too: devolver turns exactly like volver, which you already say. The o becomes ue when the stress lands on it:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
form: yo devuelvo, I give back.
form: nosotros devolvemos, we give back.
The stress moves off the o in devolvemos, and the ue goes with it. This is the same pattern as poder / puedo and dormir / duermo; learning it once buys you a whole family of verbs.

Grammar Lens: the two ways back.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
you mean: I return the order. you say: devuelvo el pedido.
you mean: I return home. you say: vuelvo a casa.
English uses return for both and lets context sort it out. Spanish splits them, and the split is not a nuisance — it is the prefix doing exactly what it says.

Guided Practice.

[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: I want to return the order]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: I paid and now I am returning it]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: the difference between devuelvo and vuelvo]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
To give a thing back? (devolver.) And to go back yourself? (volver.) What does the de- add? (Back.)


Lesson 3 of 7.
el retraso — put further behind.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say el pedido. Say devolver. The order is not late enough to return yet — it is only late. Say that.

You'll want to know: el retraso.
el retraso — the delay.
con retraso — late, running behind. That is the phrase you will hear on a platform: el tren sale con veinte minutos de retraso.

The word, taken apart.
re- again, plus tras, behind. Something put further behind than it should be.
tras is worth a moment on its own, because it is hiding in words you will meet all week:
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
word: detrás. the behind in it: behind, in place.
word: atrasar. the behind in it: to put back.
word: el retraso. the behind in it: the being-behind.
Spanish also has tardar, to take time, from a different Latin word for slow. Two families, one idea, and a speaker picks whichever fits: a train lleva retraso, a journey tarda two hours.

[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: the order is arriving late]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: the train leaves with twenty minutes' delay]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
The delay? (el retraso.) Running late? (con retraso.) What does tras mean on its own? (Behind.)


Lesson 4 of 7.
el proveedor — the one further up the line.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say la tienda. Say el retraso. The shop says the delay is not their fault. Whose, then?

You'll want to know: el proveedor.
el proveedor — the supplier.
The company that supplies the shop. You rarely deal with one; you hear about one when something has gone wrong: un problema con nuestro proveedor.

The word, taken apart.
proveer, to provide, plus -dor, the ending that makes a doer.
That ending is worth more to you than this one word, because it is completely regular and it turns verbs you already hold into people and machines:
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
verb: proveer. the doer: el proveedor.
verb: trabajar. the doer: el trabajador.
verb: vender. the doer: el vendedor.
Once you notice -dor, a long unfamiliar noun often decomposes into a verb you know plus the one who does it. That is a reading strategy, not a word.

[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: the doer-word made from trabajar]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
The supplier? (el proveedor.) What does -dor add? (The one who does it.) From which verb? (proveer.)


Lesson 5 of 7.
cobrar — the other end of paying.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say pagar. Say el dinero. You have had both a long time. Now say what the shop does, from its own side of the counter.

You'll want to know: cobrar.
cobrar — to charge somebody, to collect money, to get paid.
One verb for all three, and which one it means depends on who is speaking.

Grammar Lens: who is on each end.
pagar and cobrar are the same transaction from opposite sides:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
who: the customer. verb: paga.
who: the shop. verb: cobra.
So me cobraron treinta euros — they charged me thirty. And cobro el viernes — I get paid on Friday, said by somebody with a job. English needs three different verbs (charge, collect, get paid) where Spanish holds one, and the one holds because the money is always moving toward the speaker.
That is also why no se cobra nada on a shop notice means nothing will be charged to you.

[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: they charged me thirty euros]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
The customer does what? (paga.) And the shop? (cobra.) What does no se cobra nada tell you? (You will not be charged.)


Lesson 6 of 7.
(pedido, devolver, retraso, proveedor, cobrar) — from cold.

Warm-up.
[pause 3 seconds]
Close the lessons before this one. Say the order, to give back and the delay.

Grammar Lens: three of the five were built, not learned.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
word: el pedido. made from: pedir. by: a participle used as a noun.
word: el proveedor. made from: proveer. by: the doer-ending -dor.
word: devolver. made from: volver. by: the prefix de-, back.
Three of this chapter's five words are a piece you already had plus one move. Do not read a rule into it — plenty of Spanish words are simple and whole. What is worth carrying is the habit of looking for the join before deciding a long word is new.

What you've built.
You could buy things and pay for them, and you could not say what happens when it goes wrong. Comprar, pagar, la tienda and el dinero have been yours for a long time, and between them they cover only the half of a transaction where everybody is content.
This chapter adds the other half: the thing itself (el pedido), it arriving late (el retraso), whose fault the shop says that is (el proveedor), sending it back (devolver) and what you are or are not charged (cobrar).
