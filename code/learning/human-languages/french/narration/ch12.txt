Lesson 1 of 2.
onze becomes seize — six numbers welded together.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, because the lesson points at something written down and your eyes for one table that cannot be read aloud. You can listen to everything else now — leave the section called The six fusions until you have stopped, and I will say so again when we reach it.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
You can count to ten. Now the teens — and they are not built the way you'd guess. French didn't say "ten-one, ten-two." It inherited six numbers already fused in Latin, and they came through almost unrecognisable.

The six fusions.
There is a table here I cannot read to you — 4 columns and 6 rows, and it has more columns than can be held in the ear at once. Its columns are: one with no heading, French, from Latin, literally. Come back and look at it when you have stopped.
Look at the ending they all share: -ze. That is Latin decem ("ten"), worn down to almost nothing — the same ten you already know in dix (10) and décembre (the old 10th month, from Chapter 9). One root, three disguises.
And the front of each word is the digit you learned in Chapter 6: un becomes on-, deux becomes dou-, trois becomes trei-, quatre becomes quator-, cinq becomes quin-, six becomes sei-. Say them slowly and the small number surfaces.

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Count 11 to 16. (Onze, douze, treize, quatorze, quinze, seize.) What does the shared -ze ending come from? (Latin decem, "ten" — the same ten as dix and décembre.) Is 13 built as "ten-three" or "three-ten"? ("Three- ten" — trēdecim, the digit first.) Next: at 17, French suddenly changes its mind.


Lesson 2 of 2.
dix-sept becomes vingt — where French changes its mind.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Here is something you can watch happen. Up to seize (16), French used the old welded-together Latin numbers. Then, at 17, it simply gives up — and starts saying the numbers plainly, the way a child would.

The seam.
[a table of 5 rows, read aloud]
16. French: seize. how it's built: one fused word ( from sēdecim).
17. French: dix-sept. how it's built: "ten-seven" — two words, plainly.
18. French: dix-huit. how it's built: "ten-eight".
19. French: dix-neuf. how it's built: "ten-nine".
20. French: vingt. how it's built: from vīgintī.
Notice the order flips too. In seize, the digit came first ("six-ten"). In dix-sept, the ten comes first ("ten-seven"). At exactly 17, French switched both its strategy and its direction — a visible seam in the counting system. (Latin itself wobbled here: it had septendecim for 17, but for 18 and 19 it counted down from twenty — duodēvīgintī, "two-from-twenty." The sisters all abandoned that subtraction trick.)
vingt (20) from Latin vīgintī, the root behind English vigesimal ("counting by twenties") — and the reason French later builds quatre-vingts (80 = "four twenties").

Guided Practice.

[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: the seam — "seize = six-ten (fused); dix-sept = ten-seven (plain)"]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: the whole run, 11 to 20]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
At which number does French stop fusing? (At 17 — seize is fused, dix-sept is plain "ten-seven".) What two things change at that seam? (The strategy — fused becomes transparent — and the order, digit-first becomes ten-first.) What English word comes from vingt's root? (Vigesimal, from vīgintī.)
