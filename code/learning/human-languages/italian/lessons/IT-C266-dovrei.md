# dovrei — what you ought to do about it

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[IT-LEX-C86-VOLERE86-02, IT-GRAMMAR-DUE-FRECCE-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Ask for a coffee politely. (*Vorrei un caffè.*) Then run a reason
forward: "I'm tired, so I'm staying at home." (*Sono stanco, quindi resto a
casa.*)

[PAUSE 1s] An explanation usually ends on a third move: what somebody ought to
do. That move has its own form.

## You'll want to know: dovrei
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[IT-LEX-DOVREI-01]; assesses=[IT-LEX-C85-POTERE85-02, IT-LEX-C48-VERBI2-05, IT-LEX-C60-VERBI5-02] -->

You have **dovere**, "to have to, must". Soften it and it becomes advice:

> **Dovrei** studiare. — I should study.
> **Dovresti** dormire. — You should sleep. *(to a friend)*
> **Dovrebbe** riposare. — He or she should rest; and, to someone you call
> *Lei*, "you should rest".

A bare infinitive follows, with no *di* and no *che*, exactly as after
*vorrei*. And listen to the ending: **dovrei**, **vorrei**. It is the same
*-ei*. Both are the **condizionale**, the "would" form of Italian, which you
have been using since chapter eighty-six without its name. Chapter
two hundred and seventy-two builds it properly. For now, two forms of it are
yours.

*Devo studiare* is "I must study": no way out. *Dovrei studiare* is "I should":
I know I ought to, and I have not promised. That softness is the register an
explanation usually wants.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[IT-ETYMON-DEBERE-02]; assesses=[] -->

**dovere** is inherited from Latin *dēbēre*, "to owe" (EtymDB 2.1), and
*dēbēre* is a compound: *dē*, "from", plus *habēre*, "to have" (Lewis &
Short). To owe is to **have something from someone**: you are holding what is
theirs. You met the noun in chapter two hundred and fifty-three: **il
debito**, "a debt". English took the same Latin word as **debt**, **due** and
**duty**.
