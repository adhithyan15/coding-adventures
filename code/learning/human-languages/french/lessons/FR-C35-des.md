# des — the plural of *un*, and the last shape of *some*

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[FR-GRAM-UN-UNE-02, FR-GRAM-DE-LA-01, FR-GRAM-DU-PARTITIF-07] -->

[PAUSE 2s] **un café** is *a café*. Say the plural. English drops the word
entirely — *cafés* — and French cannot.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[FR-GRAM-DES-01]; assesses=[FR-GRAM-UN-UNE-02, FR-GRAM-DE-LA-01, FR-GRAM-LE-LA-GENDER-05, FR-LEX-CAFE-01, FR-GRAM-CEST-01] -->

> **des** — some; and the plural of **un** and **une**.

> **un café → des cafés** · **une amie → des amies**

> **Il y a des cafés.** — There are cafés.

**des** is **de** + **les**, contracted exactly as *du* is *de* + *le*. So it
completes two systems at once and adds no new machinery to either.

Here English is the language with the hole. English has *a* and no plural of it,
so it says *books*, *cafés*, *friends* with nothing in front. French will not
leave the slot empty, and a sentence with a bare plural noun in it sounds
unfinished to a French ear. That is the single commonest mistake an English
speaker makes in French, and it is a missing word rather than a wrong one.

The **s** is silent, and it comes back as a **z** before a vowel: *des amies*
is *day-ZAH-mee*.
