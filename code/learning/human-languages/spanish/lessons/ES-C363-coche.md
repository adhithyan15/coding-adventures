## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-C363-WHERE-41]; assesses=[] -->

**el coche** — "the car."

A village did this. **Kocs** is a small place west of Budapest, and in the
fifteenth century its wagon-makers worked out how to hang a carriage body on
straps, so that the passengers stopped being shaken to pieces on a long road.
Hungarian called the result a **kocsi szekér**, a "cart from Kocs", then dropped
the *szekér* and kept **kocsi**, "the Kocs one". It is attested in that sense
from 1469.

Europe wanted the ride, and took the name with it. German made **Kutsche** of
it, French made **coche**, and Spanish took *coche* from French. English took it
too and spelled it **coach** by the 1550s. So *coche* and *coach* are one word
in two spellings, and underneath both of them there is still a village.

Then English did something odd with it. At Oxford and Cambridge, a tutor who
carried a struggling student through an examination was called a **coach** —
student slang, the tutor as vehicle. Sport borrowed the joke, and now a coach
shouts from the touchline with no memory at all of the wagon-makers.

One warning, and it matters the moment you travel: *coche* is the car in
**Spain**. Across much of Latin America the car is *carro* or *auto* instead,
and in some countries *coche* means a pram, or is a word nobody reaches for.
