\section{\emph{\^{e}tre} --- ``to be''}
\label{lesson:etre}

The other verb everything is built on. \emph{Avoir} was one half; this is its
partner --- and it is the most irregular verb in French.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}
\toprule
Singular & Plural \\
\midrule
\emph{je \textbf{suis}} (I am) & \emph{nous \textbf{sommes}} (we are) \\
\emph{tu \textbf{es}} (you are) & \emph{vous \textbf{\^{e}tes}} (you are) \\
\emph{il/elle \textbf{est}} (he/she is) & \emph{ils/elles \textbf{sont}} (they are) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

There is no pattern to find. Don't look for one --- these are among the oldest
word-shapes in Europe, worn smooth by three thousand years of daily use.

\begin{grammarlens}
\textbf{Three stems in a trench coat.} Line the forms up against Latin and the
seams show.
\end{grammarlens}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}
\toprule
French & $\leftarrow$ Latin \\
\midrule
\emph{je \textbf{suis}} & \emph{sum} --- ``I am'' \\
\emph{tu \textbf{es}} & \emph{es} \\
\emph{il \textbf{est}} & \emph{est} \\
\emph{nous \textbf{sommes}} & \emph{sumus} \\
\emph{ils \textbf{sont}} & \emph{sunt} \\
\emph{\textbf{\^{e}tre}} & \emph{*essere}, Vulgar Latin's remodelling of \emph{esse} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

All of those come from \emph{esse}, ``to be'' --- the infinitive included. Now
the pieces that come from somewhere else entirely:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}
\toprule
French & $\leftarrow$ Latin \\
\midrule
\emph{je \textbf{fus}}, \emph{il \textbf{fut}} (\emph{pass\'{e} simple}) & \emph{fu\={\i}} --- an ancient perfect stem \\
\emph{\textbf{\'{e}t\'{e}}} (past participle) & \emph{st\=are} --- ``to stand'' \\
\emph{\textbf{\'{e}tant}} (present participle) & \emph{st\=are} again (\emph{stantem}) \\
\emph{j'\textbf{\'{e}tais}}, \emph{il \textbf{\'{e}tait}} (imperfect) & \emph{st\=are} again (\emph{st\=abam}) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{cousinweb}
\emph{Fus} and \emph{\'{e}t\'{e}} are not related to \emph{suis} at all. The
first is an ancient perfect stem (from Proto-Indo-European \emph{*b$^h$uH-},
``grow, become'' --- the source of English \textbf{be}); the second is the Latin
verb for \textbf{standing}.\\[3pt]
And look how much of \emph{st\=are} is in there. It isn't one stray form:
\emph{every} French \emph{\^{e}tre} form beginning \emph{\'{e}t-} comes from the
standing verb --- \emph{\'{e}t\'{e}}, \emph{\'{e}tant}, \emph{\'{e}tais},
\emph{\'{e}tait}. A whole limb of the verb.
\end{cousinweb}

\begin{grammarlens}
Three stems, then: \emph{es-} for the present and infinitive, \emph{fu-} for the
\emph{pass\'{e} simple}, \emph{\'{e}t-} for the participles and the imperfect.
That patching is called \textbf{suppletion} --- one verb's paradigm filled in
with pieces of another, exactly like English \emph{go} / \emph{went} (where
\emph{went} was stolen from \emph{wend}).
\end{grammarlens}

\begin{culture}
\textbf{The Spanish connection.} \emph{St\=are} should look familiar. It is the
verb Spanish turned into \emph{estar}, its second ``to be,'' held apart from
\emph{ser}. So Spanish has \emph{ser} $\leftarrow$ \emph{esse} and \emph{estar}
$\leftarrow$ \emph{st\=are}, kept as two separate verbs; French has one verb,
\emph{\^{e}tre} $\leftarrow$ \emph{esse}, having absorbed \emph{st\=are}'s whole
\emph{\'{e}t-} limb.\\[3pt]
Spanish kept the two verbs \emph{apart} and made you choose between them. French
kept one verb and swallowed a large piece of the other. Same two Latin sources,
opposite solutions.\\[3pt]
(\emph{St\=are} also left French words outside \emph{\^{e}tre} ---
\emph{rester} $\leftarrow$ \emph{re-st\=are}, ``to stay,'' and \emph{co\^{u}ter}
$\leftarrow$ \emph{const\=are}. What it did \emph{not} do is survive as a
separate ``to be'' the way \emph{estar} did.)
\end{culture}

\begin{sounds}
\textbf{Practice.} Say the six: \emph{je suis, tu es, il est, nous sommes, vous
\^{e}tes, ils sont}. Say two whole sentences: \emph{Je suis fran\c{c}ais},
\emph{Je suis fatigu\'{e}}. Then say the seam --- \emph{suis} $\leftarrow$
\emph{sum} \ldots{} \emph{fus} $\leftarrow$ \emph{fu\={\i}} \ldots{}
\emph{\'{e}t-} $\leftarrow$ \emph{st\=are}. And the English parallel:
\emph{go} / \emph{went}.
\end{sounds}

\section{\emph{je suis all\'{e}} --- the past built from ``be''}
\label{lesson:passe-compose-etre}

The last chapter built the past with \emph{avoir}: \emph{j'ai parl\'{e}}. But a
small, important group of verbs refuses \emph{avoir} and takes
\emph{\^{e}tre} instead --- and when they do, something visible happens to the
participle.

\begin{center}\itshape
Verbs of motion and change of state take \^{e}tre.
\end{center}

\emph{Aller} (``to go'') is the headline case:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}
\toprule
French & English \\
\midrule
\emph{je \textbf{suis} all\'{e}} & I went \\
\emph{tu \textbf{es} all\'{e}} & you went \\
\emph{il \textbf{est} all\'{e}} & he went \\
\emph{nous \textbf{sommes} all\'{e}s} & we went \\
\emph{ils \textbf{sont} all\'{e}s} & they went \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Others in the family: \emph{venir} (come), \emph{partir} (leave),
\emph{arriver} (arrive), \emph{entrer}, \emph{sortir}, \emph{monter},
\emph{descendre}, \emph{na\^{i}tre} (be born), \emph{mourir} (die),
\emph{rester} (stay), \emph{tomber} (fall). Read that list as a shape rather
than a list: \textbf{going, coming, being born, dying} --- arriving and leaving,
in space or in life.

Plus one whole extra family: all pronominal verbs take \emph{\^{e}tre} ---
\emph{je me suis lev\'{e}}, \emph{elle s'est lav\'{e}e}. Careful, though: they
take \emph{\^{e}tre} but they do \emph{not} follow \emph{\^{e}tre}'s agreement
rule. See the warning at the end of this section.

\begin{grammarlens}
Two warnings, so the rule doesn't mislead you.\\[3pt]
\textbf{Not every motion verb qualifies.} \emph{Marcher, courir, nager, danser,
voyager} all take \emph{avoir} (\emph{j'ai couru}). The \emph{\^{e}tre} verbs
are about arriving, leaving, and changing state --- not about moving your
legs.\\[3pt]
\textbf{Some switch when they take an object.} \emph{Monter, descendre, sortir,
rentrer, passer} take \emph{\^{e}tre} alone (\emph{je suis mont\'{e}}, ``I went
up'') but \emph{avoir} with a direct object (\emph{j'ai mont\'{e} les valises},
``I took the suitcases up'').
\end{grammarlens}

\textbf{The participle agrees with the subject.} This is the part you can see
and hear:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}
\toprule
French & English \\
\midrule
\emph{Il est \textbf{all\'{e}}.} & He went. \\
\emph{Elle est \textbf{all\'{e}e}.} & She went. \\
\emph{Ils sont \textbf{all\'{e}s}.} & They went. \\
\emph{Elles sont \textbf{all\'{e}es}.} & They (f.) went. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The participle takes the subject's gender and number --- exactly like an
adjective.

\begin{grammarlens}
\textbf{Why?} The same fossil as the last chapter. \emph{Elle est all\'{e}e}
began as a plain description: ``\emph{she is} gone'' --- \emph{gone} describing
\emph{her}, the way \emph{elle est fatigu\'{e}e} describes her. It was never an
action she performed on something; it was a state she was in. So the participle
agreed with her, and it still does.
\end{grammarlens}

Compare the two halves of the system:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
Auxiliary & Participle agrees with & Because it was\ldots \\
\midrule
\emph{avoir} & a \textbf{preceding object} (\emph{les lettres que j'ai \'{e}crites}) & ``I have letters written'' \\
\emph{\^{e}tre} & the \textbf{subject} (\emph{elle est all\'{e}e}) & ``she is gone'' \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

One rule underneath both: \textbf{the participle was an adjective, and it agrees
with whatever it described.}

\begin{grammarlens}
\textbf{The pronominal exception.} Pronominal verbs take \emph{\^{e}tre} --- but
their agreement follows the \emph{avoir} rule, not the \emph{\^{e}tre} one. They
agree with a preceding direct object, which usually \emph{is} the reflexive
pronoun, and sometimes isn't.\\[3pt]
\emph{Elle s'est \textbf{lav\'{e}e}.} --- She washed \emph{herself};
\emph{se} is the direct object, so it agrees.\\[3pt]
\emph{Elle s'est \textbf{lav\'{e}} les mains.} --- She washed \emph{her hands};
the object is \emph{les mains}, and it follows, so no agreement.\\[3pt]
\emph{Elles se sont \textbf{parl\'{e}}.} --- They spoke \emph{to each other};
\emph{se} is indirect, so no agreement.\\[3pt]
Which is, satisfyingly, the same rule as the last chapter all along. The
auxiliary looks like \emph{\^{e}tre}; the agreement behaves like \emph{avoir}.
Don't let the first mislead you about the second.
\end{grammarlens}

One group in particular escapes even that. Verbs that exist \emph{only} in
pronominal form --- \emph{se souvenir} (remember), \emph{s'enfuir} (flee),
\emph{s'\'{e}vanouir} (faint) --- have no non-reflexive version for the pronoun
to be an object \emph{of}, so they simply agree with the subject: \emph{elles se
sont \textbf{souvenues}}.

\begin{sounds}
\textbf{Practice.} Say \emph{je suis all\'{e}, il est all\'{e}, elle est
all\'{e}e}. Say the contrast --- \emph{j'\textbf{ai} parl\'{e}} \ldots{}
\emph{je \textbf{suis} all\'{e}}. Say the shape of the list: going, coming,
born, dying. And say the reason out loud: ``\emph{she is gone}'' ---
\emph{gone} describes \emph{her}.
\end{sounds}

You now have two verbs holding up the whole language, \emph{avoir} and
\emph{\^{e}tre}; a past tense built from each; and the single old idea --- the
participle as an adjective --- that explains both. That is a working past. The
next volume turns from what has happened to what is in front of you:
describing what you see.
