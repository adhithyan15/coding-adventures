Spanish, chapter 471: El curso de cocina.
8 lessons. All 8 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 8.
demasiado — too much.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say bastante and poco. You have a scale with a bottom and a middle. Today it gets its top.

You'll want to know: demasiado.
demasiado — too much, too many, too.
El curso era demasiado difícil para mí.
Before an adjective it does not change: demasiado difícil, demasiado avanzada. Before a noun it agrees like any adjective: demasiada gente, demasiados libros.

Grammar Lens: the word that says it went past the point.
Bastante built you a scale of degree. This is the member above the top of it, and the step matters.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
the word: poco. where on the scale: below what is wanted.
the word: bastante. where on the scale: enough — the point itself.
the word: demasiado. where on the scale: past the point.
Muy difícil is a judgement about the thing. Demasiado difícil is a judgement about a fit: too hard for something, usually for you. That is why it so often arrives with para — demasiado avanzado para mí.
English blurs this, because too and very sit close together in speech. Spanish does not: say muy when you mean intense, and demasiado when you mean it has gone past what works.

The word, taken apart.
Underneath is de más — of more, in excess — which became demasía, an excess, and then this adjective. The más in it is the más you already use, from Latin magis, more.
So the word is built out of an admission: there is more here than the situation has room for. Not more than exists, more than fits.
Keep that and the grammar follows. Demasiado always implies a measure it has overrun, even when nobody says what the measure was.

Lesson 2 of 8.
el nivel — the level.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say el curso and demasiado. A class has one of these, and when it is wrong the second word is how you say so.

You'll want to know: el nivel.
el nivel — the level.
El nivel era demasiado alto para ella.
Nivel is what a class, a language exam or a game has. ¿Qué nivel tienes? is the ordinary way to ask how far along somebody is. Note the stress: ni-VEL, on the end, and the plural is los niveles.
It is also a level in the physical sense — a floor of a building, the surface of water, the tool a builder uses.

Grammar Lens: the phrase the question will use.
Two ways of saying the same complaint, and you need to recognise both.
[a table of 1 row, read aloud]
about the class: el curso era demasiado avanzado. about its level: el nivel era demasiado alto.
A speaker will usually say the first. Somebody reporting it afterwards — a summary, a question, an exam option — will often reach for the second, because nivel lets you talk about the difficulty without naming the class again.
Alto and bajo are what nivel takes, not grande or mucho. A level is a height.

The word, taken apart.
El litro already told you about Latin libra, the balance — hiding in lb, in Libra the scales in the sky, in deliberate and equilibrium.
Nivel is the same word, made small. Latin libella is a little libra, a builder's level: a tiny balance that tells you when a surface is true. It reached Spanish through French, and on the way the first l turned into an n — livel became nivel — because two l sounds so close together pull apart.
English took libella too, without that swap, and got level.
So nivel and litro are cousins, and a level is not a measure of height so much as an instrument for finding balance.

Lesson 3 of 8.
avanzado — advanced.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say el curso and antes. The second is hiding inside today's word, and you would not guess it from the outside.

You'll want to know: avanzado.
avanzado — advanced.
El curso de los lunes era demasiado avanzado para mí.
Un nivel avanzado, un grupo avanzado: it describes the class, not the person. For the person you say tiene un nivel avanzado.
It agrees like any adjective — avanzada, avanzados, avanzadas.

Grammar Lens: a participle doing an adjective's work.
Avanzado is the past participle of avanzar, to advance, standing in as an adjective. Spanish does this constantly, and you have seen it before:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
the participle: invitado. what it describes: the one who was invited.
the participle: avanzado. what it describes: the one that has moved ahead.
El invitado made the point first — Spanish names a person by what was done to them. Here the same move names a course by how far it has got.
So a curso avanzado is not a course that is difficult in itself. It is one that has already travelled, and expects you to have travelled with it. That distinction is the whole reason the woman in the recording changed groups.

The word, taken apart.
Avanzar is Latin ab ante — from in front — which became abantiare and then the Spanish verb. The ante in it is the same ante you already hold in antes.
To advance is to put yourself in front. Not faster; further along.
One thing worth knowing about the English. Advance has a d that does not belong to it: the word arrived as avancer, and English later added the d on the mistaken assumption that the av- was a worn-down Latin ad-. It was not. Spanish never made that mistake, which is why avanzar has no d and never did.

Lesson 4 of 8.
el principiante — the beginner.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say el principio. Say el estudiante. One gives today's word its meaning and the other gives it its ending — try it before reading on.

You'll want to know: el principiante.
el principiante — the beginner.
Ahora voy con los principiantes y disfruto mucho más.
La principiante for a woman. It is the natural opposite of what you learned a moment ago:
[a table of 1 row, read aloud]
the class: un curso avanzado. the person in it: un principiante.

Grammar Lens: you could have built this one.
El estudiante gave you the rule outright: verb plus -ante names the person doing it. Estudiar, cut the -ar, add -ante.
Here it is again, one step further back. From el principio, the beginning, Spanish makes the verb principiar, to begin. Take that, cut the -ar, add -ante:
principiar becomes principiante.
The one who is beginning. The ending has not changed its job since chapter nine; the only new thing is the verb it is sitting on.
English wore the same ending down to -ant, which is why participant, assistant and inhabitant all name the doer too.

The word, taken apart.
El principio already traced this for you: Latin principium, from princeps, the one who takes first place — the same first-taking inside English prince, principal and principle.
So a principiante is not somebody who is bad at the thing. The word says only that they are at the start of it. Spanish has kept that neutral, and so should you when you use it about yourself.

Lesson 5 of 8.
sencillo — simple.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say difícil. Say el principiante. Today's word is what a beginner's class is full of, and it is not the opposite of the first one.

You'll want to know: sencillo.
sencillo — simple, plain, straightforward.
Hacemos cosas sencillas, pero las hacemos bien.
Una comida sencilla — a plain meal. Un billete sencillo — a one-way ticket, where the word means single rather than easy.

Grammar Lens: sencillo is not fácil.
English lets simple and easy stand in for each other. Spanish keeps them apart, and the line runs between parts and effort.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
the word: sencillo. what it measures: how few parts it has.
the word: fácil. what it measures: how little effort it costs.
A dish with three ingredients is sencillo. A dish you can cook half asleep is fácil. They often overlap and they are not the same claim — climbing a staircase is sencillo and not remotely fácil.
So hacemos cosas sencillas, pero las hacemos bien is not modest about the effort. It says the things have few parts, and then insists the execution is good. Hear it as easy and you lose the point the speaker is making.

The word, taken apart.
Latin singulus, one at a time, single — through a late form singellus. English took the same root for single, singular and singly.
That is why un billete sencillo means a single: the word's oldest sense is one, not easy. Something sencillo is made of one thing, or nearly.
The -illo on the end is not doing what it usually does. Elsewhere it makes things small — but here it was already fused into the Latin, and there is no word senci underneath for it to shrink.

Lesson 6 of 8.
perderse — to get lost.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say perder. Say equivocarse. You have had both, and the second one already told you what happens when you put -se on the first.

You'll want to know: perderse.
perderse — to get lost.
Todos habían hecho cursos antes y yo me perdía.
Note what is lost there. Not the way — the thread. In a class, in a film, in a conversation, me pierdo is I lose track, and it is the most useful sense the verb has for you.
Me he perdido — I've got lost, or I've lost the thread. No te pierdas — don't get lost, and also don't miss it.

Grammar Lens: the family you were told to notice.
Equivocarse named this verb explicitly, in a group worth recalling whole:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
plain: perder — to lose a thing. reflexive: perderse — to get lost.
plain: olvidar — to forget a thing. reflexive: olvidarse — it slips your mind.
The reflexive marks something that befalls you rather than something you do to an object. Perder el paraguas is an act with a victim. Perderse has no object at all: the losing happens to you.
That is why me perdía is not an admission of carelessness. It reports a state she was in, which is exactly why it is the honest thing to say about a class that had moved on without her.

The word, taken apart.
Latin perdere: per- plus dare, to give — to give away thoroughly, to hand something over for good.
That per- is the thorough one, the same intensifier inside perfect (thoroughly made) and perturb. So losing, in Latin, is a kind of extreme giving: the thing goes and does not come back.
English perdition is the same word, kept for the most complete loss there is.

Lesson 8 of 8.
(el curso de cocina) — follow it, then say your own.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say avanzado and perderse. The first explains the second in everything below.

Reading: two friends, one question.
— ¿Qué tal el curso de cocina? ¿Sigues yendo? — Sí, pero he cambiado de grupo. El de los lunes era demasiado avanzado para mí; todos habían hecho cursos antes y yo me perdía. — ¿Y ahora? — Ahora voy los miércoles con los principiantes y disfruto mucho más. Hacemos cosas sencillas, pero las hacemos bien.
El de los lunes. The noun grupo is dropped and el carries it — the same move the printer notice made with la de la tercera planta.

Grammar Lens: she never says the class was bad.
Every word of the complaint is about fit, not quality.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
what she says: demasiado avanzado para mí. what she does not say: that the class was bad.
what she says: yo me perdía. what she does not say: that the teacher was bad.
Para mí puts the mismatch between the class and her; me perdía reports a state rather than blaming anyone; and sencillas, pero las hacemos bien refuses the obvious conclusion.
Now read it the way somebody else would report it:
El nivel era demasiado alto para ella.
Not a word of that is in what she said, and it is what she meant. She talks about the class; a summary talks about its nivel. Move between the two, because the person asking what happened will use the second.
Spanish gives you a way to leave something that is not working without criticising it, and an exam will ask you to hear the difference between it did not suit me and it was no good.

Guided Practice.
[pause 3 seconds]
Say why she changed groups, using para mí the way she does.
[pause 3 seconds]
Now say it about something of your own — a class, a book, a game. Name what was too advanced, say you lost the thread, and say what you do now instead. Keep the blame out of it.

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Why did she change? (Era demasiado avanzado para ella.) What happened to her in class? (Se perdía.) Who is she with now? (Los principiantes.) Are the new dishes worse? (No — sencillas, pero bien hechas.)
