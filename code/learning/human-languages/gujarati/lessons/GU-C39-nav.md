# નવ (nav) — nine, and the number that came through whole

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GU-LEX-AATH, GU-PRON-TE-01] -->

- [YOU RECALL: say *eight*, and name its two signs — **R1**, one lesson back]
- [YOU RECALL: say **તે**, and say who it is for — **R3**, twenty lessons back]

**તે** points at somebody who is not in the room. In a moment you will be able
to point at the ninth of anything, which is a different kind of pointing and
needs a number first.

## You'll want to know: નવ
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[GU-LEX-NAV]; assesses=[GU-SCRIPT-NA-01, GU-SCRIPT-VA-01, GU-LEX-AATH] -->

> **નવ** — *nav* — **nine**

Two consonants, no attached sign at all: **ન** carrying its inherent *a*, then
**વ**. Both have been in your hand since the first two chapters.

**એક, બે, ત્રણ, ચાર, પાંચ, છ, સાત, આઠ, નવ.**

## The word, taken apart — the one that lost nothing
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GU-LEX-NAV, GU-LEX-SAAT, GU-LEX-AATH, GU-LEX-CHHA-SIX] -->

Wiktionary hands **નવ** down from Sauraseni Prakrit *nava*, and that from
Sanskrit *náva*.

Read that line twice. Nothing happened. Sanskrit *n*, Gujarati **ન**; Sanskrit
*v*, Gujarati **વ**; only the last vowel fell away, which is what happens to
every Gujarati word.

Now look back over the chapter:

| number | what it lost |
|---|---|
| **છ** | a whole second syllable — *ṣáṣ* to one letter |
| **સાત** | the *p* of *saptá* |
| **આઠ** | the *ṣ* of *aṣṭá* |
| **નવ** | nothing |

Four numbers, and only one of them arrives with every consonant still standing.
