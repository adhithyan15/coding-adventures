Lesson 1 of 4.
خدا (khudā) — the first half of an Urdu farewell.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]

You'll want to know first — one word.
خدا — khudā — God.
Read from right to left: خ kh + د d + ا long ā. The short u is learned with the word and is normally unwritten. Let kh come from farther back than English h, but keep the sound relaxed.

The word, taken apart — Persian history inside Urdu.
Urdu borrowed khudā from Persian khodā, whose older Middle Persian ancestor xwadāy meant “lord.” Learn the Urdu sound and script first; the Persian connection then explains why both tracks will share the phrase's first historical layer.

Lesson 2 of 4.
حافظ (hāfiz) — “guardian” or “protector”.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]

You'll want to know first — one word.
حافظ — hāfiz — guardian, protector.
Read ح ا ف ظ from right to left. ا carries long ā. Final ظ is pronounced z in this Urdu word. The short i is heard in hāfiz but is normally not written as a separate letter.

The word, taken apart — the Arabic root.
Arabic ḥāfiẓ is an active participle from ḥ-f-ẓ, “guard, preserve.” Urdu receives hāfiz through its Persian-Arabic literary layer. Keep “guardian” as the useful meaning; the three-consonant root is a memory hook.

Lesson 3 of 4.
خدا حافظ (khudā hāfiz) — goodbye.

Before we start: this one needs your eyes, so it is not fully a driving lesson. You will want your eyes, for letter shapes on the page. You can listen to everything else now — leave the section called Script Bridge — shared history, local spacing until you have stopped, and I will say so again when we reach it.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Retrieve khudā and hāfiz separately: “God” + “guardian.” Now use the two known pieces as one parting move.

The two words — put the known pieces together.
خدا حافظ — khudā hāfiz — goodbye.
Urdu keeps a visible space between the two words. Read خدا first, then حافظ, and preserve that space in the complete spelling خدا حافظ (khudā hāfiz). Say the whole expression smoothly.

Grammar Lens — a whole social move.
The protective sense is “God [be] guardian,” but everyday speakers use the formula simply as goodbye. No new verb or agreement ending is needed. It is a reliable polite close for the short interaction learned so far.

Script Bridge — shared history, local spacing.
Urdu خدا حافظ (khudā hāfiz) and Persian خداحافظ share the same historical pieces. Urdu conventionally keeps the visible space; Persian normally joins them. Mixed practice may compare the two only now that each local phrase is independently readable.

The exchange — the complete interaction.
A: سلام! آپ کیسے ہیں؟ Salām! Āp kaise haiṅ? B: میں ٹھیک ہوں، شکریہ۔ Maiṅ ṭhīk hūṅ, shukriyā. A: خدا حافظ (khudā hāfiz)۔ Khudā hāfiz. B: خدا حافظ (khudā hāfiz)۔ Khudā hāfiz.
Every line is already learned. Salām opens; khudā hāfiz closes. The same farewell works for both speakers, so this practice adds no gender choice, pronoun, or copula form.
