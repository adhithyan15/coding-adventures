Lesson 1 of 6.
rě — to stir up trouble; to provoke.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Mandarin for to arise from something, then the Mandarin for not in the least.

You'll want to know: rě.
rě — "to stir up trouble; to provoke".
nǐ búyào rě tā bùrán tā huì shēngqì — Don't provoke him, or he'll get angry.
Its characters come later; for now it is a word for the ear.

The word, taken apart.
rě is inherited from Middle Chinese nyaeX (Guangyun, in Baxter's transcription), glossed 'a troubled mind'; the dictionary also lists nyak. The sense 'to provoke' is not given there.

Lesson 2 of 6.
怪 (guài) — to put the blame on someone.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Mandarin for not in the least, then the Mandarin for to stir up trouble.

You'll want to know: 怪.
怪 — guài — "to put the blame on someone".
这不能怪你原因不在你 zhè bù néng guài nǐ yuányīn bú zài nǐ — You can't be blamed for this: the cause doesn't lie with you.
The new character, in pieces: 怪 (guài) — eight strokes in three pieces: the upright heart on the left, three strokes, as in 快; on the right 又, two strokes; and under it the earth piece, three strokes. The next lesson puts it in your hand.

The word, taken apart.
怪 (guài) is inherited from Middle Chinese kweajH (Guangyun, in Baxter's transcription), glossed 'strange'. The sense 'to blame' is not given there; it is usually explained as finding fault, but how it came is not settled.

Script — the upright heart, then 又 over the earth piece.
怪 (guài).
Three pieces: the upright heart on the left, then 又, then the earth piece under it. Count the pieces, not the strokes.
The upright heart, three strokes, as in 快: a short dot on the left, a short dot on the right, then a long vertical down between them.
又, two strokes, as you wrote it: a bar that turns into a long stroke falling to the left, then a long stroke falling to the right across it.
The earth piece, three strokes, as on the left of 地, but at the foot: a short bar, a vertical down through it, then a long bar along the foot.
3 + 2 + 3 = eight.

[once you have stopped driving — cover: write it again from memory, and count the pieces, not the strokes]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
How many pieces does 怪 (guài) have? (Three: the upright heart, 又 and the earth piece.) Which piece is written last? (The earth piece, at the foot.)

Lesson 4 of 6.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Mandarin for to stir up trouble, then the Mandarin for to put the blame on.

You'll want to know: 怪事.
怪事 — guàishì — "a strange business; an odd thing".
这真是怪事 zhè zhēn shì guàishì — That really is a strange business.

The word, taken apart.
怪事 (guàishì) is built from 怪 (guài) 'strange' and 事 'a matter', both inherited from Middle Chinese, kweajH and dzriH (Guangyun, in Baxter's transcription), which glosses the first 'strange'.

Lesson 5 of 6.
chīkuī — to come off worse; to lose out.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Mandarin for to put the blame on, then the Mandarin for a strange business.

You'll want to know: chīkuī.
chīkuī — "to come off worse; to lose out".
rúguǒ nǐ bù tīng wǒ de jiù huì chīkuī — If you don't listen to me, you'll come off worse.
Its characters come later; for now it is a word for the ear.

The word, taken apart.
chīkuī is built from 吃 'to eat' and kuī 'a loss'. For the first the dictionary has khek, glossed 'to eat', under another spelling; the second is inherited from Middle Chinese khjwe (Guangyun, in Baxter's transcription), glossed 'to be lacking': to swallow a loss.

Lesson 6 of 6.
chuǎnghuò — to get into trouble; to cause a mess.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Mandarin for a strange business, then the Mandarin for to come off worse.

You'll want to know: chuǎnghuò.
chuǎnghuò — "to get into trouble; to cause a mess".
yàoshi nǐ zài chuǎnghuò wǒ jiù gàosu nǐ māma — If you get into trouble again, I'll tell your mum.
Its characters come later; for now it is a word for the ear.

The word, taken apart.
chuǎnghuò is built from chuǎng 'to rush' and huò 'disaster'. The second is inherited from Middle Chinese hwaX (Guangyun, in Baxter's transcription), glossed 'harm'. The dictionary lists the first as trhimH, a horse dashing out of a gate, but chuǎng is not the regular outcome of that form, so its history is not settled.
