# six, sept, huit, neuf, dix — and the calendar's secret

## Warm-up

[PAUSE 2s] The rest of the way to ten. These four hide the same fact the English
and Spanish numbers do: **septembre–décembre are just Latin for 7, 8, 9, 10.**
And *huit* shows how violently French can wear a Latin word down.

## Sounds you'll need

- **six** and **dix** end in a *-s* sound when counting (*sees*, *dees*) but go
  **silent** before a consonant (*six livres* = *see livres*) — a liaison quirk.
- **neuf** = *nuhf*; its *f* turns to a *v* sound before a vowel (*neuf ans* =
  *neu-v-ans*).

## The numbers, taken apart

| French | ← Latin | Spanish twin | English cousins |
|---|---|---|---|
| **six** (6) | *sex* | *seis* | **sextet, sextant, semester** |
| **sept** (7) | *septem* | *siete* | **septembre** (the *7th* month!), septet |
| **huit** (8) | *octō* | *ocho* | **octobre** (8th), **octave, octopus** |
| **neuf** (9) | *novem* | *nueve* | **novembre** (9th), novena |
| **dix** (10) | *decem* | *diez* | **décembre** (10th), **decimal, dime** (← French *disme*) |

Look at **huit**: Latin *octō* lost its *c*, softened, and grew an *h-* prop —
*octō → oit → huit*. The number is unrecognizable, but *octobre* keeps the old
*oct-* intact, so the 8 is still visible in the **month**. And *dix* gave English
**dime** (a tenth of a dollar), through Old French *disme* ← *decima* "a tenth."

## Grammar Lens: why the months are "off by two"

*Septembre* means "7th," yet it's the **9th** month. Why? The old **Roman year
began in March**, so *September* really was the seventh. Julius and Augustus later
inserted **juillet** and **août** (named for themselves), shoving the counting
months out of place — but the Latin numbers stuck. Every *décembre*, you're saying
Roman "**ten**." (Identical to the Spanish story — *diciembre* ← *decem* too.)
