5 lessons. You can do the first 1 of them in the car; after that you will want to have stopped.


Lesson 1 of 5.
సోమవారం to ఆదివారం — Telugu's week, with a twist on Sunday.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Like Kannada, Telugu's week runs on Sanskrit deity-names — but its Sunday breaks the "planet" pattern entirely, in a way even Kannada's doesn't.

The word, taken apart - The pattern: [deity] + వారం, "day".
[a table of 7 rows, read aloud]
Telugu: సోమవారం (Somavāram). deity/planet: Moon (Soma). day: Monday.
Telugu: మంగళవారం (Maṅgaḷavāram). deity/planet: Mars (Maṅgala). day: Tuesday.
Telugu: బుధవారం (Budhavāram). deity/planet: Mercury (Budha). day: Wednesday.
Telugu: గురువారం (Guruvāram). deity/planet: Jupiter (Guru/Bṛhaspati). day: Thursday.
Telugu: శుక్రవారం (Śukravāram). deity/planet: Venus (Śukra). day: Friday.
Telugu: శనివారం (Śanivāram). deity/planet: Saturn (Śani). day: Saturday.
Telugu: ఆదివారం (Ādivāram). deity/planet: — not a planet at all. day: Sunday.

Why it's said this way: Be honest: Sunday isn't a planet-name here.
Five days match the familiar planet-deity pattern — but ఆదివారం (Ādivāram) doesn't name the Sun at all. ఆది (ādi) is Sanskrit for "the beginning, the first." So Telugu's Sunday literally means "the first day" — a positional name, like Arabic's numbered weekdays, sitting right next to six planet-names that don't work that way. Even within one single week, a language can mix completely different naming strategies.

Two of the day names from this chapter carry a third one.

Script you'll notice: శ.
శ — śa.
It is a consonant, and in this script a consonant is never bare: it comes with an a already in it. So it is not ś, it is śa.
శుక్రవారం śukravāram — Friday.
శనివారం śanivāram — Saturday.
Both open on today's character. The two ends of the week you have been reciting begin with a letter you could not yet read.
Now you have the whole set:
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
letter: స. romanized: sa. where the tongue goes: tip behind the teeth.
letter: ష. romanized: ṣa. where the tongue goes: curled back along the roof.
letter: శ. romanized: śa. where the tongue goes: flat against the front of the roof.
Telugu, Kannada and Hindi keep all three apart on the page. Tamil's own alphabet has none of them and adds borrowed shapes for Sanskrit words — same family, opposite decision.

Writing: శ — copy what you see.
Put your pen on శ (śa) and follow its line. Copy the shape you can see — slowly, and larger than it is printed. Then write స and ష beside it.

Copying what is in front of you needs no such source, and it is how the shape gets into your hand in the meantime.

Warm-up.

Script: the five, and the one question behind them.
అ, ఆ, ఏ, ఐ, ఒ.
Five letters, and every one of them is on the page for the same reason: a vowel turned up at the front of a word with no consonant to sit inside.
[a table of 5 rows, read aloud]
letter: అ. sound: a. the word it opens: అవును — yes.
letter: ఆ. sound: ā. the word it opens: ఆరు — six.
letter: ఏ. sound: ē. the word it opens: ఏమిటి — what?
letter: ఐ. sound: ai. the word it opens: ఐదు — five.
letter: ఒ. sound: o. the word it opens: ఒకటి — one.
Look down the right-hand column. Three of the five open a number you can already count with, and the other two open two of the commonest words in the book. None of these is an exotic letter kept for later; they were on your pages from chapter one, and only now do you have the shapes for them.
The question to ask of any vowel in Telugu is the same each time: is a consonant carrying it? If yes, a sign. If no, a letter.

Lesson 5 of 5.
The three goodbyes again, now that you can name the day — second pass over the farewells — the goodbye that promises to come back, now that you can name the day.

Warm-up.
[pause 3 seconds]
From memory: the ordinary Telugu goodbye. Then say what it means word for word, which is not "goodbye" at all.

You'll want to know: none of the three says "goodbye".
You have three ways to leave, and every one of them promises a return:
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
what you say: వెళ్ళి వస్తాను veḷḷi vastānu. what it says: having gone, I come.
what you say: రేపు కలుద్దాం rēpu kaluddām. what it says: tomorrow, let us meet.
what you say: మళ్ళీ కలుద్దాం maḷḷī kaluddām. what it says: again, let us meet.
Telugu has no comfortable word for a plain, final parting. The polite thing is to name the return, and the three above are three sizes of it — the next hour, the next day, some day.

Grammar Lens: the "having …ed" link.
వెళ్ళి is not a finished verb — it is having gone, waiting for a second verb to complete it. Telugu chains actions this way: every verb but the last takes the having …ed shape, and the last one carries the person and the time.
వెళ్ళి వస్తాను = having gone + I come. The ‑తాను at the end is the same "I" ending you met on మాట్లాడతాను.
