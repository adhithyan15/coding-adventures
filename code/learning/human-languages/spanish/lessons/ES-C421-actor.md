# el actor — the one who does, which is all the word has ever meant

## Warm-up

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-C421-ARTS-01]; assesses=[] -->

**el actor** — "the actor." Latin **actor**, from the verb **agere**, "to
drive, to do, to set in motion."

Latin built agent nouns with **-tor**: take a verb, add *-tor*, and you get
*the one who does it*. The pattern is still visible in dozens of English words
that were borrowed whole:

| Latin verb | meaning | the one who does it |
|---|---|---|
| agere | to do | **actor** |
| docere | to teach | doctor |
| dirigere | to direct | director |
| inspicere | to look into | inspector |

So *actor* is not a theatre word at all. It is **doer**, and Latin used it for
anyone who acted in any sense — an agent, a driver of cattle, a man pleading a
case in court. The stage borrowed a perfectly ordinary word and kept it.

*Agere* is still visible all over English, and it never stopped meaning "do":
an **agent** is someone doing something on your behalf, an **agenda** is
literally *things that must be done*, and an **action** is a doing.

## Grammar Lens: the two workplaces
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C421-ARTS-01, ES-LEX-C420-ARTS-01, ES-LEX-PELICULA] -->

The same person, two places you already have words for:

| where | what is watched | live? |
|---|---|---|
| el teatro | the actor | yes |
| el cine | la película | no |

*El actor* is masculine and takes *el*. That is the easy half. The other half —
what to call a woman who does the same work — is the next lesson, and Spanish
answers it with an ending that almost nothing else in the language uses.
