Italian, chapter 347: Valutare e giudicare.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
valutare — to evaluate, to weigh up.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Italian for to specify, then the Italian for the clarification.

You'll want to know: valutare.
valutare — "to evaluate, to weigh up".
Dobbiamo valutare bene i costi prima di comprare la casa. We have to weigh up the costs carefully before buying the house.
Also to value in money: valutare un quadro.

The word, taken apart.
valutare is built on valuta "value", from valere "to be worth", which is inherited from Latin valēre "to be strong, to be worth" (Lewis & Short).

What you've built.

You'll want to know: il giudizio.
il giudizio — "the judgement; good sense".
Non dare un giudizio prima di conoscerlo. Do not pass judgement before you know him.
Mettere giudizio — to grow sensible. A mio giudizio — in my judgement.

The word, taken apart.
giudizio is inherited from Latin iūdicium "a trial, a judgement, an opinion" (Lewis & Short), built on iūdex "a judge".

Lesson 4 of 5.
oggettivo — objective.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Italian for the judgement, then the Italian for to judge.

You'll want to know: oggettivo.
oggettivo — "objective".
Cerco di essere oggettivo, anche se è mio figlio. I try to be objective, even though he is my son.
Oggettivamente — objectively.

The word, taken apart.
oggettivo is a learned borrowing from Medieval Latin obiectīvus, from obiectum "a thing set before (the mind)", from obicere "to throw before" (Lewis & Short), built on iacere "to throw".

Lesson 5 of 5.
soggettivo — subjective.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Italian for to judge, then the Italian for objective.

You'll want to know: soggettivo.
soggettivo — "subjective".
Il gusto è soggettivo: a me piace, a te no. Taste is subjective: I like it, you do not.
È una cosa soggettiva — it is a matter of taste.

The word, taken apart.
soggettivo is a learned borrowing from Late Latin subiectīvus, from subiectum "what is placed under", from subicere "to throw under" (Lewis & Short), built on iacere "to throw".
