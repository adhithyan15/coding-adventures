# みた (mita) — the plain past, what friends say

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[JA-LEX-C236-FILM236-02, JA-LEX-C236-FILM236-01, JA-LEX-C133-KINOU] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Before the new form: say "I saw a film yesterday", politely. (*kinō eiga o mimashita*.) The polite past is what you say to a teacher or in a shop. To a friend, the past is shorter.

## You'll want to know: the plain past
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[JA-GRAMMAR-C250-PAST-01]; assesses=[JA-LEX-C25-FAMILY25-01, JA-LEX-ANCHOR-TABERU, JA-LEX-C138-NEMASU, JA-LEX-C138-OKIMASU] -->

A friend asks what you did yesterday, and you answer as friends do:

> **えいがを みた。**
>
> *eiga o mita.* — "I saw a film."

> **きのう えいがを みた。**
>
> *kinō eiga o mita.* — "I saw a film yesterday."

**みた** (*mita*) is **みました** (*mimashita*) without the politeness: the plain past, the form friends and family use with each other.

For **みる** (*miru*), "to see", drop the last **る** and add **た**. **たべる** (*taberu*), "to eat", goes the same way: **たべた** (*tabeta*), "ate".

> **ばんごはんを たべた。**
>
> *bangohan o tabeta.* — "I ate dinner."

> **うちで ばんごはんを たべた。**
>
> *uchi de bangohan o tabeta.* — "I ate dinner at home."

The polite form shows the way too: take **ます** off **ねます** (*nemasu*) and add **た**: **ねた** (*neta*), "went to bed". From **おきます** (*okimasu*) comes **おきた** (*okita*), "got up".

> **きのう はやく ねた。**
>
> *kinō hayaku neta.* — "I went to bed early yesterday."

## You'll want to know: した and きた
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[JA-GRAMMAR-C250-PAST-02]; assesses=[JA-LEX-C40-HOME40-03, JA-LEX-C37-HOME37-01] -->

Two more verbs: **した** (*shita*), "did". It is the past of **する** (*suru*), "to do", the verb inside **します** (*shimasu*). And **くる** (*kuru*), "to come", the verb inside **きます** (*kimasu*), has **きた** (*kita*), "came".

> **しゅくだいを した。**
>
> *shukudai o shita.* — "I did my homework."

> **ともだちが きた。**
>
> *tomodachi ga kita.* — "A friend came."

> **きのう ともだちが きた。**
>
> *kinō tomodachi ga kita.* — "A friend came yesterday."

The plain past has a second job, and later lessons give it to you: it goes inside a polite sentence, in front of the word that makes the sentence polite. So **みた** is worth owning even if you speak politely to everyone.
