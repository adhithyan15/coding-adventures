# duro — hard, and hard going

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-DURANTE, ES-LEX-C327-STATE-18] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say **durante** and **fuerte**. One of them is about how long, the
other about how much force.

## You'll want to know: duro
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-C448-MUDA-03]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C448-MUDA-02] -->

**duro** — hard.

*El pan está duro* — the bread has gone hard. *Un colchón duro* — a firm
mattress. And then, away from things you can press: *un día muy duro* — a hard
day. *El trabajo es duro* — the work is heavy going. *No seas tan duro con
él* — don't be so hard on him.

A mudanza is the standard example of *un día duro*, and no Spanish speaker
would call it *un día fuerte*.

## Grammar Lens: duro is not fuerte
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C448-MUDA-03, ES-LEX-C327-STATE-18] -->

The two get confused because English "strong" and "hard" overlap. Spanish
keeps them apart by asking a different question about each:

| word | the question it answers |
|---|---|
| **fuerte** | how much force does it have? |
| **duro** | how much does it resist? |

*Una persona fuerte* can lift the boxes. *Una persona dura* will not soften
when you ask for a favour. A rock is *duro*; it has no force at all. Wind is
*fuerte*; it is not hard in the least.

So when a day is *duro*, the picture is not that the day was powerful. It is
that the day would not give.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C448-MUDA-03, ES-LEX-DURANTE] -->

Latin **durus**, hard — and from it the verb **durare**, to last.

That derivation repays a moment. What lasts is what resists wearing away, so
"hard" and "long-lasting" are one idea seen twice. Spanish kept both halves:
*duro* is the resisting, and **durar** — with **durante** inside it — is the
lasting. *La mudanza duró todo el día*: the move held out all day.

English took both halves too, from the same place: **durable** and
**duration** on one side, **endure** on the other.
