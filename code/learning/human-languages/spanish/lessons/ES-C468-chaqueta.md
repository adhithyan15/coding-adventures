# la chaqueta — the jacket

in front of you without stopping to teach it.

## You'll want to know: la chaqueta
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-C468-ABRIGO-01]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C430-VIDA-03] -->

**la chaqueta** — the jacket.

> Hace frío. Llévate una **chaqueta**.

*Una chaqueta de piel* — a leather jacket, the phrase *la piel* already gave
you. *La chaqueta del traje* — the suit jacket.

Among knitwear the line is clean: a *jersey* goes over your head, a *chaqueta
de punto* opens down the front — which is what a cardigan is. Beyond knitwear
a *chaqueta* is any short tailored jacket.

| where | the everyday word |
|---|---|
| Spain | **la chaqueta** |
| much of Latin America | **el saco**, **la campera**, **la chamarra** |

Take the regional column seriously here. In Mexico and much of Central
America *chaqueta* carries a vulgar second sense, and the neutral word is
*saco* or *chamarra*.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C468-ABRIGO-01] -->

You were shown this one in passing when you learned *el jersey*, in a table of
garments named after somewhere. It sat there as the odd row, because it is not
named after a place at all.

French **jaquette** is a small **jaque**, a short tunic — and that much is
firm. Where *jaque* came from is not. One account takes it to **Jacques**, the
stock name for a French peasant, the way English once said *Jack* for any
ordinary man. Another takes it through Spanish *jaco* to an Arabic word for a
mail jerkin. Both are live; neither is proved.

You already know Spanish will name a garment after its wearer — *los
vaqueros* are the cowherd's trousers. If the Jacques account is right, *la
chaqueta* is the same habit, which is why it sat in the wrong column of the
*jersey* chapter's table:

| garment | named after |
|---|---|
| el jersey | an island |
| la chaqueta | a man's name, probably |

English took both ends of the pair: **jack**, a medieval padded tunic,
straight from *jaque*; and **jacket**, from the little one, *jaquette*.
