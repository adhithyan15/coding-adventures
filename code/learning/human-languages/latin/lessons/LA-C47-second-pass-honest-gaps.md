## You'll want to know: three shelves, not one
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[LA-LEX-QUOMODO-01, LA-LEX-NIHIL-EST-01, LA-PRAGMATICS-NIHIL-EST-03, LA-PRAGMATICS-BENE-03, LA-LEX-BENE-01, LA-PRAGMATICS-IGNOSCE-MIHI-02] -->

| | |
|---|---|
| **quōmodo** | fully classical, in Cicero and Seneca — "by what manner" |
| **bene** | attested standing alone in Plautus, though in a **drinking toast** |
| **nihil est** | real words, real texts — but **never** attested answering thanks |

**ignōsce mihi** belongs on the first shelf: a plain imperative of *ignōscere*,
"to overlook, pardon." Beside it Latin kept an impersonal form with no "you" in
it at all — **paenitet mē**, "it grieves me" — which is where English gets
*penitent* and *repent*.

The middle shelf is the interesting one. **bene** on its own is genuinely
attested; what is not attested is **bene** answering *how are you*, because that
exchange is itself a modern habit. A phrase can be real and its **job** modern,
and those are two different claims.

## Why it's said this way: tū and vōs carry no politeness
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[LA-LEX-TU-VOS-01, LA-LEX-TU-VOS-02, LA-PRAGMATICS-TU-VOS-03] -->

**tū** is one person, **vōs** is more than one, and in Classical Latin that is
the whole of it. There is **no** formal-against-familiar dimension — addressing
one powerful person as *vōs* had not started yet.

It started in Late Latin, and French *vous* is that habit's direct descendant.
German solved the same problem separately and reached for a different pronoun
altogether. So the polite plural is real, and it is younger than the Latin you
are reading.
