Lesson 1 of 6.
hòuchēshì — station waiting room.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Mandarin for to change trains, then the Mandarin for a railway carriage.

You'll want to know: hòuchēshì.
hòuchēshì — "station waiting room".
nǐ zuò zài hòuchēshì děng wǒ — Sit in the waiting room and wait for me.
Its characters come later; for now it is a word for the ear.

The word, taken apart.
hòuchēshì is built from 候车 'to wait for a train' and shì 'a room', all inherited from Middle Chinese, huwH, tsyhae and syit (Guangyun, in Baxter's transcription), which glosses the last 'a room'.

Lesson 2 of 6.
旅客 (lǚkè) — traveller; passenger on a long journey.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Mandarin for a railway carriage, then the Mandarin for a station waiting room.

You'll want to know: 旅客.
旅客 — lǚkè — "traveller; passenger on a long journey".
旅客都上车了 lǚkè dōu shàngchē le — The passengers have all got on.
The new character, in pieces: 旅 (lǚ) — ten strokes in two pieces: on the left a dot, a bar, a stroke that runs right and turns down with a hook, and a long stroke falling to the left; on the right a short stroke falling to the left and a bar, then a short stroke falling to the left, a vertical that turns up at the foot, a short stroke falling to the left and a long stroke falling to the right. The next lesson puts it in your hand.

The word, taken apart.
旅客 (lǚkè) is built from 旅 (lǚ) and 客 'a guest', both inherited from Middle Chinese, ljoX and khaek (Guangyun, in Baxter's transcription). The dictionary glosses the first as a body of troops, five hundred men after an older book; the sense 'to travel' is not given there, so how it came is not settled.

Script — a left piece of four strokes, then a right piece of six.
旅 (lǚ).
Two pieces, left before right. Count the pieces, not the strokes.
The left piece, four strokes. A dot on top. A bar under it. A stroke that runs right, turns down and hooks to the left at the foot. Then a long stroke falling to the left from under the bar.
The right piece, six strokes. A short stroke falling to the left and a bar to its right, like a small roof. Under them a short stroke falling to the left, a vertical that turns up to the right at the foot, another short stroke falling to the left, and last a long stroke falling to the right.
4 + 6 = ten.

[pause 3 seconds]

Lesson 4 of 6.
正点 (zhèngdiǎn) — on schedule (of a train or plane).

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Mandarin for a station waiting room, then the Mandarin for a passenger on a long journey.

You'll want to know: 正点.
正点 — zhèngdiǎn — "on schedule (of a train or plane)".
我们的火车正点到了 wǒmen de huǒchē zhèngdiǎn dào le — Our train got in on time.

The word, taken apart.
正点 (zhèngdiǎn) is built from 正 'exactly' and 点 'o'clock', both inherited from Middle Chinese, tsyengH and temX (Guangyun, in Baxter's transcription), which glosses the first 'right, proper' and the second as a dot made with the brush: on the hour the timetable gives.

Lesson 5 of 6.
lièchēyuán — train attendant.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Mandarin for a passenger on a long journey, then the Mandarin for on schedule.

You'll want to know: lièchēyuán.
lièchēyuán — "train attendant".
zhǎo bu dào zuòwèi dehuà kěyǐ wèn lièchēyuán — If you can't find your seat, ask the train attendant.
Its characters come later; for now it is a word for the ear.

The word, taken apart.
lièchēyuán is built from liè 'a row', 车 'vehicle' and yuán 'a member of staff', all inherited from Middle Chinese, ljet, tsyhae and hjwen (Guangyun, in Baxter's transcription). The dictionary glosses the first 'a row, an order' and the third as the number of things; a train is a row of carriages, and the sense 'staff' for the third is not given there.

[pause 2 seconds]

You'll want to know: 站长.
站长 — zhànzhǎng — "stationmaster; head of a station".
旅客去找站长 lǚkè qù zhǎo zhànzhǎng — The passenger goes to find the stationmaster.

The word, taken apart.
站长 (zhànzhǎng) is built from 站 'a station' and 长 'the head', both inherited from Middle Chinese, treamH and trjangX (Guangyun, in Baxter's transcription). The dictionary glosses the first as an everyday word for standing alone and the second 'big'; a station is usually explained as a place where vehicles stand.
