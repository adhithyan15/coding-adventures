Latin, chapter 434: Stable, Manger, Hay, To neigh, Muleteer.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
stabulum, stabulī — a stable, a stall; a lodging.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a pack saddle, then the Latin for to load.

You'll want to know: stabulum, stabulī.
stabulum, stabulī — "a stable, a stall; a lodging".
Vesperī equī in stabulō quiēscunt. In the evening the horses rest in the stable.
Second declension, neuter: stabulum, stabulī n. Also a cheap roadside lodging where people and beasts stayed. English stable comes from it.

The word, taken apart.
stabulum is built on stō 'to stand' with the suffix -bulum 'place for' (EtymDB; Lewis & Short): a standing place. English stable comes from it through French.

praesēpe, praesēpis — "a manger, a crib; a stall".
Servus cibum in praesēpe pōnit. The slave puts food in the manger.
Third declension, neuter: praesēpe, praesēpis n. Also spelled praesaepe.

The word, taken apart.
praesēpe is built on prae- 'in front' and saepiō 'to fence in' (Lewis & Short, under praesaepe): a fenced place in front of the animal. EtymDB lists it without a parent.

Lesson 3 of 5.
faenum, faenī — hay.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a stable, then the Latin for a manger.

You'll want to know: faenum, faenī.
faenum, faenī — "hay".
Aestāte agricolae faenum in prātīs colligunt. In summer the farmers gather hay in the meadows.
Second declension, neuter: faenum, faenī n., also spelled fēnum. English fennel comes from faeniculum 'little hay'.

The word, taken apart.
faenum is inherited; EtymDB traces it to PIE dʰeh₁(y)-no-, and Lewis & Short tie it to the root of fēmina and fēlīx. English fennel comes from its diminutive faeniculum (Lewis & Short).

Lesson 4 of 5.
hinniō, hinnīre — to neigh, to whinny.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a manger, then the Latin for hay.

You'll want to know: hinniō, hinnīre.
hinniō, hinnīre — "to neigh, to whinny".
In stabulō equī hinniunt, quod cibum exspectant. In the stable the horses neigh, because they are waiting for food.
Fourth conjugation: hinniō, hinnīre; the perfect is rare. A sound word, like English whinny.

The word, taken apart.
hinniō is usually taken as an imitation of the sound a horse makes, like English whinny; Lewis & Short give no origin, and EtymDB lists it without a parent.

Warm-up.

Before the new one: say the Latin for hay, then the Latin for to neigh.

You'll want to know: mūliō, mūliōnis.
mūliō, mūliōnis — "a muleteer, a mule driver".
Mūliō mūlōs ad flūmen dūcit et aquam dat. The muleteer leads the mules to the river and gives them water.
Third declension, masculine: mūliō, mūliōnis m. Muleteers hired out mules and carts to travellers.

The word, taken apart.
mūliō is built on mūlus 'mule' (EtymDB; Lewis & Short) with the suffix -iō for a person who works with something.
