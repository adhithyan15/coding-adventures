# celebrar — to make a crowd of it

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C440-FIESTA-02, ES-LEX-C349-CLOCK-18, ES-LEX-MUSICA] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say **el cumpleaños**, **la fiesta** and **la música**. You have the
day, the party and the sound. Name the verb that puts them together.

## You'll want to know: celebrar
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-C440-FIESTA-03]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C440-FIESTA-02] -->

**celebrar** — to celebrate.

*Celebramos su cumpleaños en casa* — we are celebrating her birthday at home.
*Celebrarse* is the form a notice uses: *la reunión se celebra el martes* — the
meeting is held on Tuesday.

That second use is worth having early. On an official notice, **celebrarse**
does not mean anybody is enjoying themselves; it means the event **takes place**.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C440-FIESTA-03] -->

Latin **celebrāre**, from **celeber** — *crowded, much-visited, thronged*.

A *celeber locus* was a place people went to in numbers. To **celebrate**
something was therefore to **make a crowd of it**, and only later to enjoy it.
The idea underneath is attendance, not happiness.

English took the same root twice: **celebrate**, and **celebrity** — a person
who has a crowd.

## Why it's said this way
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C440-FIESTA-03] -->

That original meaning is exactly why a town-hall notice can say *se celebra* for
an event nobody would call festive.

| what the sign says | what it means |
|---|---|
| se celebra la fiesta | the party takes place |
| se celebra la reunión | the meeting takes place |

Both are *celebrar*, because both are a gathering. English would need two verbs
here, and a reader who knows only the happy sense will misread the second one as
a joke.

When you meet **celebrarse** on a notice, read it as **is held**. The crowd is
what the word is really about, and a meeting has one.
