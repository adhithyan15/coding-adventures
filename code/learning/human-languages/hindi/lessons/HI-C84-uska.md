# uskā — one word for his, her and its

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[HI-CONCEPT-C02-MERAA-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] You have had *merā*, *merī*, *mere* since the second chapter. Say
all three. You have been able to say whose something is for exactly one person:
your own.

## You'll want to know: uskā
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[HI-LEX-C84-POSS-01]; assesses=[] -->

*uskā* — "his," "her," or "its."

Hindi does not choose between those three, and the reason is the point of the
lesson: **the possessive agrees with the thing owned, not with the owner.**

## Grammar Lens: English agrees with the owner, Hindi with the thing
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[HI-LEX-C84-POSS-01, HI-CONCEPT-C02-MERAA-01, HI-GRAMMAR-C41-ADJ-SYSTEM] -->

This is the single biggest difference between the two systems, and it runs the
opposite way from what an English speaker expects:

| | what decides the form |
|---|---|
| English *his* / *her* | the **owner**'s gender |
| Hindi *uskā* / *uskī* | the **owned thing**'s gender |

So *uskā* before a masculine noun and *uskī* before a feminine one — and
whether the owner is a man or a woman changes nothing at all. A sentence with
*uskī kitāb* tells you the book is feminine and tells you **nothing** about
whose it is.

This is not a new rule. It is the **-आ** agreement you already have, doing its
ordinary job: *uskā* ends in **-आ**, so it bends like every other **-आ** word
in the book.
