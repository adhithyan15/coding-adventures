# grātiās (tibi) agō — "thank you" (I give thanks)

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[LA-PHONO-VALE-01, LA-ETYMON-VALE-02, LA-GRAMMAR-VALE-03] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say this and you have said *grace*, *gratitude*, *gracias*, and
*grazie* all at once.

## Sounds you'll need
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[LA-PHONO-GRATIAS-01]; assesses=[] -->

*Grātiās* = **GRAH-tee-ās**; *agō* = **AH-go**. In classical Latin *c* and *t*
before *i* stay hard — **GRA-tee**, never "GRA-shee."

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[LA-ETYMON-GRATIAS-02]; assesses=[] -->

The full phrase is *grātiās tibi agō* — literally "**I give thanks to you**"
(*grātiās* "thanks," *tibi* "to you," *agō* "I do/drive"). The noun *grātia*
means "favour, grace, gratitude," from *grātus* "pleasing, thankful." Into
English: **grace, gratitude, gratis, grateful, gracious, ingratiate,
congratulate**. And straight into the Romance "thank you" you already learned —
Italian *grazie*, Spanish *gracias*, Portuguese *graças* — all just the plural
*grātiās*, "thanks," worn smooth.

## Grammar Lens
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[LA-GRAMMAR-GRATIAS-03]; assesses=[] -->

*agō* means "I do" — the *-ō* ending **is** "I," so no separate pronoun is
needed. This is Latin's great habit: the verb ending tells you *who* acts. *Agō*
itself is a root you know: **agent, act, agenda** ("things to be done"), **agile,
navigate**.
