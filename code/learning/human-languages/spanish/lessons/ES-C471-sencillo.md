# sencillo — simple

## You'll want to know: sencillo
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-C471-CURSO-05]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C471-CURSO-04] -->

**sencillo** — simple, plain, straightforward.

> Hacemos cosas **sencillas**, pero las hacemos bien.

*Una comida sencilla* — a plain meal. *Un billete sencillo* — a one-way
ticket, where the word means *single* rather than *easy*.

## Grammar Lens: sencillo is not fácil
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C471-CURSO-05, ES-LEX-DIFICIL, ES-LEX-FACIL] -->

English lets *simple* and *easy* stand in for each other. Spanish keeps them
apart, and the line runs between **parts** and **effort**.

| the word | what it measures |
|---|---|
| **sencillo** | how few parts it has |
| [fácil](./ES-C398-facil.md) | how little effort it costs |

A dish with three ingredients is *sencillo*. A dish you can cook half asleep
is *fácil*. They often overlap and they are not the same claim — climbing a
staircase is *sencillo* and not remotely *fácil*.

So *hacemos cosas sencillas, pero las hacemos bien* is not modest about the
effort. It says the things have few parts, and then insists the execution is
good. Hear it as *easy* and you lose the point the speaker is making.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C471-CURSO-05] -->

Latin **singulus**, one at a time, single — through a late form *singellus*.
English took the same root for **single**, **singular** and **singly**.

That is why *un billete sencillo* means a **single**: the word's oldest sense
is *one*, not *easy*. Something *sencillo* is made of one thing, or nearly.

The *-illo* on the end is not doing what it usually does. Elsewhere it makes
things small — but here it was already fused into the Latin, and there is no
word *senci* underneath for it to shrink.
