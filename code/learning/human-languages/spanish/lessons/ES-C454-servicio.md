## You'll want to know: el servicio
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-C454-CORTE-04]; assesses=[ES-LEX-SERVIR] -->

**el servicio** — the service.

*El servicio de grúa* — the recovery service, the tow truck. *Los servicios de
emergencia* — the emergency services. *Fuera de servicio* — out of service,
the sign on a broken lift or a stopped bus.

And the one that catches people out: **el servicio** on a door means **the
toilet**. *¿Dónde está el servicio?* is how you ask, and *los servicios* is
what the sign in a bar says.

## Grammar Lens: fuera de servicio, and its cousins
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C454-CORTE-04, ES-LEX-C449-TALLER-01] -->

*Fuera de* plus a bare noun is a fixed frame, and it turns up on notices
constantly:

| sign | what it means |
|---|---|
| *fuera de servicio* | out of order |
| *fuera de plazo* | past the deadline |
| *fuera de temporada* | out of season |

No article after *de* in any of them. That bare noun is the signal that you
are looking at a set phrase rather than a description, and trying to insert
*el* will sound wrong to a Spanish ear for the same reason "out of the order"
does to an English one.

Set beside **estropeado**, the difference is who is speaking: *fuera de
servicio* is the institution's wording, *estropeado* is what a neighbour says.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C454-CORTE-04, ES-LEX-C454-CORTE-03] -->

Latin **servitium**, from **servire**, to serve — and behind that **servus**,
a slave.

That is the uncomfortable root of a very ordinary word, and it has not
disappeared: *el siervo* is a serf, and English **servile** keeps the sting.
Spanish softened the rest of the family — *servir* is what a waiter does, *el
servicio* is what a company sells — while the original sense stayed in the
words nobody uses lightly.

English got **serve**, **servant** and **sergeant** from the same place. The
last one is *serviens*, "one serving", which rose from servant to soldier to
rank.
