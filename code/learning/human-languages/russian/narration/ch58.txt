Russian, chapter 58: Sky, Sun, Moon, Star, Cloud.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
небо (nébo) — the sky.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Russian for an island, then the Russian for a field.

You'll want to know: небо.
небо (nébo) — "the sky".

The word, taken apart.
небо (nébo) — borrowed from Church Slavonic, says Vasmer; the native East Slavic form is нёбо, now "the palate". Both go back to Proto-Slavic nebo, a cousin of Sanskrit nábhas, "mist, sky", Greek néphos, "cloud", and Latin nebula, "mist".

Lesson 2 of 5.
солнце (sólntse) — the sun.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Russian for a field, then the Russian for the sky.

You'll want to know: солнце.
солнце (sólntse) — "the sun". The л is written and not said.

The word, taken apart.
солнце (sólntse) from Proto-Slavic sŭlnĭce, a diminutive of sŭlnĭ, "sun", built like сердце, "heart" (Vasmer). Vasmer lists Lithuanian sáulė, "sun", and Sanskrit svar, "sun, light"; Latin sōl and English sun go back to the same PIE word.

Lesson 3 of 5.
луна (luná) — the moon.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Russian for the sky, then the Russian for the sun.

You'll want to know: луна.
луна (luná) — "the moon".

The word, taken apart.
луна (luná) from Proto-Slavic luna, from louksnā, "the bright one" — inherited. Vasmer lists Latin lūna, Old Prussian lauxnos, "stars", and Greek lýchnos, "a lamp", all from a root "to shine", with луч, "a ray".

You'll want to know: звезда.
звезда (zvezdá) — "a star".

The word, taken apart.
звезда (zvezdá) from Proto-Slavic gvězda — inherited; г before ě became з. Vasmer relates it to Lithuanian žvaigždė, "star", and Latvian zvaigzne, from a root "to shine".

Lesson 5 of 5.
облако (óblako) — a cloud.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Russian for the moon, then the Russian for a star.

You'll want to know: облако.
облако (óblako) — "a cloud".

The word, taken apart.
облако (óblako) — borrowed from Church Slavonic, says Vasmer; the native form was Old Russian obolokŭ. Both are ob-volkŭ, "what is drawn over", from the root of волочь, "to drag", a cousin of Greek hélkō, "I drag".
