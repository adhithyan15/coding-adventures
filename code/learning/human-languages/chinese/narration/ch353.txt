Lesson 1 of 6.
shuōfú — to talk someone round; to persuade.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Mandarin for what one really meant, then the Mandarin for to make your view known.

You'll want to know: shuōfú.
shuōfú — "to talk someone round; to persuade".
wǒ juéde nǐ néng shuōfú tā — I think you can talk him round.
Its characters come later; for now it is a word for the ear.

The word, taken apart.
shuōfú is built from 说 'to say' and fú, both inherited from Middle Chinese, sywet and bjuwk (Guangyun, in Baxter's transcription), which glosses the first 'to tell' and the second 'to serve' and 'clothes'. How the pair came to mean persuading is not settled.

Lesson 2 of 6.
道理 (dàoli) — sense; the reason something makes sense.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Mandarin for to make your view known, then the Mandarin for to talk someone round.

You'll want to know: 道理.
道理 — dàoli — "sense; the reason something makes sense".
你说得很有道理 nǐ shuō de hěn yǒu dàoli — You've got a point there.
The new character, in pieces: 道 (dào) — twelve strokes in two pieces: first a head piece of nine, two short strokes, a bar, a short left-falling stroke and a narrow box with two bars inside; then the walking piece of 这 and 过, three strokes, written last. The next lesson puts it in your hand.

The word, taken apart.
道理 (dàoli) is built from 道 (dào) 'a way' and 理 'order', both inherited from Middle Chinese, dawX and liX (Guangyun, in Baxter's transcription), which glosses the first 'a principle' and 'a road', and the second 'to put in order'. The second is said light here.

What you've built.

Script — a head piece, then the walking piece.
道 (dào).
Two pieces: the head piece first, the walking piece last, although it sits on the left and along the foot. Count the pieces, not the strokes.
The head piece, nine strokes. A short stroke falling to the right and a short stroke falling to the left, like two horns. A horizontal under them. A short left-falling stroke. Then a narrow box: a vertical, a horizontal that turns down, two short horizontals inside and one closing the foot.
The walking piece, three strokes, as in 这: a dot, a stroke that zigzags down, then a long sweep along the foot to the right, under the head piece.
9 + 3 = twelve.

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]

Lesson 4 of 6.
不见得 (bújiànde) — not necessarily.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Mandarin for to talk someone round, then the Mandarin for the sense something makes.

You'll want to know: 不见得.
不见得 — bújiànde — "not necessarily".
guì de dōngxi bújiànde hǎo — Expensive things aren't necessarily good.
The tone of bù changes here: before a falling tone it is said bú, and the spelling writes the change.

The word, taken apart.
不见得 (bújiànde) is built from 不 'not', 见 'to see' and 得 'to get', all three inherited from Middle Chinese, pjut, kenH and tok (Guangyun, in Baxter's transcription), which glosses the first as another word for not, the second 'to see' and the third 'gain'. How the three came to mean not necessarily is not settled.
