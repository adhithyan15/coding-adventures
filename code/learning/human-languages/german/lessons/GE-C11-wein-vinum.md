# Wein and vinum

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GE-LEX-WEIN-01, GE-ETYMON-WASSER-WATER-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] *Wasser* and *water* differ by a consonant. *Wein* and **wine**
differ by nothing you can hear. That difference is the lesson.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[GE-ETYMON-WEIN-VINUM-01]; assesses=[GE-LEX-WEIN-01, GE-GRAMMAR-SOUND-LAW-01] -->

*Wein* is **not** an inherited Germanic word. It was **borrowed from Latin
*vīnum*** in Roman times, when the legions brought the grapevine north of the
Alps.

Here is how you can tell without being told. Inherited words drift apart, because
a sound law runs through them:

| pair | how far apart |
|---|---|
| *Wasser* / water | a whole consonant — *ss* against *t* |
| *Mutter* / mother | two shifts of distance |
| *Wein* / wine / *vīnum* | **no distance at all** |

Three words that match this closely are not three cousins who happen to look
alike. They are **one word, borrowed**, arriving too late for the law to touch it.

## Why it's said this way
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GE-ETYMON-WEIN-VINUM-01, GE-GRAMMAR-NATIVE-VS-LOAN-01, GE-LEX-WEIN-01] -->

The vine did not grow north of the Alps until Rome carried it there. A people
who have never had a thing have no word for it, and when the thing arrives the
word arrives with it — which is the same reason *Uhr* is Latin and the twelve
months are Latin.

So one table holds both halves of the rule. *Brot* and *Wasser* were always
here and are German's own. *Wein* came on a cart, and its name was in the same
load.
