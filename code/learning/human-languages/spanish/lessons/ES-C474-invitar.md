# invitar — to invite

## You'll want to know: invitar
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-C474-PAGO-01]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C282-MEET-05] -->

**invitar** — to invite.

> Nos **invitaron** a la fiesta.

You have met this verb once without being handed it. [El
invitado](./ES-C282-invitado.md) named it in passing — *"from **invitar**,
Latin **invitare**, to invite; **invitado** is its participle"* — and then
stayed with the guest. Here it is as yours.

*Invitar a alguien a algo* — the person with **a**, and the thing with **a**
as well.

## Grammar Lens: in Spanish, inviting can mean paying
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C474-PAGO-01, ES-LEX-PAGAR] -->

This is the use you will meet first in real life, and English has no single
word for it.

| Spanish | what it means |
|---|---|
| *te invito* | **I'm paying** |
| *invito yo* | this one's on me |

Somebody who says *te invito a un café* is not asking whether you are free.
They are telling you they will pay for it. Answering *vale, ¿a qué hora?* when
the coffee is in front of you will land oddly.

So *invitar* covers both the asking and the paying, and which one is meant is
settled by whether a time is mentioned.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C474-PAGO-01] -->

Latin **invitare**, to invite — and the honest answer about what is inside it
is that nobody is sure. The older guess tied it to a root about **willingness**,
which would make an invitation an appeal to somebody's good will.

English took the whole word twice: **invite**, and the noun **invitation**.
There is no native English word for this, which is a small clue that the
custom travelled with the Latin.

What Spanish added is the paying. That is not in the Latin and not in the
English — it is a courtesy that attached itself to the word here.
