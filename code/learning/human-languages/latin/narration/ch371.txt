Latin, chapter 371: At the Speakers' Platform.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
rōstra, rōstrōrum — the speakers' platform (in the Forum).

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for lightness, then the Latin for rashness.

You'll want to know: rōstra, rōstrōrum.
rōstra, rōstrōrum — "the speakers' platform (in the Forum)".
Senex in rōstra ascendit. The old man went up onto the speakers' platform.
rōstra, rōstrōrum n. pl. The platform in the Forum was decked with the bronze beaks (rōstra) of captured warships. English rostrum.

The word, taken apart.
rōstra is the plural of rōstrum "a beak, a ship's ram", which is built on rōdō "to gnaw" (Lewis & Short). English rostrum comes from it.

You'll want to know: cōntiō, cōntiōnis.
cōntiō, cōntiōnis — "a public meeting; a speech made to it".
Cōnsul cōntiōnem habuit. The consul held a public meeting.
cōntiō, cōntiōnis f. A meeting of the people called to hear a magistrate, where no vote was taken; also the speech made there.

The word, taken apart.
cōntiō is contracted from an old form coventiō "a coming together" (Lewis & Short), built from con- and veniō "to come".

You'll want to know: prōnūntiō, prōnūntiāre.
prōnūntiō, prōnūntiāre — "to proclaim, to announce; to deliver (a speech)".
Praecō sententiam iūdicum prōnūntiāvit. The herald announced the judges' verdict.
prōnūntiō, prōnūntiāre, prōnūntiāvī, prōnūntiātum. Built on nūntiō, to announce. It is also a speaker's delivery. English pronounce.

The word, taken apart.
prōnūntiō is built from prō "forth" and nūntiō "to announce" (Lewis & Short). English pronounce comes from it through French.

Lesson 4 of 5.
recitō, recitāre — to read aloud, to recite.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a public meeting, then the Latin for to proclaim.

You'll want to know: recitō, recitāre.
recitō, recitāre — "to read aloud, to recite".
Poēta carmina sua amīcīs recitāvit. The poet read his poems aloud to his friends.
recitō, recitāre, recitāvī, recitātum. At Rome authors read new work aloud to invited listeners. English recite.

The word, taken apart.
recitō is built from re- and citō "to call, to summon" (EtymDB; Lewis & Short): to call words out again. English recite comes from it.

Lesson 5 of 5.
temerē — rashly, blindly; at random.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to proclaim, then the Latin for to read aloud.

You'll want to know: temerē.
temerē — "rashly, blindly; at random".
Nihil temerē faciēbat. He did nothing rashly.
An adverb; its noun is temeritās, rashness. Nōn temerē, not without good reason.

The word, taken apart.
temerē is inherited from Proto-Italic temezi "in the dark" (EtymDB), kin to tenebrae "darkness" (Lewis & Short): to act blindly.
