# vier — "four," written with a *v* and said with an *f*

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GE-LEX-DREI-01, GE-GRAMMAR-SOUND-LAW-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] The fourth number is the easiest one yet — as long as you do not
trust the first letter.

## You'll want to know: vier
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[GE-LEX-VIER-01]; assesses=[GE-SOUND-EI-AS-EYE-01] -->

> **vier** — "four."

## Sounds you'll need
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[GE-SOUND-V-AS-F-01]; assesses=[GE-LEX-VIER-01, GE-SOUND-EI-AS-EYE-01] -->

- `v-as-f` — German **v** is said as an **f**. It is not a *v* sound; that job
  belongs to **w**.
- `ie-long-ee` — **ie** is a long *ee*, the mirror of **ei**.

So *vier* is *feer*. Put the two vowel rules side by side and the pair is
settled for good:

| Spelling | Sound | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **ei** | *eye* | *eins*, *zwei*, *drei* |
| **ie** | *ee* | *vier*, *wieder* |

Both times, **the second letter is the one you say**.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[GE-ETYMON-VIER-FOUR-02]; assesses=[GE-LEX-VIER-01, GE-GRAMMAR-SOUND-LAW-01] -->

**vier** is English **four**, and once you know the *v* is an *f* you can hear
it: *feer*, *four*. Latin's cousin is *quattuor*, which is where Spanish
*cuatro* and French *quatre* come from — so this is a number where English sides
with German and not with the Romance languages at all.

The *v*-said-as-*f* is not special to this word. German writes **v** in a
handful of its oldest words — *vier*, *Vater*, *von*, *voll* — and says every
one of them with an *f*, which is exactly the letter English uses in *four*,
*father*, *from*, *full*.
