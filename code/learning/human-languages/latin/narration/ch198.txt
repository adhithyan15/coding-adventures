vetō, vetāre — to forbid.

Warm-up.

You'll want to know: vetō, vetāre.
vetō, vetāre — "to forbid".

The word, taken apart.
vetō has no sure origin; Lewis & Short call it doubtful. English veto, "I forbid", is the same word.

Lesson 2 of 5.
dēfendō, dēfendere — to defend.

You'll want to know: dēfendō, dēfendere.
dēfendō, dēfendere — "to defend".

The word, taken apart.
dēfendō is dē, "away", with an old verb fendō, "to strike" (Lewis & Short), usually traced to Proto-Indo-European gwhen-, "to strike, to ward off" (EtymDB 2.1): to strike away, to ward off.

Lesson 3 of 5.
morior, morī — to die.

Warm-up.

Before the new one: say the Latin for to forbid, then the Latin for to defend.

You'll want to know: morior, morī.
morior, morī — "to die".

The word, taken apart.
morior is inherited, usually traced to Proto-Indo-European mer-, "to die" (EtymDB 2.1). English murder is a distant cousin.

Lesson 4 of 5.
nūbō, nūbere — to marry (of a bride).

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to defend, then the Latin for to die.

You'll want to know: nūbō, nūbere.
nūbō, nūbere — "to marry (of a bride)".

The word, taken apart.
nūbō is inherited, usually traced to Proto-Indo-European snewbh-, "to marry, to wed" (EtymDB 2.1). English nuptial comes from its participle nūptus, "married".

Lesson 5 of 5.
quiēscō, quiēscere — to rest.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to die, then the Latin for to marry.

You'll want to know: quiēscō, quiēscere.
quiēscō, quiēscere — "to rest".

The word, taken apart.
quiēscō is built on quiēs, "rest" (Lewis & Short), with -scō, the ending of verbs of becoming: to come to rest. quiēs is usually traced to Proto-Indo-European kwyeh1-, "to rest" (EtymDB 2.1).
