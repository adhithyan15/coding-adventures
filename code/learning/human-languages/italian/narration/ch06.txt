Italian, chapter 6: Numbers One to Ten.
2 lessons. Both can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 2.
uno, due, tre, quattro, cinque — counting to five.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Of all the Romance sisters, Italian kept the numbers closest to Latin — it stayed in Italy, next to Rome, and wore the words down the least. Beside each come its Spanish and French cousins, for contrast.

Sounds you'll need.
double-tt — quattro: hold the double t audibly, KWAT-tro (Italian really doubles its consonants).
c-before-i — cinque = CHEEN-kweh: c before i/e is a "ch" sound, but the -que stays a hard kw.

The numbers, taken apart.
[a table of 5 rows, read aloud]
Italian from Latin: uno (1) from ūnus. Spanish / French: uno / un. English cousins: unit, union, unique (and a/an, one).
Italian from Latin: due (2) from duo. Spanish / French: dos / deux. English cousins: duo, dual, duet, double.
Italian from Latin: tre (3) from trēs. Spanish / French: tres / trois. English cousins: trio, triple, triangle.
Italian from Latin: quattro (4) from quattuor. Spanish / French: cuatro / quatre. English cousins: quart, quarter, quatrain.
Italian from Latin: cinque (5) from quīnque. Spanish / French: cinco / cinq. English cousins: quintet, quintessence.
Notice cinque — Italian kept Latin quīnque almost whole (-que and all), while Spanish softened it to cinco and French clipped it to cinq. And quattro is why a musical foursome is a quartet and four singers make a quattro-anything. Italian is the sister who changed her Latin the least.

Grammar Lens: uno becomes un / uno / una.
uno is also "a/an," and it flexes by gender and the following sound: un (before most masc. nouns), uno (before tricky masc. clusters like s+consonant or z-), una (fem.). un caffè "a/one coffee," una birra "a/one beer." The others (due, tre…) don't change.

Guided Practice.
[pause 1 second]
[your turn — say: "uno, due, tre, quattro, cinque" — count up]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: each with its English cousin — "due/duo, tre/trio, quattro/quart, cinque/quintet"]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: "un caffè, una birra" — uno becomes un/una before a noun]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Give 1–5 in Italian. (Uno, due, tre, quattro, cinque.) Which Italian number kept Latin quīnque most intact, versus Spanish and French? (cinque, vs cinco / cinq.) What does un/una mean besides "one"? ("A/an.") Next: 6–10, and the months hidden inside them.


Lesson 2 of 2.
sei, sette, otto, nove, dieci — and the calendar's secret.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
The rest of the count hides a calendar fact: settembre–dicembre are Latin for 7, 8, 9, 10. Italian even keeps the old Latin double consonants that show it.

Sounds you'll need.
double-tt — sette = SET-teh, otto = OT-toh: lean on the doubled t, a giveaway that Latin's -pt- and -ct- both collapsed into -tt-.
c-before-i — dieci = DYE-chee (the c before i becomes "ch").

The numbers, taken apart.
[a table of 5 rows, read aloud]
Italian from Latin: sei (6) from sex. Spanish twin: seis. English cousins: sextet, sextant.
Italian from Latin: sette (7) from septem. Spanish twin: siete. English cousins: settembre (the 7th month!), septet.
Italian from Latin: otto (8) from octō. Spanish twin: ocho. English cousins: ottobre (8th), octave, octopus.
Italian from Latin: nove (9) from novem. Spanish twin: nueve. English cousins: novembre (9th), novena.
Italian from Latin: dieci (10) from decem. Spanish twin: diez. English cousins: dicembre (10th), decimal, decade.
Watch the doubled consonants do historical work: Latin septem had a -pt- that Italian smoothed to -tt- becomes sette; Latin octō had a -ct- that likewise became -tt- becomes otto. Italian didn't drop those clusters (as French did in huit) — it assimilated them into twins. So otto and ottobre still show the 8 side by side.

Grammar Lens: why the months are "off by two".
Settembre means "7th," but it's the 9th month. The old Roman year began in March, so September really was the seventh; later luglio and agosto (July and August, for Julius and Augustus) pushed the counting months down two slots. The Latin numbers stuck to the labels. Every dicembre, you say Roman "ten" — just as in Spanish diciembre and French décembre.

[your turn — say: "sette/settembre, otto/ottobre, nove/novembre, dieci/dicembre"]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]
[your turn — say: "octō becomes otto" — the -ct- becoming a doubled -tt-]

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Give 6–10 in Italian. (Sei, sette, otto, nove, dieci.) What happened to Latin's -ct- in octō becomes otto? (It assimilated to a doubled -tt-.) Why is dicembre Latin for "ten"? (The Roman year began in March.) Next chapter builds on this toward time and days.
