Urdu, chapter 25: More Than One.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
کمرے (kamre) — more than one.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say in the room, and say what the noun had to do first.
[your turn — recall: two chapters back, the postposition that marks the one a thing is done to — ko]
[pause 8 seconds for the answer]

You'll want to know: the plural.
کمرہ — kamrā — a room.
کمرے — kamre — rooms.
Twenty-four chapters, and no noun in this book has ever been more than one.
Look hard at the plural, because you have seen it before. کمرے (kamre) is exactly the oblique singular from chapter twenty-three — same three letters, same sound. Urdu spells rooms and of the room identically and lets the rest of the sentence tell them apart.

Grammar Lens: the table, and what the postposition settles.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
direct. one: کمرہ. more than one: کمرے (kamre).
oblique. one: کمرے (kamre). more than one: next lesson.
Three of the four cells are filled and two of them look the same. A postposition settles it: کمرے (kamre) میں can only be in the room, because a plural before a postposition takes a different shape again — which is the next lesson.
Nouns not ending in -ā do not change at all. ماں is one mother or several and is spelled ماں either way, so number, like the oblique, is often invisible in Urdu and has to be read off the sentence.
And the adjective agrees: کالا کمرہ becomes کالے کمرے (kamre).

Lesson 2 of 5.
کمروں (kamroṅ) — more than one, before a postposition.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say rooms. Then say in the room, and say why those two are spelled the same.

You'll want to know: کمروں.
کمروں میں — kamroṅ meṅ — in the rooms.
Here is the fourth cell, and it is the one that removes the ambiguity:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
direct. one: کمرہ. more than one: کمرے (kamre).
oblique. one: کمرے (kamre). more than one: کمروں (kamroṅ).
-وں is unmistakable. Nothing else in Urdu ends that way, so a reader who meets it knows immediately that the noun is plural and that a postposition is coming.

Grammar Lens: the ending that catches every noun.
This is the one place where every Urdu noun changes visibly, including the ones that have refused to move so far. کان is one ear or two and is spelled the same either way, and it does not mark an oblique — but put it in the plural and in front of a postposition and it becomes کانوں.
So the rule to hold is: -وں on the end means plural, and something is coming after me.
That final ں is the nūn-e-ghunna of chapter one — the nasal with no dots that colours the vowel rather than sounding as an n. The same letter ends ہاں and کہاں and یہاں.
And the demonstrative in front of it is already yours: the plural oblique ان from the last chapter.

Lesson 3 of 5.
ہم (ham) — we.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say I. Then name the three words for you.

You'll want to know: ہم.
ہم یہاں ہیں — ham yahāṅ haiṅ — we are here.
Twenty-four chapters of this book have been about one person at a time. میں or آپ, and nobody else. ہم (ham) is the first pronoun that covers a group.
Notice the verb. میں … ہوں but ہم (ham) … ہیں: the plural subject takes the plural form of to be, and it is the same ہیں you have been saying to آپ since chapter four.

Grammar Lens: a "we" that is sometimes one person.
Urdu does the same thing with ہم (ham) that it does with آپ and ان: a plural form used of one person, for distance and formality.
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
word: آپ. grammatically: you, plural. often used for: one person, respectfully.
word: ان. grammatically: those. often used for: one person, respectfully.
word: ہم (ham). grammatically: we. often used for: one speaker, formally.
A speaker who says ہم (ham) of themselves is being formal or a little grand, and in much of Pakistan it is the ordinary way to say I in some registers. A learner should say میں and should recognise ہم (ham).

Lesson 4 of 5.
ہمارا (hamārā) — our.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say we. Then say my name and your name, and notice that one of them has a separate little word in it and the other does not.

You'll want to know: ہمارا.
ہمارا کمرہ — hamārā kamrā — our room.
ہماری ماں — hamārī māṅ — our mother.
And now the thing worth seeing. میرا has looked like a word since chapter two and it is not one:
آپ کا — āp kā — pronoun, then کا, written apart.
میرا — merā — pronoun and کا, fused.
ہمارا — hamārā — pronoun and کا, fused.
All three are the same construction. The first person fuses and the respectful second person does not, which is an accident of wear, not a difference of grammar.

Grammar Lens: the possessive agrees, like any adjective.
کا ends in -ا, so it behaves like کالا and پرانا and changes with the noun after it:
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
the noun is: masculine, direct. the possessive: -ا. example: ہمارا (hamārā) کمرہ.
the noun is: feminine. the possessive: -ی. example: ہماری ماں.
the noun is: oblique or plural. the possessive: -ے. example: ہمارے کمرے (kamre) میں.
That is the same three-way agreement chapter thirteen taught for colours, applied to a postposition. Nothing new to learn — one rule paying twice.
