# Review — the three se's

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-SE-LLAMA, ES-GRAMMAR-SE-IMPERSONAL] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Nothing new. Say *se llama*, then *se habla español*, then
*se lo digo*. Three sentences, three *se*s, learned about a hundred chapters
apart.

## What you've built
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-GRAMMAR-REFLEXIVE-THIRD-PERSON, ES-GRAMMAR-SE-IMPERSONAL, ES-GRAMMAR-SE-PASSIVE-AGREEMENT, ES-GRAMMAR-SE-FOR-LE, ES-ETYMON-GELO, ES-LEX-SE-LLAMA] -->

| | example | what the *se* is |
|---|---|---|
| **reflexive** | *se llama* | himself |
| **impersonal** | *se habla español* | nobody named |
| **the impostor** | *se lo digo* | *le*, in disguise |

And here is the fact that makes this a review rather than a fourth thing to
learn:

> **The first two are the same word. The third is not.**

The impersonal grew out of the reflexive — *the books sell themselves* becoming
*books are sold* — which is why the verb still agrees with the thing. Same word,
stretched.

The third only looks like them. It is Latin *illī* — the *le* you already know —
which arrived at this spelling by an accident of medieval pronunciation, as you
saw when *gelo* became *se lo*.

So Spanish does not have three *se*s. It has **two, and a coincidence**.

## Grammar Lens: how to tell them apart in the wild
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-GRAMMAR-SE-FOR-LE, ES-GRAMMAR-SE-IMPERSONAL, ES-LEX-COMO-SE-DICE] -->

You will not have to work this out consciously for long, but while you do:

1. **Is there a *lo*, *la*, *los* or *las* right after it?** Then it is the
   impostor: *se lo*, *se la*. It means *to him / to her / to them*.
2. **Otherwise, is anybody named as the doer?** If not, it is the impersonal:
   *se habla*, *se dice*, *se come bien*.
3. **Otherwise it is the reflexive**, pointing back at whoever the sentence is
   about: *se llama*, *se levanta*.

The first test is nearly free, because that direct object pronoun is sitting
right there in the open.
