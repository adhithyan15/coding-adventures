# Unit 9: Numbers 11-30

## Warm-up

[PAUSE 2s] Count *uno* to *diez* out loud. Today extends that up to thirty
— and the pattern behind it is worth watching closely.

## New Material

**once, doce, trece, catorce, quince** — 11-15
> ← Latin *undecim, duodecim, tredecim, quattuordecim, quindecim* — each
> literally "one-ten," "two-ten," etc. (*-decim* is the same *decem* root
> from *diez*).

**dieciséis, diecisiete, dieciocho, diecinueve** — 16-19
> These fuse *diez y seis* ("ten and six") into one written word. From here
> the pattern is transparent: *diez-i-[number]*.

**veinte** — 20
> ← Latin *viginti* (cognate with English *vigesimal*, "base-20"). 21-29
> fuse the same way as the teens: *veintiuno, veintidós...veintinueve*
> ("twenty-and-one," compressed).

**treinta** — 30
> ← Latin *triginta*. Unlike 16-29, *treinta y uno* (31) stays as two
> separate words — the fusion pattern stops at 29.

## Grammar Lens

Spanish visibly fuses "ten-and-six" into *dieciséis*. English looks
different but secretly did the exact same thing, just longer ago: "sixteen"
is *six* + *teen*, and *teen* is a worn-down form of "ten." The fusion is
identical — English's just isn't spelled in a way that still shows it.

[PAUSE 3s] Which number is the last one that fuses into a single word
before the pattern switches to two separate words? ... *veintinueve* (29) —
*treinta y uno* (31) onward stays split.
