Latin, chapter 427: Overnight stop, Coachman, Post, Travel money, To set out on.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
mānsiō, mānsiōnis — an overnight stop, a halting place; a stay.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for weary, then the Latin for to wear out.

You'll want to know: mānsiō, mānsiōnis.
mānsiō, mānsiōnis — "an overnight stop, a halting place; a stay".
Prīmā mānsiōne viātōrēs cēnant et dormiunt. At the first halting place the travellers have dinner and sleep.
Third declension, feminine: mānsiō, mānsiōnis f. A journey could be counted in mānsiōnēs, day stages. English mansion comes from it.

The word, taken apart.
mānsiō is built on maneō 'to stay, to remain' (EtymDB; Lewis & Short), through its participle mānsus. English mansion comes from it through French.

raedārius, raedāriī — "a coachman, the driver of a four-wheeled carriage".
Raedārius equōs ad raedam dūcit et iungit. The coachman leads the horses to the carriage and harnesses them.
Second declension, masculine: raedārius, raedāriī m.

The word, taken apart.
raedārius is built on raeda 'four-wheeled travelling carriage' with the suffix -ārius 'one who works with' (Lewis & Short). Lewis & Short call raeda probably Celtic, citing Quintilian.

Lesson 3 of 5.
statiō, statiōnis — a post, a station; a stopping place; an anchorage.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for an overnight stop, then the Latin for a coachman.

You'll want to know: statiō, statiōnis.
statiō, statiōnis — "a post, a station; a stopping place; an anchorage".
Ad statiōnem mīlitēs viātōrēs exspectant. At the post soldiers wait for the travellers.
Third declension, feminine: statiō, statiōnis f. A guard post on a road, or a place where ships lie at anchor. English station comes from it.

The word, taken apart.
statiō is built on stō 'to stand', from PIE steh₂- (EtymDB; Lewis & Short): first a standing still, then a place where people stand or stop. English station comes from it.

Lesson 4 of 5.
viāticum, viāticī — travel money; provisions for a journey.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a coachman, then the Latin for a post.

You'll want to know: viāticum, viāticī.
viāticum, viāticī — "travel money; provisions for a journey".
Pater fīliō viāticum dat, et fīlius ad urbem proficīscitur. The father gives his son travel money, and the son sets out for the city.
Second declension, neuter: viāticum, viāticī n. What you take with you for the road: coins, food or both.

The word, taken apart.
viāticum is the neuter of viāticus 'of a road or journey', built on via 'road' (EtymDB; Lewis & Short). English keeps it as viaticum.

Lesson 5 of 5.
ingredior, ingredī — to set out on, to enter upon; to go in.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for a post, then the Latin for travel money.

You'll want to know: ingredior, ingredī.
ingredior, ingredī — "to set out on, to enter upon; to go in".
Prīmā lūce viātōrēs iter longum ingrediuntur. At first light the travellers set out on the long journey.
Deponent: ingredior, ingredī, ingressus sum, with an accusative: iter ingredī, to begin a journey. English ingredient comes from its participle.

The word, taken apart.
ingredior is built from in- 'in, on' and gradior 'to step' (EtymDB; Lewis & Short). English ingredient comes from its present participle ingrediēns.
