## Across the family: the inherited stock
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[AR-CONCEPT-C11-YAD-01, AR-CONCEPT-C11-YAD-02, AR-CONCEPT-C11-RAS-01, AR-CONCEPT-C10-AB-UMM-02, AR-CONCEPT-C10-AKH-UKHT-02] -->

| Arabic | meaning | Hebrew cousin |
|---|---|---|
| **يد** *yad* | hand | **יד** *yad* |
| **رأس** *raʾs* | head | **ראש** *rosh* |
| **أب** *ab* | father | **אב** *av* |
| **أم** *umm* | mother | **אם** *em* |
| **أخ** / **أخت** *akh / ukht* | brother / sister | **אח** *ach* |

Neither language borrowed these from the other; both inherited them. **يد** has
barely moved at all — two letters, one syllable, and its Hebrew cousin is said
almost the same way after several thousand years.

**أخت** is **أخ** with one letter added. Set that beside the colour words, which
needed a whole reshaped template to change gender, and the contrast is the point:
the oldest words take the cheapest endings.

## Why it's said this way: roots still at work
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[AR-CONCEPT-C11-RAS-02, AR-CONCEPT-C10-AB-UMM-02] -->

**رئيس** *raʾīs*, **president** or **chief**, is built on *head* with nothing
borrowed — Arabic reached for its own oldest word, the way English reached for
Latin *caput* to make *capital* and *captain*.

**أم** and **أبو** are still forming names today. Add a child's name and you have a
**kunya**: *mother of* and *father of*, used as an honorific in ordinary speech.

## Script: the two halves of the farewell
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[AR-CONCEPT-W12-MAA-SALAMA-01, AR-CONCEPT-W12-MAA-SALAMA-02] -->

- [YOU WRITE: **مع** — *mīm*, round head, joining left into *ʿayn*]
- [YOU WRITE: **السلامة** — **الـ**, then *sīn · lām · alif · mīm*, then the tied **ة**]

**مع** needed no new letters when you first wrote it. And **السلامة** is not a new
word: it is **الـ** wrapped round the very first word you learned to draw, with a
tied *tāʾ* closing it — the same **ة** that closes **ملعقة**, the spoon in your
hand.
