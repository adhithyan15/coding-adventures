# muy — the same word, bitten short

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-MUCHO, ES-LEX-CANSADO] -->

[PAUSE 2s] You have said *mucho* since chapter six — *mucho gusto*.

[PAUSE 1s] Now you want to say that something is **very** something. *Cansado*
is tired; how do you say *very tired*?

## The word, taken apart: muy
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-MUY, ES-ETYMON-MUY-MULTUM]; assesses=[] -->

> *Estoy* ***muy*** *cansado.*

**muy** is not a new word to learn. It is ***mucho***, with its ending bitten
off. Both are Latin ***multum***, *much* — one kept whole, one worn short.

[PAUSE 2s] Spanish does this a lot: a long form for standing alone, a short
form for leaning on the word after it. You will meet the same trick again in
this chapter's neighbours.

## Why it's said this way
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-GRAMMAR-DEGREE-MUY]; assesses=[ES-LEX-BIEN] -->

The two shapes are not interchangeable, and the rule is easy because the word
tells you itself.

| | |
|---|---|
| ***mucho*** *café* | in front of a **noun** — the long form |
| ***muy*** *grande* | in front of an **adjective** — the short one |
| ***muy*** *bien* | in front of an **adverb** — the short one |

[PAUSE 2s] So *muy* never stands alone. If somebody asks how you are and you
want to say *very*, you cannot answer *muy* — there is nothing for it to lean
on. You would answer *muy bien*.

[PAUSE 2s] And this is the strongest *very* Spanish has in one word. *Muy
grande* is as big as *grande* gets without reaching for another sentence.
