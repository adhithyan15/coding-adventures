Spanish, chapter 709: Echar de menos.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
el aislamiento — isolation.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Spanish for to envy, then the Spanish for resentment.

You'll want to know: el aislamiento.
el aislamiento — "isolation".
Muchas personas mayores viven en aislamiento. Many older people live in isolation.
Aislado — isolated, cut off.

The word, taken apart.
aislamiento is built on aislar "to isolate", made from isla "island", which is inherited from Latin īnsula (EtymDB): to be cut off like an island.

Lesson 3 of 5.
extrañar — to miss (someone); to find strange.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Spanish for isolation, then the Spanish for lonely.

You'll want to know: extrañar.
extrañar — "to miss (someone); to find strange".
Te extraño mucho. I miss you a lot.
To miss is the Latin American sense; in Spain people mostly say echar de menos. Me extraña — I find it strange.

The word, taken apart.
extrañar is inherited from Late Latin extrāneāre, built on extrāneus "external, foreign, strange" (EtymDB); English strange comes from the same adjective through French.

Lesson 4 of 5.
la nostalgia — nostalgia, homesickness.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Spanish for lonely, then the Spanish for to miss.

You'll want to know: la nostalgia.
la nostalgia — "nostalgia, homesickness".
Siento nostalgia de mi pueblo. I feel homesick for my village.
Nostalgia de + place or time.

The word, taken apart.
nostalgia was borrowed from New Latin nostalgia (EtymDB), a word a Swiss doctor coined from Greek nóstos "return home" and álgos "pain".

Lesson 5 of 5.
añorar — to long for, to yearn for.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Spanish for to miss, then the Spanish for nostalgia.

You'll want to know: añorar.
añorar — "to long for, to yearn for".
Añoro los veranos de mi infancia. I long for the summers of my childhood.
A little literary; it takes a direct object: añoro mi casa.

The word, taken apart.
añorar was borrowed from Catalan enyorar, which comes from Latin ignōrāre "not to know" (EtymDB); the sense moved from not knowing where someone is to missing them.
