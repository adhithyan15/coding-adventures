## You'll want to know first — надо
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[RU-LEX-NADO-01]; assesses=[RU-LEX-MOCH-01, RU-GRAMMAR-INFINITIVE-AFTER-VERB-01, RU-LEX-CHITAT] -->

- **надо** — *náda* — **it is necessary, one must**

Stress the first syllable; the **о** at the end is unstressed and says *a*, so
the word comes out *NÁ-da*.

Like **хотеть** and **мочь**, it is followed by a bare infinitive:

- **надо читать** — *náda chitát* — one must read.

## Grammar lens — the sentence with no subject
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[RU-GRAMMAR-NADO-IMPERSONAL-01]; assesses=[RU-LEX-NADO-01, RU-GRAMMAR-ZERO-COPULA, RU-LEX-CHITAT] -->

Look at **надо читать** again and count the people in it. There are none.

There is no **я**, no **ты**, and no verb agreeing with anybody. English cannot do
this: it has to invent a subject — *one* must read, *it* is necessary, *you*
should read — because an English sentence is not allowed to go without one.

Russian is allowed. **надо** is an **impersonal** word: the obligation is stated
and left hanging in the air, belonging to nobody in particular. This is the same
freedom you met when the present tense of **быть** turned out to be missing
altogether — Russian is content to leave out what it does not need.

Naming the person who must do it needs a case this book has not opened yet, so
for now the impersonal form is the whole of it — and it is genuinely useful on
its own.

## Wrap-up Recall
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[RU-LEX-NADO-01, RU-GRAMMAR-NADO-IMPERSONAL-01, RU-LEX-DUMAT] -->

[PAUSE 3s] How many people are named in *надо читать*? (**None** — it is
impersonal.) What must English add that Russian does not? (**A subject** —
*one*, *it*, or *you*.) Which other missing word does that remind you of? (**The
present tense of быть**, which Russian also leaves out.) And one look back: which
council is named after the verb *to think*? (**The Duma**.)
