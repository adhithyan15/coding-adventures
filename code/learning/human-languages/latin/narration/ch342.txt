Latin, chapter 342: Clādēs.
5 lessons. All 5 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 5.
fugō, fugāre — to put to flight, to rout.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to take by storm, then the Latin for a battering ram.

You'll want to know: fugō, fugāre.
fugō, fugāre — "to put to flight, to rout".
Equitēs nostrī hostēs fugāvērunt. Our cavalry put the enemy to flight.
fugō, fugāre, fugāvī, fugātum: to make others run.

The word, taken apart.
fugō is built on fuga, "flight" (Lewis & Short): to cause to flee.

superō, superāre, superāvī, superātum, from super, 'above'. Also to climb over: montēs superāre.

Lesson 3 of 5.
caedō, caedere — to cut down, to kill; to beat.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to put to flight, then the Latin for to overcome.

You'll want to know: caedō, caedere.
caedō, caedere — "to cut down, to kill; to beat".
Mīlitēs hostēs fugientēs caedēbant. The soldiers were cutting down the fleeing enemy.
caedō, caedere, cecīdī, caesum. Also to fell trees and to beat. Its noun is caedēs.

The word, taken apart.
caedō is inherited from a Proto-Indo-European root meaning "to cut, to hew" (EtymDB). English words in -cide, such as homicide, come from its family.

Lesson 4 of 5.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to overcome, then the Latin for to cut down.

You'll want to know: vāstō, vāstāre.
vāstō, vāstāre — "to lay waste, to ravage".
Hostēs agrōs vīllāsque vāstāvērunt. The enemy laid waste the fields and the farmhouses.
vāstō, vāstāre, vāstāvī, vāstātum: to make a land empty. English devastate.

The word, taken apart.
vāstō is built on vāstus, "empty, desolate" (EtymDB, Lewis & Short): to make a place empty. English waste and devastate come from its family.

Lesson 5 of 5.
clādēs, clādis — a defeat, a disaster.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Before the new one: say the Latin for to cut down, then the Latin for to lay waste.

You'll want to know: clādēs, clādis.
clādēs, clādis — "a defeat, a disaster".
Post clādem paucī mīlitēs in castra rediērunt. After the defeat few soldiers returned to the camp.
Feminine, 3rd declension. A heavy defeat with great loss of life.

The word, taken apart.
clādēs is usually traced to a root meaning "to beat, to break": EtymDB gives a Proto-Indo-European root of that sense, and Lewis & Short compare Greek klaō, "to break". The details are not settled.
