Latin, chapter 289: If.
4 lessons. All 4 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 4.
sī — if.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Your very first politeness gave you sīs, a squeezed sī vīs, "if you wish". And you own nisi, "unless".
[pause 1 second]
Both have the word for "if" inside them. Now it stands on its own.

You'll want to know: sī.
sī — "if". It opens the condition, and the condition usually comes first:
Sī vīs, ambulāmus. — If you like, we'll go for a walk. Sī pluit, nōn ambulō. — If it rains, I don't go walking.
[pause 2 seconds]
nisi, "unless, if not", opens the same slot the other way round:
Nisi pluit, ambulō. — Unless it rains, I go walking.
[pause 1 second]
Sī nōn also exists, and it puts the weight on the nōn. Nisi is the everyday way to say "unless".

The word, taken apart: sī.
sī is inherited, usually traced to a small Proto-Indo-European word swe (EtymDB 2.1); Lewis & Short derive it from a pronoun stem and record an older spelling, seī. The details are not settled. nisi, as its own lesson said, is ne, "not", in front of sī.

Lesson 2 of 4.
sī pluit — a condition that may well be true.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say "if it rains, I don't go walking". (Sī pluit, nōn ambulō.)
[pause 1 second]
That is a real condition: it may well rain, and if it does, the second half follows. Latin keeps both halves plain.

Grammar Lens: ordinary verbs on both sides.
Sī pluit, in vīllā manēmus. — If it rains, we stay in the farmhouse.
[pause 2 seconds]
Each half has the tense the facts need. A real condition about the past takes the perfect, the way any past event does:
Sī herī in forō fuistī, amīcum vīdistī. — If you were in the forum yesterday, you saw my friend.
[pause 2 seconds]
The speaker does not know whether you were there. If you were, the seeing follows. Nothing in the verbs is pretending.

Why it's said this way: advice with sī.
[pause 2 seconds]
The second half can be a command, or the dēbēs of advice:
Sī fessus es, quiēsce. — If you are tired, rest. Sī aeger es, quiēscere dēbēs. — If you are ill, you should rest.
[pause 1 second]
Quiēsce is the command of quiēscō: the stem with -e, like ignōsce in ignōsce mihi.

Lesson 3 of 4.
pendet ex — it depends.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
You own pendeō, "to hang". Say "if it rains, we stay in the farmhouse". (Sī pluit, in vīllā manēmus.)
[pause 1 second]
Asked whether you will walk tomorrow, the honest answer is often neither yes nor no.

You'll want to know: pendet ex.
pendet ex — "it depends on", word for word "it hangs from". ex is "out of, from", and the thing it hangs from goes in the ablative:
Tōtum ex tempestāte pendet. — It all depends on the weather.
[pause 2 seconds]
Cicero says it of hope: spēs pendet ex fortūnā, hope hangs on fortune (Lewis & Short quote it under pendeo). English "depend" is the same picture, dē-pendēre, to hang down from.

You'll want to know: in tuā potestāte est.
When it depends on the person you are talking to:
In tuā potestāte est. — It is up to you; it is in your power.
[pause 2 seconds]
You own potestās, "power, the ability to act"; in tuā potestāte is "in your power". Cicero, writing about fate, asks what is in nostrā potestāte, in our power.

The phrase, taken apart.
pendeō, as its own lesson said, is the intransitive partner of pendō, "to weigh, to hang up", usually traced to Proto-Indo-European (s)pend-, "to stretch, to pull" (EtymDB 2.1). ex is inherited from Proto-Indo-European h1eghs, "out" (EtymDB 2.1), and English exit begins with it. A thing that pendet ex is held up by something else, and falls if that gives way.

Lesson 4 of 4.
Review — if, and what it depends on.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say "if you like, we'll go for a walk". (Sī vīs, ambulāmus.) Then "unless it rains". (Nisi pluit.)

What you've built.
Three lessons, and the real condition is yours. Sī or nisi first, then the result, and ordinary verbs on both sides: present, perfect, or a command. And when the answer is neither yes nor no: tōtum ex tempestāte pendet, or, handing it back, in tuā potestāte est.
— Crās iter Rōmam facimus? — Ex tempestāte pendet. Sī pluit, in vīllā manēmus; nisi pluit, iter facimus.
[pause 2 seconds]
Crās, "tomorrow", with a present verb: Latin speech often lets the present stand for a plan.
