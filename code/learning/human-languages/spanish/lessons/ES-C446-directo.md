# directo — straight through, and live

## Warm-up

[PAUSE 2s] Say **recto** and **el tren**. Say **acompañar**. *Recto* is the
shape of a line. Name the word for a train that does not stop.

## You'll want to know: directo
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-C446-MOVER-04]; assesses=[ES-LEX-TREN] -->

**directo** — direct, straight through.

*Un tren directo* — a through train, no changes. *Un vuelo directo* — a direct
flight. *En directo* — **live**, on radio or television, which is the use you
will meet most.

*Directo* also describes a person: *es muy directo* means blunt, and in Spain it
is usually a compliment.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C446-MOVER-04, ES-LEX-RECTO] -->

**di-** apart, thoroughly, plus **-recto**, which you already have.

*Recto* is Latin **rectus**, straightened, ruled — the participle of *regere*,
to rule or guide. To *dirigere* was **to set something straight**, to aim it,
and *directo* is the result of that aiming.

| word | the straightening in it |
|---|---|
| recto | straight |
| directo | straightened **towards** |
| dirigir | to direct, to conduct |

English keeps *regere* everywhere once you look: **correct** (straightened
*with*), **erect** (straightened *up*), **regular** (by the rule), and **rule**
itself.

## Why it's said this way
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[ES-LEX-C446-MOVER-04] -->

**En directo** is the one to fix, because it is not obvious from the parts.

A broadcast *en directo* is going straight from the event to you with nothing in
between — no recording, no editing. English calls that **live**, naming the
event; Spanish names the **path**. Once you see that, *en directo* stops needing
translation: the line is straight.

The opposite is **grabado**, recorded. A Spanish television corner reading *EN
DIRECTO* is telling you that what you are watching is happening now.
