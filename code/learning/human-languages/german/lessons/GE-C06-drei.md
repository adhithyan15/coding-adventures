# drei — "three," and German's missing *th*

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GE-LEX-ZWEI-01, GE-SOUND-EI-AS-EYE-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Third number, third *ei*. And it shows you something that will keep
paying: German has no **th** sound at all, and you can predict what it uses
instead.

## You'll want to know: drei
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[GE-LEX-DREI-01]; assesses=[GE-SOUND-EI-AS-EYE-01, GE-SOUND-LERNEN-01] -->

> **drei** — "three."

Said *dry*: the scraped *r* you met in *lernen*, and the *ei* saying *eye*.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[GE-ETYMON-DREI-THREE-02]; assesses=[GE-LEX-DREI-01, GE-LEX-DER-DIE-DAS-02] -->

**drei** is English **three**. German simply has no *th*, and where English says
one, German says **d**. That is not a fact about numbers — it is a fact about
the whole language, and you have been using it since your first *danke*:

| English | German |
|---|---|
| **th**ree | **d**rei |
| **th**e | **d**er, **d**ie, **d**as |
| **th**ank | **d**anke |
| **th**at | **d**as |

Five words, one swap. Whenever an English word starts with *th*, try a *d* and
you will often land on the German.
