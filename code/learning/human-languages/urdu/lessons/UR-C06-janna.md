# جاننا — “to know,” a word English has been carrying all along

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[UR-LEX-HONA, UR-LEX-JANA, UR-LEX-ANA, UR-LEX-BOLNA, UR-GRAMMAR-NA-INFINITIVE] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Run the four verbs you have — *honā, jānā, ānā, bolnā* — and pull
the stem off each one. The fifth is the closest of them to English.

## You'll want to know first — one word
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[UR-LEX-JANNA, UR-SCRIPT-JANNA-DOUBLE-NUN]; assesses=[] -->

> **جاننا** — *jānnā* — **to know**

From the right edge: **ج** *j*, **ا** long *ā*, then **ن**, then **ن** a
second time, then **ا** long *ā*. Two separate nūns, written out one after
the other, and both are pronounced: hold the *n* long, *jān-nā*, the way
English does across the seam of *thin·ness*. Strip the final **-nā** and the
stem is **jān-**.

That doubling is what keeps **jānnā** “to know” apart from **jānā** “to go.”
One letter, and the whole meaning turns.

## The word, taken apart — the widest family in the chapter
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[UR-ETYMON-JANNA-KNOW]; assesses=[] -->

**jānnā** continues Sanskrit *jānāti*, from the root *jñā-*, “know.” That
root is Indo-European \**ǵneh₃-*, and English is stuffed with it.

Straight down the Germanic line: **know**, **knowledge**, **can** (once “to
know how”), **cunning**, **ken**, and **uncouth** — literally “un-known.”
Borrowed from Latin *(g)nōscere*: **notice**, **note**, **notion**,
**cognition**, **recognize**, **acquaint**, and **ignore**, which is “not to
know.” Borrowed from Greek *gnōsis*: **diagnosis**, **prognosis**, and
**agnostic**.

So the Urdu verb is not a stranger. It is a first cousin of the plainest word
English has for the same idea, and every one of those borrowings is another
place to hang it.
