# اپنا (apnā) — one's own

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[UR-GRAMMAR-IS-KA, UR-GRAMMAR-HAMARA] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Say **his room**, then **our room**. Then say **my name**.

## You'll want to know: اپنا
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[UR-GRAMMAR-APNA]; assesses=[UR-LEX-MAAN, UR-GRAMMAR-IS-KA] -->

> **میں اپنے کمرے میں ہوں** — *maiṅ apne kamre meṅ hūṅ* — **I am in my (own)
> room.**

Here is a rule English does not have and Urdu will not let you skip. When the
owner is **the same person as the subject of the sentence**, Urdu replaces the
ordinary possessive with **اپنا**.

So it is **میرا کمرہ** when somebody else is speaking about my room, and **اپنا
کمرہ** the moment *I* am the one doing something in it.

## Grammar Lens: getting it wrong changes the meaning
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[UR-GRAMMAR-APNA, UR-GRAMMAR-IS-KA, UR-LEX-KAMRA, UR-GRAMMAR-MEIN-POST] -->

This is not a politeness or a nicety. The two sentences mean different things:

| Urdu | what it means |
|---|---|
| **وہ اپنے کمرے میں ہے** | he is in **his own** room |
| **وہ اس کے کمرے میں ہے** | he is in **somebody else's** room |

An English speaker hears *he is in his room* for both and has no way to tell
them apart. Urdu marks the difference and requires you to.

**اپنا** agrees like every other possessive: **اپنا کمرہ**, **اپنی ماں**, **اپنے
کمرے میں**.
