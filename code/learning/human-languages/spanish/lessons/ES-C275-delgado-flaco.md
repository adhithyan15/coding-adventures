## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-LEX-DELGADO-FLACO]; assesses=[] -->

**delgado** and **flaco** both mean "thin."

*Delgado* is Latin **delicatus** — the same word as English **delicate**. It is
the neutral-to-complimentary one: slim.

*Flaco* is Latin **flaccus**, "flabby, drooping" — English **flaccid**. It is
blunter, and depending on where you are and who you are talking to it ranges from
plainly descriptive to unkind.

**Regional warning, and it matters more than the etymology.** In much of Latin
America *flaco* is affectionate — a common term of address to a friend,
regardless of their build. In parts of Spain it lands harder. This is one of the
places where a single word's warmth genuinely does not travel.

## What you've built
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[ES-SEMANTIC-CONNOTATION]; assesses=[] -->

Two words with the same **denotation** — the fact they point at — and different
**connotation** — what choosing one says about you.

This is where a dictionary stops being enough. It will give you both words and
the same gloss for each, and the gloss is where the information you need has been
removed.

That completes the four shades: a modal that marks a guess, a verb the noun
insists on, an emphasis that implies a doubt, and a synonym that is not one.
Every one of them is invisible to a translation and audible to a native
speaker.
