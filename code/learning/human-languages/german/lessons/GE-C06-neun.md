# neun — "nine," in the vowel of *Deutsch*

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[GE-LEX-ACHT-01, GE-SOUND-DEUTSCH-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] Ninth number, and again nothing new to learn in the mouth — you got
this vowel from the name of the language itself.

## You'll want to know: neun
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[GE-LEX-NEUN-01]; assesses=[GE-SOUND-DEUTSCH-01, GE-LEX-DEUTSCH-01] -->

> **neun** — "nine." Said *noyn*.

The **eu** is the *oy* of English *boy*, which is exactly what it does in
*Deutsch*. So *neun* rhymes with English *coin*, and it is one syllable with an
*n* at each end.

Do not let the spelling talk you into *new-n*. German **eu** is never two
separate vowels.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[GE-ETYMON-NEUN-NINE-02]; assesses=[GE-LEX-NEUN-01] -->

**neun** is English **nine**, and the two have kept the same skeleton: *n*, a
vowel, *n*. Only the vowel wandered — German to *oy*, English to *eye*.

The Latin cousin is the odd one out here. *Novem* has a *v* where the Germanic
words have nothing, which is why Spanish *nueve* and French *neuf* look like a
different word. Between *neun* and *nine* there is nothing to explain.
