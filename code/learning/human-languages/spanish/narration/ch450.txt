Spanish, chapter 450: El césped y el patio.
7 lessons. All 7 can be done entirely by ear.


Lesson 1 of 7.
el patio — the space in the middle.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say el jardín and la calle. A patio is neither, and it sits between them.

You'll want to know: el patio.
el patio — the courtyard: an open space with the building around it.
Un piso con patio — a flat with a courtyard. Los niños están en el patio — the children are out in the yard. El patio de butacas — the stalls of a theatre, the flat part with the seats.
A jardín has plants and can be anywhere. A patio is defined by what surrounds it, and it may hold nothing but a washing line.

Grammar Lens: the school patio.
In a school, el patio is the playground, and with it comes a phrase you will hear constantly:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
Spanish: salir al patio. what it covers: to go out at break.
Spanish: la hora del patio. what it covers: break time.
The second one names the time by naming the place, and Spanish does this often — la hora de la calle for knocking-off time works the same way. When a Spanish phrase seems to be about a location, check whether it is really about a moment in the day.

The word, taken apart.
Here the honest answer is that nobody knows.
The best-supported account brings it from Occitan pati, waste ground, and from there back to Latin pactum, an agreement — common land held by agreement. A rival account points at Latin patere, to lie open, which fits the meaning better and the sound worse. Neither has settled the matter.
That is why this lesson lists no root. An empty list is not an oversight in this material; it is a claim, and the claim is that the origin is genuinely unresolved. Saying so is more useful than picking the prettier story, because the next time you see a root listed you can trust that somebody checked.
What is certain is where the word went. Spanish carried patio into English, French and German, and it is now the ordinary word for a paved space behind a house in places that have never used it for anything else.

Lesson 2 of 7.
la hierba — grass, the plant.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say la planta and verde. Now name the smallest, commonest green plant there is.

You'll want to know: la hierba.
la hierba — grass, and more widely any low soft plant: a herb, a weed.
Hay hierba entre las piedras — there is grass growing between the stones. Las malas hierbas — the weeds, literally the bad grasses. Una infusión de hierbas — a herbal tea.
You will also see it written yerba, mostly in the Americas, and yerba mate is the usual spelling for the drink. Both are the same word said the same way.

Grammar Lens: the h that is not pronounced and not decorative.
Latin herba had an h that had already gone silent by Roman times. Spanish kept the letter and added it back where Latin had none:
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
Latin: herba. Spanish: la hierba. the h: inherited.
Latin: ovum. Spanish: el huevo. the h: added.
Latin: ossum. Spanish: el hueso. the h: added.
Look at what the added ones have in common: they all begin ue. Scribes put an h in front because a word starting with u was read as a v, and uevo would have been read vevo. The h is a fence around the vowel.
Hierba begins ie rather than ue, so it did not need the fence. It kept what Latin had already handed it. The letter is silent in all of them.

The word, taken apart.
Latin herba, grass or herb. English borrowed the same word twice: once through French as herb, and once as the botanical herbaceous. Spanish has the learned form too, in herbicida and herbívoro, where the h sits in front of an unbroken e because those words came from books rather than from speech.
That is the tell. Hierba has the broken vowel because ordinary speakers wore it down over centuries; herbívoro has not, because it was lifted from Latin whole and handed to a scientist.

Lesson 3 of 7.
la piedra — the stone.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say la madera and duro. Now name the building material that is harder than the first.

You'll want to know: la piedra.
la piedra — the stone.
Una casa de piedra — a stone house. Hay piedras en el camino — there are stones on the path. De piedra also works as a description of a person who has not moved: me quedé de piedra, I was stunned.
For the material in the mass — quarried, cut, built with — Spanish stays singular: un muro de piedra. For the things you can pick up, plural: piedras.

Grammar Lens: the same vowel trick, one row over.
You have already watched a stressed o break into ue in rueda. A stressed e does the same thing and becomes ie:
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
Latin: petra. Spanish: la piedra. what broke: e under stress.
Latin: rota. Spanish: la rueda. what broke: o under stress.
Latin: herba. Spanish: la hierba. what broke: e under stress.
Three of this chapter's words are the same sound change caught at different moments. Once you hear ie and ue as stressed vowels that gave way, a large part of Spanish spelling stops looking arbitrary.
And the test still works: petrificar keeps the plain e, because the stress has moved off it.

The word, taken apart.
Latin petra, taken from Greek pétra, a rock.
Latin already had its own word for stone, lapis, and it is the one that survived in the learned vocabulary — lapidario, and English dilapidated. The Greek borrowing won out in ordinary speech instead, which is the reverse of what usually happens.
The name Pedro is the same word: Greek pétros, a stone, handed out as a nickname and then carried by enough people to become a name in its own right. So Pedro and piedra are one word twice, and a petrol is an oil that comes out of rock.

Lesson 4 of 7.
el césped — grass you look after.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say el campo and plantar. Somebody planted the grass on a football pitch, and that is the whole difference.

You'll want to know: el césped.
el césped — the lawn, and on a sports ground the pitch.
Cortar el césped — to mow the lawn. No pisar el césped — keep off the grass, the wording on the sign in every park. El césped está seco — the pitch is dry.
It has no everyday plural. Where English says the lawns, Spanish repeats the singular or names the places instead.

Grammar Lens: césped is not hierba.
Both are grass. They answer different questions:
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
the question: what plant is that? the word: la hierba.
the question: what surface is that? the word: el césped.
Hierba is the thing growing; césped is the maintained expanse of it. So grass coming up between the paving stones is hierba, and nobody would call it césped. A mown lawn is césped, though it is made of hierba.
Sports tell the same story. Jugar en el césped is to play on the pitch, and a pitch that has stopped being looked after has stopped being césped.

The word, taken apart.
Latin caespes, a cut sod of turf — a slab of earth with grass on top, lifted with a spade for building banks and walls.
That is a good picture to hold, because it explains why césped is about the surface rather than the plant. A caespes was a thing you could carry.
The written accent is doing real work. A Spanish word ending in a consonant other than n or s takes its stress on the last syllable by default, so cesped without a mark would be read ces-PED. The accent overrules the default and pins the stress on CÉS-ped.

Lesson 5 of 7.
el entrenamiento — the session.

Warm-up.
[pause 2 seconds]
Say el tren and correr. One of those two is about to turn up somewhere you would not expect it.

You'll want to know: el entrenamiento.
el entrenamiento — training, and a single training session.
Tengo entrenamiento los martes — I have training on Tuesdays. El entrenamiento es en el césped de atrás — training is on the back pitch. And the people: el entrenador, the coach; entrenar, to train; entrenarse, to train yourself.
Away from sport it keeps working: un entrenamiento de dos días for a course at work.

Grammar Lens: -miento names the process.
You have met -anza turning a verb into a noun. -miento does the same job, and the two split the language between them:
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
verb: entrenar. noun: el entrenamiento.
verb: mover. noun: el movimiento.
verb: conocer. noun: el conocimiento.
Every -miento noun is masculine, as every -anza noun is feminine, so the ending hands you the article both times.
Which verb takes which is not predictable and has to be met word by word — esperanza but movimiento, with nothing in the verbs to tell you. What you can rely on is the gender, and that is worth having.

The word, taken apart.
French entraîner, to drag along — and inside it the same traîner that gave Spanish el tren.
Hold the two together. A tren is the set of carriages dragged behind the engine. To entrenar somebody is to drag them along with you until they can keep up. The coach is out in front and the squad is the train.
English took the identical picture from the identical French word, which is why train in English means both the thing on rails and what a coach does to a team. Spanish split the two forms — tren and entrenar — and so kept the relationship visible in a way English hides.

Lesson 6 of 7.
(el patio, la hierba, la piedra, el césped, el entrenamiento) — from cold.

Warm-up.
[pause 3 seconds]
Close the lessons before this one. Say all five: the courtyard, grass, the stone, the lawn, the training session.

Grammar Lens: three words, one broken vowel.
Say these aloud and listen for where the stress sits:
[a table of 3 rows, read aloud]
Latin or French: herba. Spanish: la hierba.
Latin or French: petra. Spanish: la piedra.
Latin or French: entraîner. Spanish: entrenamiento.
The first two broke their stressed e into ie. The third did not, because the stress in entrenamiento never lands on that e at all — it lands three syllables later. Same vowel, same language, different outcome, and the only variable is stress.
This is why piedra has a diphthong and petrificar does not, and why rueda has one and rodamos does not. One condition explains all four.

Grammar Lens: two pairs that are not synonyms.
[a table of 2 rows, read aloud]
the pair: patio / jardín. what separates them: a patio is defined by what surrounds it.
the pair: hierba / césped. what separates them: hierba is the plant, césped is the tended surface.
Both pairs fail in the same direction for an English speaker: "yard" and "grass" are wide words that cover each pair whole. Spanish is narrower here than English, which is the harder direction, because nothing in your own language warns you that a choice is waiting.

Why it's said this way.
The notice uses césped once and hierba once, four lines apart, and swapping them would make it unreadable.
The big pitch is césped: a maintained playing surface, currently closed for work on it. The strip down the side is hierba: grass as a plant, being replanted. A Spanish reader takes se está replantando as confirmation — you replant hierba, since that is the living thing; césped is what you get once the hierba has taken and somebody starts mowing it.
Notice also no dejen and disculpen. Both are the ustedes form, which is what public notices use: addressed to everyone, and formal without being cold. A sign saying no dejes would read as though it had been written for one particular person who had already done it once.

Wrap-up Recall.
[pause 3 seconds]
Now your turn, out loud and then in writing. Four lines for a gate notice: say the pitch is closed, say where training moves to, ask people to keep off the grass at the side, and say there are stones on the path.
Then check yourself: did césped go to the playing surface and hierba to the plant, and did you use the ustedes form?
