# आसान / मुश्किल — easy, difficult, and two words that refuse to agree

## Warm-up
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[HI-GRAMMAR-C41-ADJ-SYSTEM, HI-CONCEPT-C11-KAALAA-SAFED-01] -->

[PAUSE 2s] You know that **काला** changes its ending to match the noun and
**सफ़ेद** never does. Before reading on: which of those two do you expect a word
borrowed from Persian to behave like?

## You'll want to know: आसान and मुश्किल
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[HI-LEX-C80-EASE-01, HI-LEX-C80-EASE-02]; assesses=[] -->

**आसान** (*āsān*) — "easy."
**मुश्किल** (*mushkil*) — "difficult."

Both are borrowed, and both behave like **सफ़ेद**: they sit in front of the noun
and **do not change ending for anything**. Whatever the noun's gender or number,
the adjective is the same shape you learned.

## The word, taken apart
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[] -->

**आसान** comes from Persian *āsān*, "easy" — a plain everyday word in both
languages, with no complication to report, which is fitting.

**मुश्किल** is the more interesting half. It is Arabic *mushkil*, from the root
**ش-ك-ل** (*sh-k-l*), which is about **form and shape**. A *mushkil* thing is
one whose shape is unclear — something you cannot make out the outline of. The
same root gives *shakl*, "shape," a word many Hindi speakers also use.

So "difficult" in Hindi is not "heavy" or "hard" but **"hard to make out."**
That is a different metaphor from the English one, and worth carrying with you.

## Grammar Lens: which adjectives bend, and why
<!-- hl-knowledge: introduces=[]; assesses=[HI-CONCEPT-C11-KAALAA-SAFED-01, HI-CONCEPT-C11-KAALAA-SAFED-02, HI-GRAMMAR-C41-ADJ-SYSTEM] -->

Line the four up:

| adjective | origin | changes ending? |
|---|---|---|
| काला | Sanskrit, ends in **-आ** | yes |
| सफ़ेद | Persian loan | no |
| आसान | Persian loan | no |
| मुश्किल | Arabic loan | no |

There is a pattern under this, and it is about shape rather than about
borrowing. A Hindi adjective bends when it ends in **-आ**; otherwise it stays
put. Loanwords land on the "no" side so often because they mostly arrive ending
in a consonant.

That means you do not have to remember a list. **Look at the ending.**
